Strategies & philosophies worth stealing

Playbook

Ten investors whose track records earned them the right to have an opinion, and whose philosophies are distinct enough to actually learn something from — not just "buy low, sell high" repackaged ten different ways. Each gets a one-line mantra and the two or three sentences behind it. None of this is advice on what to buy today; it's the thinking underneath a fixed checklist like the one this whole site runs on.

Where I land on this list

Closest fit: Peter Lynch. My own portfolio logic is almost word-for-word his — a concentrated set of names where two or three real winners are expected to carry the whole portfolio, bought because I understand the business, not because a screen says it's statistically cheap. Growth-tilted, fundamentals-driven, and honestly more active than Buffett or Graham would probably sign off on.

Strong second: Charlie Munger, for the process itself rather than the picks. The fixed 14-criterion checklist every company on this site runs through, whether I like the answer or not, is straight out of his "checklist to avoid stupidity" approach — discipline in how I decide, not just what I decide.

Where it started: Warren Buffett. He was my original go-to guru, starting in 2013. That first year I ran roughly 90% blue chips and 10% growth — and noticed the returns were slow, single-digit stuff. The one real risk I'd taken, a Facebook/Meta position bought a few months after its 2012 IPO, turned out to be the name actually carrying the portfolio — later joined by ASML. So I started shifting, bit by bit swapping blue chips for what I believed were winners in growth sectors and industries — which is roughly how I ended up closer to Lynch than to the Buffett I started with.

What I'd add myself: patience, consistency and fine-tuning over frequent trading — I don't chase the latest hype, and I don't think investing needs to be complicated. Focus on the key information, hold a few consistent principles, and don't overanalyze a company into paralysis. Palantir was the one real inconsistency in that — I don't normally buy names where the P/E has gone to the moon; high multiples are fine, but there's a line, and that one crossed it.

Benjamin Graham
1894–1976 · "The Intelligent Investor," Columbia Business School · taught Buffett
"Price is what you pay. Value is what you get."
The father of value investing: buy businesses trading meaningfully below what they're actually worth, and let that gap — the margin of safety — protect you when you're wrong, because you will sometimes be wrong. Treat the market itself (his "Mr. Market") as a manic-depressive business partner whose daily mood swings are an opportunity to exploit, never a verdict on what something is worth.
Warren Buffett
b. 1930 · Berkshire Hathaway · retired as CEO Jan 2026, remains Chairman
"It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price."
Took Graham's cigar-butt value investing and upgraded it: pay a sensible price for a genuinely great, understandable business with a durable moat and honest management, then hold it for decades and let compounding do the work. Patience is the whole edge — most of his wealth was built after age 65, simply by not selling.
Charlie Munger
1924–2023 · Berkshire Hathaway Vice Chairman · Buffett's longtime partner
"Invert, always invert."
Solve problems backward: instead of asking how to succeed, ask what guarantees failure, then avoid that. Build a "latticework" of mental models borrowed from psychology, biology and physics rather than relying on finance alone, and concentrate capital in your best few ideas — a long checklist of avoidable stupidities beats a long list of clever moves.
Peter Lynch
b. 1944 · ran Fidelity Magellan 1977–1990, +29.2%/yr average
"Invest in what you know."
Ordinary investors have a real edge in ordinary life — the stores they shop in, the products they use, the companies they already understand before Wall Street notices. Look for growing "tenbaggers" with simple, explainable businesses, and remember a whole portfolio only needs a handful of real winners to carry everything else that just muddles along.
Philip Fisher
1907–2004 · "Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits" · growth-investing pioneer
"The stock market is filled with individuals who know the price of everything, but the value of nothing."
Flipped classic value investing on its head: don't hunt for cheap companies, hunt for exceptional ones and pay a fair price for years of growth ahead. His "scuttlebutt" method meant talking directly to customers, suppliers, competitors and former employees before buying — then holding for the long run once genuinely convinced.
John Templeton
1912–2008 · Templeton Growth Fund · pioneer of global investing
"Bull markets are born on pessimism, grow on skepticism, mature on optimism, and die on euphoria."
A committed contrarian who believed the only way to get a genuine bargain was to buy exactly when everyone else was selling — he famously bought every NYSE stock trading under $1 at the outbreak of World War II. Searched relentlessly and globally for undervalued assets, on the theory that the crowd's despair is where the biggest mispricings hide.
Howard Marks
b. 1946 · Oaktree Capital co-founder · memos read by Buffett himself
"You can't do the same thing as everyone else and expect to outperform."
First-level thinking says "good company, buy it"; second-level thinking asks what's already priced in and where the consensus is simply wrong. Since nobody can reliably predict market cycles, the real edge is risk control and knowing roughly where in the cycle you stand — protecting the downside matters more than chasing the next leg up.
Ray Dalio
b. 1949 · founded Bridgewater Associates · author of "Principles"
"He who lives by the crystal ball will eat shattered glass."
Write your decision-making rules down as explicit, testable principles rather than trusting gut feel in the moment, then apply them consistently. Diversify across genuinely uncorrelated bets rather than just more stocks, and actively seek out people who will disagree with you — radical transparency surfaces mistakes before they compound into real damage.
Seth Klarman
b. 1957 · Baupost Group · "Margin of Safety" (1991, now a collector's item)
"Patience and discipline can make you look foolishly out of touch until they make you look prudent and even prescient."
Margin of safety comes first: buy assets for meaningfully less than they're worth and let that discount, not forecasting skill, do the risk management. Willing to hold large amounts of cash and simply wait when nothing qualifies — looking wrong or overly cautious for a while is the price of real discipline.
John Bogle
1929–2019 · founded Vanguard · invented the retail index fund
"Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack."
The deliberate counterpoint to everyone else on this page: after fees and costs, most active stock-picking fails to beat the market over time, so stop trying to find winners and simply own all of them at the lowest possible cost. Time in the market, discipline, and rock-bottom fees compound more reliably than skill — a useful check on how much confidence any "checklist" really deserves.
Personal research notes, not financial advice. Philosophies summarized from public writings, letters and interviews — verify before acting.
