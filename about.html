Hi, I'm Maarten

MSc Business Economics CPA 13 years investing

ASML's one of my better calls, GoPro one of my worse ones. I don't know what's coming next — AI, market volatility, inflation, US jobs numbers, factory indices, or how to time a buy or a sell. Nobody reliably does. What I do believe in is consistency, and having a fixed set of indicators I trust enough to check every company against. This site is that checklist, made public.

Why

I like turning complicated things into something simple — summaries and dashboards, not investor calls or 200-page annual reports. What I actually want to see is what the business is doing, not just what management says about it. I can't predict the future, and past results are no guarantee of future ones — but if a company holds up well against a fixed, simple set of KPIs, that's at least something solid to base a decision on. Every write-up ends in one simple score, not just a wall of numbers. My hope is that these write-ups are simple enough to help you make up your own mind too.

How I invest

No technical analysis, no day trading, no chasing hype — fundamentals only. I started out almost entirely in blue chips; over time that's shifted toward a heavier weighting in growth stocks, but the process hasn't changed. Every company gets the same checklist: growth, margins, return on capital versus its cost, balance sheet health, ownership, and valuation against its own history — including whatever doesn't look good. Some figures are third-party estimates, flagged where it matters. Don't expect a lot of trading from me — I buy or sell about one company a year, and most of what happens in between is fine-tuning the existing portfolio, not drastic moves. On top of that, I add money into the existing portfolio on a fairly regular basis — the amount varies, and it's not strictly every month, but it adds up over time. I treat it almost like a savings account, just less liquid, and hopefully with better long-term returns.

One honest caveat: the score isn't perfect. A company with a lower score can still outperform one with a higher score, and vice versa — treat it as a structured starting point, not a verdict.

The scorecard, and the Martinero Index

Every dashboard runs the same company through a fixed set of 13 criteria: revenue growth, EBIT growth, EBIT margin, free cash flow growth, market share, market concentration, return on capital versus its cost, balance sheet leverage, free cash flow conversion, valuation against the company's own trading history, share count trend, insider or founder ownership, and capex intensity. Each dashboard shows a simple X/13 score — how many of the 13 a company clears.

That count treats every criterion as equally important, which isn't really how I weigh a business. So alongside it, every dashboard also carries a Martinero Index: the same 13 criteria, scored 1-100, with growth counted for the most (revenue, EBIT and free cash flow growth combined), profitability and capital efficiency next, balance sheet health and capital allocation after that, competitive moat below that, valuation below that, and ownership weighted lowest — deliberately, since I'd rather a business earn a high score on what it does than on who owns it. A criterion that's met earns its full weight; one that isn't earns zero. The two scores don't always move together, and that's the point — a company can clear most of the simple checklist and still land modestly on the weighted one, or the other way around.

Neither score is a stand-in for judgment. Sometimes a company misses a criterion for a reason that says more about a one-off accounting item than the underlying business — Arm's EBIT criteria are a good example, dragged down by a one-time IPO-related stock compensation charge rather than weakening performance. Where that happens, I've tried to flag it directly on the dashboard rather than quietly change the scoring to fit the story.

I'm in this for the long run, with two goals in mind:

Baseline goal
10%+ CAGR
Stretch goal
2x / 5 yrs

The stretch goal — doubling my portfolio every five years — is something I've fortunately managed to do so far. It's not purely a return target, either: part of it comes from regularly adding fresh money into the portfolio too, so even landing closer to the 10% baseline on returns alone can still add up to a double within five years once new contributions are factored in.

My take on why the return side is achievable in the first place: in a ten-stock portfolio, you don't need every pick to work out. Two or three real stars are enough to carry double-digit annual growth for the whole portfolio — the rest just need to not blow up. I hope this site helps you find a few of your own.
