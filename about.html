About

Hi, I'm Maarten

MSc Business Economics CPA 13 years investing

I'm a finance professional and CPA with 15+ years in finance business partnering, FP&A, and management reporting. That same toolkit — KPIs, variance analysis, reading a balance sheet for what it actually says — is what I've used for the last 13 years to analyze companies as an investor. ASML's one of my better calls, GoPro one of my worse ones. Nobody can reliably time AI hype, inflation, or the next macro headline. What I believe in is consistency: a fixed set of indicators I trust enough to check every company against. This site is that checklist, made public.

Why

This started as a hobby — I wanted to learn how to build a website and see how AI could speed up research I was already doing by hand. It turned into something more useful: an emotionless filter for my own investing process.

I like turning complicated things into something simple — a dashboard, not a 200-page annual report. I want to see what a business is doing, not what management says about it. I can't predict the future, but if a company holds up against a fixed, simple set of KPIs, that's something solid to base a decision on. Every write-up ends in one score, not a wall of numbers — simple enough, I hope, to help you make up your own mind too.

How I invest

No technical analysis, no day trading, no chasing hype — fundamentals only. I started almost entirely in blue chips; over time that's shifted toward growth stocks, because my biggest returns haven't come from timing mature, cyclical names — they've come from finding real secular compounders and holding them. The checklist hasn't changed either way: growth, margins, return on capital versus its cost, balance sheet health, ownership, valuation against its own history — including what doesn't look good. Some figures are third-party estimates, flagged where it matters. I trade about once a year; most activity in between is fine-tuning, not drastic moves. I also add fresh money in fairly regularly — not strictly monthly, but it adds up. Think of it as a savings account, just less liquid, hopefully with better returns.

One honest caveat: the score isn't perfect. A company with a lower score can still outperform one with a higher score, and vice versa — treat it as a structured starting point, not a verdict.

Why this checklist exists

The financial internet is noisy — hyperactive gurus, fear-mongering headlines, hype. This checklist strips that out: the same cold, hard financial data, run through the same strict audit every time, regardless of sentiment or what happened in the market this morning. It doesn't replace judgment — see the caveat above — but it's a consistent, boring starting point that doesn't move with the news cycle.

The scorecard, and the Martinero Index

Every dashboard runs the same company through a fixed set of 14 criteria: revenue growth, EBIT growth, EBIT margin, free cash flow growth, market share, market concentration, return on capital versus its cost, balance sheet leverage, free cash flow conversion, valuation against the company's own trading history, share count trend, insider or founder ownership, capex intensity, and margin stability — whether gross margin (or operating margin for banks, insurers and other financials that don't report a traditional gross margin) has held up or eroded over the trailing three years, as a read on pricing power and moat durability. Each dashboard shows a simple X/14 score — how many of the 14 a company clears.

That count treats every criterion as equally important, which isn't really how I weigh a business. So alongside it, every dashboard also carries a Martinero Index: the same 14 criteria, weighted 1-100, with growth counted for the most (revenue, EBIT and free cash flow growth combined), profitability and capital efficiency next, balance sheet health and capital allocation after that, competitive moat and margin durability below that, valuation below that, and ownership weighted lowest — deliberately, since I'd rather a business earn a high score on what it does than on who owns it. The two scores don't always move together, and that's the point — a company can clear most of the simple checklist and still land modestly on the weighted one, or the other way around. I'm keeping the exact weighting and scoring mechanics off this page for now.

Neither score is a stand-in for judgment. Sometimes a company misses a criterion for a reason that says more about a one-off accounting item than the underlying business — Arm's EBIT criteria are a good example, dragged down by a one-time IPO-related stock compensation charge rather than weakening performance. Where that happens, I've tried to flag it directly on the dashboard rather than quietly change the scoring to fit the story.

I'm in this for the long run, with two goals in mind:

Baseline goal
10%+ CAGR
Stretch goal
2x / 5 yrs

The stretch goal — doubling my portfolio every five years — is something I've fortunately managed to do so far. It's not purely a return target, either: part of it comes from regularly adding fresh money into the portfolio too, so even landing closer to the 10% baseline on returns alone can still add up to a double within five years once new contributions are factored in.

My take on why the return side is achievable in the first place: in a ten-stock portfolio, you don't need every pick to work out. Two or three real stars are enough to carry double-digit annual growth for the whole portfolio — the rest just need to not blow up. I hope this site helps you find a few of your own.

What's next

Still a hobby, fine-tuned weekly. This isn't built for absolute beginners with zero interest in the numbers — but it's not just for veterans either. If you're newer to investing and willing to spend a little time familiarizing yourself with KPIs and how a scorecard works, this is a genuinely good place to learn by doing: read the summary, see how the criteria connect to the number. If you're further along, it's a quick, honest scorecard you can scan in a minute. Either way: check the scorecard, read the summary, and decide for yourself whether it's a stock you'd actually want to own. Where this goes from here, I don't know.
