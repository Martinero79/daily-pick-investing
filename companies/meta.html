Meta is, at its core, the highest-margin advertising business in the world wrapped around a second, far riskier bet. The Family of Apps segment (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Messenger — 3.60 billion daily active people) throws off well over $100B a year in operating profit, and that cash is funding two things at once: a Reality Labs division that has now lost roughly $88B cumulatively since 2020 ($4.62B in Q2 2026 alone, on just $431M of quarterly revenue), and an AI/data-center capex program that has exploded from $18.7B in 2021 to a guided $125–145B in 2026. Gross margin, the new margin-stability criterion, has also expanded (79.6% FY22 to 82.0% FY25). Zuckerberg controls ~61% of the vote on roughly a 13% economic stake via Class B super-voting shares, so capital-allocation calls of this size — including whether Reality Labs and the AI buildout ever earn their cost of capital — ultimately rest with one person. The ads business itself is executing exceptionally well (Meta is on track to overtake Google as the single largest digital ad platform globally in 2026), but the capex ramp is now large enough to have flipped a decade of net cash into net debt and to be actively dragging on free cash flow growth — that tension is the center of this case.
Data vintage note: figures refreshed through Q2 2026 (reported Jul 29, 2026) and spot price as of Aug 11, 2026. Trailing vs. forward P/E: current P/E divides today's price by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings; the FY21-25 trailing series above is that same backward-looking ratio measured at each fiscal year-end, showing how the market has re-rated Meta relative to earnings it had already booked. Forward P/E instead divides today's price by consensus estimates of the next 12 months of earnings — forward-looking, and better at capturing expected growth (or capex-driven earnings pressure) that trailing P/E can't see. For Meta specifically, forward P/E (18.3x) sits well below trailing (22.6x) today, a wider gap than in most of the prior five years — the market is pricing in continued earnings growth even as the AI-capex ramp weighs on near-term margins and free cash flow. Dividing that forward P/E by consensus EPS growth gives a PEG ratio of roughly 1.0 — right at the traditional "fair value" line, which is a fairly rare read among the mega-caps on this site: Meta's multiple looks neither cheap nor expensive once its expected earnings growth is accounted for.

By economic (share-count) ownership, Zuckerberg holds only ~13% of shares outstanding — the 61% voting figure comes entirely from Class B shares carrying 10 votes each vs. 1 vote for Class A. He holds ~99.7% of all outstanding Class B shares.
Governance note: unlike a widely-held mega-cap, Meta is functionally controlled by one person. Zuckerberg cannot be outvoted on director elections, executive pay, or major capital decisions — including the Reality Labs bet and the AI capex ramp — regardless of what the other ~87% of economic owners think. This is a real, durable governance risk, not a technicality — even though it has arguably enabled decisive, long-horizon capital allocation (the Reels pivot, the current AI buildout) that a more consensus-driven board might have resisted or delayed.

What's Working

The ads engine is firing on all cylinders — revenue growth has reaccelerated into the high-20s%, AI-driven ad targeting (Advantage+) is lifting conversion, and Meta is on track to become the single largest digital ad platform globally in 2026. EBIT growth, margin, ROIC and market position all clear the bar; the entry multiple is roughly in line with its own history despite the AI narrative; share count keeps shrinking; and a single, deeply invested founder-CEO still runs the show.

What to Watch

Capex intensity has roughly doubled as a share of revenue since 2021 and is guided toward ~50%+ of revenue in 2026 (twice-raised guidance, now $125–145B), funded partly by newly issued debt that flipped a decade of net cash into net debt. FCF conversion and FCF CAGR both miss badly as a direct result. Reality Labs has burned ~$88B since 2020 with no disclosed profitability path. And because Zuckerberg holds ~61% of the vote on ~13% of the economic interest, none of this capital allocation can be overridden by other shareholders — the AI bet succeeding or failing is essentially a wager on one person's judgment.
