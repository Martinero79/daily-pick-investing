Unilever reads exactly the way you'd expect a mature, 95-year-old consumer-goods giant to read: genuinely low growth, paired with real, structural profitability. Reported revenue actually has a small negative five-year CAGR (−0.94%, €52.4B → €50.5B), and EBIT and free cash flow are both essentially flat over the same window — a materially different picture from the mid-single-digit "underlying sales growth" Unilever prefers to headline, which strips out currency and the portfolio disposals (Russia exit, the 2022 sale of the Ekaterra tea business, and the December 2025 demerger of Ice Cream as the separately listed "The Magnum Ice Cream Company"). What's genuinely working: operating margin has climbed every year in this window to a first-time-in-the-period 20.05% in FY2025, gross margin has expanded a full 6.7 points versus three years ago, capex is a lean 2.8% of revenue, and ROIC has stayed remarkably strong and stable (15.9%-17.8%) throughout — comfortably clearing both this framework's 15% bar and an assumed ~7% cost of capital, a genuine rarity among the slow-growers on this site. Like Nestlé in the same GRANOLAS batch, Unilever's problem isn't the quality of the business, it's that there's barely a growth story to tell: the current 21.3x trailing P/E sits above its own five-year average of 17.8x, net debt/EBITDA is right at the 2x line rather than clearly under it, and no founder or controlling family remains to provide long-term alignment. H1 2026 showed the clearest recent bright spot — underlying sales growth accelerating to 4.8% (5.8% in Q2 alone) on real volume gains, not just pricing.

H1 2026 Headlines

Turnover was €25.6B, up a modest +0.5% reported YoY (+0.7% from acquisitions net of disposals, offset by −4.9% currency). The more meaningful number is underlying sales growth (USG) — Unilever's preferred, FX/portfolio-adjusted measure — which accelerated to 4.8% in H1 (4.2% volume + 0.6% price), and to 5.8% in Q2 alone (5.5% from volume), a genuine sign of real demand rather than just price increases. Underlying operating margin rose 10bps to 20.3%, with gross margin at 46.8% despite higher brand investment (16.1% of turnover). Power Brands — 78% of total turnover — grew USG 6.0%. The €800M productivity program was completed ahead of schedule, the quarterly dividend was raised 3%, and a €1.5B share buyback programme was completed.

Gross Margin Trend (FY2021-FY2025)
Gross margin has expanded every year since the FY2022 low, from 40.23% to 46.95% — a genuine +6.72pp improvement versus FY2022 (the window used for criterion #14), driven by pricing discipline, mix shift toward Power Brands, and the productivity program.
Market position, honestly stated: Unilever holds genuine #1 or #2 positions in skin cleansing and deodorants globally (Dove is a top-line global brand in both), and is a clear #2 in home care behind Procter & Gamble's Tide (Persil/OMO/Surf are all €1B+ brands) — but it's only #4 globally in oral care, and faces genuinely well-resourced competitors (P&G, Nestlé, Reckitt, Colgate-Palmolive, L'Oréal, Henkel) across every category it operates in. Both things are true: real category leadership in its core brands, in a market that remains structurally fragmented and competitive.
Portfolio reshaping, completed: Unilever sold its global tea business (Ekaterra, including Lipton) to CVC Capital Partners in 2022, and completed the demerger of its Ice Cream business (Magnum, Ben & Jerry's, Wall's, Talenti) as the separately listed "The Magnum Ice Cream Company" in December 2025. The continuing business is now organized around four divisions — Beauty & Wellbeing, Personal Care, Home Care, and Nutrition (Foods) — a genuinely simpler, more focused portfolio than five years ago, though it makes clean long-run revenue comparisons harder.

Trailing & Forward P/E — FY21-25 & Current
Current trailing P/E of ≈21.3x sits above the FY21-25 average of 17.8x — Unilever is trading somewhat rich relative to its own recent history, likely reflecting the FY2025 margin improvement and the market's hope that H1 2026's volume-led acceleration continues. Forward P/E (≈16.5x now) runs meaningfully below trailing, implying the market expects real earnings growth from here — see explainer below.
Trailing P/E Forward P/E
Reading the three P/E numbers: current P/E is today's share price divided by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings — a backward-looking snapshot at today's price. Trailing P/E (the FY21-25 series above) is that same backward-looking ratio calculated at each fiscal year-end. Forward P/E is today's share price divided by analysts' consensus estimate of the next 12 months of earnings — forward-looking, and the more useful of the two for judging whether a stock is priced fairly relative to where its earnings are actually headed. For Unilever specifically: trailing P/E (≈21.3x) sits above its own five-year history, but forward P/E (≈16.5x) is meaningfully cheaper, implying consensus expects real earnings growth from here as the margin recovery continues. One more lens worth a glance: the PEG ratio, forward P/E divided by expected annual EPS growth. With Unilever guiding to 4-6% underlying sales growth for 2026 and analysts penciling in mid-single-digit EPS growth, the forward-P/E-implied PEG works out to roughly 3.0 — well above the traditional 1.0 "fair value" line, a reminder that even at a discounted forward multiple, this isn't a statistically cheap stock once its genuinely slow growth is accounted for.
Balance sheet: net debt was €23.3B at FY2025-end, down from €24.7B a year earlier, aided by free cash flow and a €3B pre-demerger cash payment from the Ice Cream business. Unilever's own disclosure puts net debt/underlying EBITDA at 2.0x post-demerger; recomputed on a reported-EBITDA basis across the window it has run a steady 2.1x-2.3x every year since FY2021 — consistently right at, not clearly under, this framework's <2x bar. ROIC, by contrast, is a genuine strength: 15.9%-17.8% every year in this window, comfortably clearing both the 15% bar and the assumed 7% WACC — a rare combination of modest leverage tolerance and consistently strong capital returns for a company with almost no top-line growth.

What's Working

Operating margin has expanded every year in this window to a first-time 20.05% in FY2025, gross margin is up 6.7 points versus three years ago, and ROIC has stayed remarkably strong and stable (15.9%-17.8%) throughout — comfortably clearing both this framework's bar and its assumed cost of capital. Capex is genuinely low at 2.8% of revenue, the share count has declined modestly via consistent buybacks, and H1 2026 showed real underlying sales growth acceleration (4.8%, then 5.8% in Q2) driven by volume, not just price. The dividend has been raised again (+3%), and the portfolio is now genuinely simpler after the tea and ice cream divestments.

What to Watch

This is a real, structural growth problem, not a one-off distortion: reported revenue, EBIT and free cash flow are all essentially flat to negative over five years, and FCF conversion has fallen well below this framework's 80% bar. Net debt/EBITDA sits right at the 2x line rather than clearly under it, the current 21.3x trailing P/E is above Unilever's own five-year average, and ownership is genuinely dispersed with no founder or controlling family for long-term alignment. The Ice Cream demerger and prior tea disposal are real strategic simplifications, but they also mean the next few years of reported figures will be compared against a smaller, restated revenue base — worth tracking closely rather than assuming like-for-like continuity.
