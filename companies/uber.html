Uber's scorecard tells a turnaround story: revenue has compounded at roughly 31% a year since FY2021, operating margin has climbed from a deeply negative −21.96% to a genuinely positive 10.70%, and free cash flow reached $9.76B in FY2025 on capex of just $336M — 0.65% of revenue, about as asset-light as this framework sees. That structural lightness is the core of the bull case: Uber owns no vehicles and employs no drivers, so growth doesn't require proportional capital. The bear case is just as real: five-year EBIT and FCF CAGR are both mathematically undefined because the FY2021 base was negative, current operating margin still sits well under this framework's 20% bar, diluted share count grew roughly 20% from FY2021 to FY2024 before flattening in FY2025, and insider/founder ownership is thin at roughly 2% — Travis Kalanick departed the board years ago and no longer holds a meaningful stake. The most interesting wrinkle sits in the autonomous-vehicle strategy: where Tesla is betting on building, owning and operating its own robotaxi fleet end-to-end, Uber is betting on being the marketplace that aggregates everyone else's vehicles — Waymo, WeRide, Nuro, Avride, Momenta, Wayve, Pony.ai and roughly two dozen others — onto its existing demand platform. It's the opposite path to the same destination, and Uber's own Waymo relationship (its most important AV partner today) is already showing the strain of that platform bet: Phoenix wound down in May 2026, and Waymo has told Uber it plans a standalone app in Austin and Atlanta starting 2028.

Q2 2026 Headlines

Revenue reached $14.2B, up 12% YoY (11% constant-currency) — a deceleration from FY2025's ~18% pace, but management flagged that business-model/accounting changes (how certain delivery and mobility contracts are recognized) shaved roughly 8 points off reported revenue growth. Gross Bookings, the cleaner volume signal, grew 24% YoY to $58.0B. Trips grew 18% YoY to 3.9 billion, GAAP operating income was $1.9B (13.4% margin) and non-GAAP EPS grew 35% YoY to $0.81. Read together, underlying demand kept accelerating even as reported revenue growth looked slower — a genuine case where the headline number understates the trend.

Quarterly Operating Income ($B) & Margin
Op income and margin have both trended up over the last five quarters despite the Q3'25 dip — Q1 and Q2 2026 are the two strongest quarters on record for GAAP profitability.
The Waymo & Tesla contrast: Tesla is building a fully vertically-integrated robotaxi stack — its own vehicles, its own FSD software, its own fleet — and betting that owning the whole stack is the winning model. Uber is making the opposite bet: after selling its own self-driving unit (ATG) to Aurora in December 2020, it now positions itself purely as the demand-side marketplace, integrating autonomous vehicles from roughly 30 partners — Waymo, WeRide, Avride, Nuro, Wayve, Momenta, May Mobility, Pony.ai and more — onto its existing rider app, plus Aurora, Volvo Autonomous Solutions and Waabi for autonomous trucking. Uber has committed roughly $10B in capital across these partnerships and targets rides in 15+ cities and 10+ countries by the end of 2026. Same destination (autonomous ride-hailing at scale), opposite paths: Tesla owns the risk and the upside of the technology itself; Uber owns the distribution and diversifies technology risk across many bets — but depends on partners who could, and in Waymo's case already are, choosing to go direct. Waymo robotaxis currently run through the Uber app in Austin and Atlanta (Phoenix ended May 2026); WeRide operates in Abu Dhabi, Dubai and Riyadh via Uber; Avride in Dallas; and Uber/Lucid/Nuro's premium robotaxi service is slated to launch later in 2026. The clearest sign the model carries real risk: Uber and Waymo are already set to compete head-on, not partner, in markets like London.

Forward P/E (17.37x now) has run persistently below trailing P/E — see explainer below.
Trailing P/E Forward P/E
Reading trailing vs. forward P/E, and the PEG caveat: current P/E is today's share price divided by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings; the trailing-P/E series above is that same ratio calculated at each year-end. Forward P/E divides today's price by analysts' consensus estimate of the next 12 months of earnings, capturing expected growth that trailing P/E can't see. For Uber, forward P/E (17.37x) sits modestly above current trailing (15.83x) — consensus actually expects lower GAAP EPS in 2026 than in 2025, but that's a base-effect artifact: FY2024 and FY2025 GAAP earnings were both inflated by one-off tax and mark-to-market items (see page 2), so the year-ahead comparison looks like a decline even though revenue, gross bookings and operating income are all still growing at double digits. On that basis, Uber's PEG ratio (forward P/E ÷ consensus EPS growth) comes out around 2.6–2.7x using the ~6.6% GAAP EPS growth analysts currently model for 2026-2028 — above the traditional 1.0 "fair value" line, but this PEG reading should be treated with real skepticism given how much the denominator is distorted by lapping one-off items rather than reflecting Uber's underlying operating growth rate.
Balance sheet: Uber ended FY2025 with $12.23B in cash & investments against $12.30B in total debt — a net debt position of just $76M, against $8.7B of FY2025 Adjusted EBITDA (+35% YoY). That's a Net Debt/EBITDA ratio of roughly 0.01x, comfortably clearing this framework's <2x bar and functionally equivalent to a net-cash balance sheet. Net debt/cash has swung between small net-cash and small net-debt positions every year since FY2021 (FY21 net cash $4.05B, FY22 net debt $5.23B, FY23 net debt $2.89B, FY24 net cash $0.65B, FY25 net debt $0.08B) — consistently low leverage in either direction, not a trend to worry about.

What's Working

Revenue has compounded above 30% a year for four years, operating margin has improved in every single year since FY2021, and free cash flow conversion is exceptional thanks to a genuinely asset-light model (0.65% capex intensity). The balance sheet carries essentially no net leverage, and the stock trades well below its own short profitable-era valuation history. Gross margin has widened by nearly 8 points over three years even as the business has scaled.

What to Watch

Operating margin, ROIC and both growth CAGRs (EBIT, FCF) still miss this framework's absolute bars even after the turnaround — Uber is a much better business than it was in 2021, but not yet an elite one by these thresholds. Share count grew roughly 20% through FY2024 before flattening, and insider/founder ownership is thin at ≈2%, with Kalanick long gone. The biggest strategic swing factor is the AV platform bet: Uber doesn't own the autonomous technology it's aggregating, and its most important partner, Waymo, is already pulling back exclusivity in some cities and preparing to compete directly in others — a real structural risk to the "Uber as universal AV marketplace" thesis that doesn't exist in the same form for a vertically-integrated competitor like Tesla.
