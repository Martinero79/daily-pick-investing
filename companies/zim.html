ZIM's own trading history is extremely distorted by its cyclicality: FY2021-22 trailing P/E was near-zero (as low as 0.45x) because pandemic-era earnings were extraordinary relative to price, while today's 23.65x reflects both a real re-rating and a trailing-earnings base near a cyclical trough.
ZIM is the smallest of the major public container carriers and a direct, if much smaller, competitor to Maersk — roughly #10 globally by capacity with about 2.4% share, versus MSC and Maersk's combined dominance of the top of the market. Its distinguishing feature has always been an unusually asset-light model: ZIM charters roughly 87.5% of its vessels (90-94% of capacity) rather than owning them, letting it scale up or down quickly with freight rates — a strategy that shows up directly in this scorecard as one of the few criteria ZIM clears cleanly (capex intensity). The rest of the picture is dominated by extreme cyclicality: revenue and EBIT both cratered from FY2021-22's pandemic-freight-rate windfall (EBIT peaked at $6.09B in FY2022) into a genuine FY2023 net loss (-$2.70B), before a partial FY2024-25 recovery that has since faded again — Q4 2025 and Q1 2026 both posted small operating losses. Only 3 of 14 criteria are met here, and the picture is complicated further by a major corporate event: in February 2026 ZIM agreed to be acquired by Hapag-Lloyd for $35.00/share in cash, a deal shareholders overwhelmingly approved in May 2026 but which still needs sign-off from 11 Israeli government ministries and agencies before an expected late-2026 close. The stock trades around $27.01 today, roughly 23% below the deal price — a real arbitrage spread reflecting genuine completion risk, not a normal standalone valuation call. Anyone evaluating ZIM today is, in effect, evaluating a merger-arbitrage situation layered on top of one of the most cyclical scorecards in this shipping batch.

Treat the P/E comparison above as the standard scorecard signal it's designed to be, not as a forecast of where ZIM would trade absent the deal.
Reading the three P/E numbers (and why they diverge so sharply here): current P/E is today's share price divided by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings. Trailing P/E (the FY21,22,24,25 series above) is that same ratio calculated at each fiscal year-end. Forward P/E is today's price divided by analysts' consensus estimate of the next 12 months of earnings — and for ZIM the two tell almost opposite stories: trailing P/E (23.65x) looks expensive because trailing earnings are near a cyclical trough (TTM EPS just $1.15, dragged down by Q1 2026's net loss), while forward P/E (4.36x) looks extremely cheap because consensus expects earnings to rebound sharply as freight rates normalize — the same cyclicality, viewed from opposite ends of the earnings cycle. The PEG ratio (forward P/E divided by expected annual EPS growth, roughly 1.0 = "fair value") isn't meaningfully computable here — stockanalysis.com reports it as not available, and ZIM's swing from a net loss to a projected profit rebound makes a growth-rate denominator unstable anyway. For a merger-arbitrage situation layered on one of the most cyclical scorecards on this site, neither multiple should be read as a clean "cheap" or "expensive" signal on its own — the real question, as elsewhere on this page, is whether the Hapag-Lloyd deal closes near its $35.00 offer price.

What's Working

ZIM's chartered-fleet, asset-light model gives it genuine flexibility to scale capacity up or down with freight rates, and shows up cleanly in low capex intensity and a broadly flat share count. FCF has stayed positive through every year of this window, including the FY2023 loss year. The pending Hapag-Lloyd acquisition at $35.00/share, if it closes, would represent a real premium (≈30%) to today's price.

What to Watch

This is one of the most cyclical scorecards in this shipping batch: revenue, EBIT and FCF all declined sharply over the FY21-25 window despite starting from a pandemic-boom base, ROIC doesn't clear even an assumed 8.5% cost of capital, and leverage (net debt/EBITDA) is well outside this framework's comfort zone. The former anchor shareholder is fully gone. Most importantly, ZIM is currently a merger-arbitrage situation as much as a standalone equity: the ~23% spread to the $35.00 Hapag-Lloyd offer reflects real regulatory completion risk (11 Israeli government approvals still needed), and a failed deal could send the stock sharply lower given the weak underlying fundamentals shown here.
