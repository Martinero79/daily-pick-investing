Colliers is a genuine top-5 global commercial real estate services firm, and the growth headline is real: revenue was up 15.3% in FY2025 and is still compounding at a similar clip on a trailing-twelve-month basis, with Engineering (+30% YoY in Q2 2026) and Investment Management (+17%) now growing faster than the core brokerage business. But this framework is built to look past the growth headline, and what it finds underneath is less comfortable: EBIT has been essentially flat since FY2021 ($401.4M → $401.5M, roughly 0% CAGR) even as revenue grew nearly 8% a year, meaning margin has been diluted by the very M&A that's driving the growth. Return on invested capital (5.46%) doesn't even clear stockanalysis.com's own auto-computed 7.38% cost of capital, let alone this framework's 15% bar, and net debt/EBITDA has widened to roughly 3.1x-4.3x depending on the measurement window, driven by an active acquisition strategy (Harrison Street in 2024, Ayesa in 2026) that is Colliers' entire growth playbook. What's genuinely working: capex is minimal (1.4% of revenue, classically asset-light), gross margin has actually held up and improved slightly over three years, and co-founder Jay Hennick's ownership and dual-class voting structure gives him outsized, genuine skin in the game. The stock itself has been hit hard — down roughly 43.5% over the past year to $94.00 — which has pulled the trailing P/E (44.4x) well below its own FY22-25 average (73.0x), and forward P/E (11.65x) looks cheap if the growth holds, though a chunk of that "cheapness" reflects a shrinking earnings base as much as a bargain price. Net: 4 of 14 criteria met, Martinero Index 43/100, one of the lower scores on this site — a real growth story that this framework's profitability and capital-efficiency criteria are specifically designed to stress-test, and they do. This isn't a call to buy or avoid Colliers — just what the checklist says today.
Maarten's note

Colliers is a useful stress test of the checklist on a services business, and it doesn't flatter the company: growth is real and the Engineering/Investment Management diversification story is genuinely interesting, but EBIT going nowhere for four years while ROIC sits below the firm's own cost of capital is not a footnote, it's the headline. The founder-control structure (Hennick's dual-class shares) cuts both ways — real alignment, but also real concentration of power in one person's hands. The stock's down over 40% in a year, which has pulled the multiple well below its own average, but a chunk of that "cheapness" is a shrinking earnings base, not a pure bargain.

Trailing & Forward P/E — FY22-25 & Current
Current trailing P/E of 44.39x sits well below the FY22-25 average of roughly 73.00x — but that average is pulled up by FY2023's 91.56x, set during a trough year for net income (only $65.5M), so part of today's "discount" reflects a smaller earnings base as much as a genuinely cheap price. Forward P/E (11.65x) is only available as a current figure — historical forward-P/E data for Colliers isn't reliably available for prior fiscal years, so only the single current point is shown, flagged as such rather than estimated.
Trailing P/E Forward P/E (current only)
Reading the three P/E numbers: current P/E is today's share price divided by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings — a backward-looking snapshot at today's price. Trailing P/E (the FY22-25 series above) is that same backward-looking ratio calculated at each fiscal year-end, showing how the market has re-rated the stock over time relative to what it had already earned. Forward P/E is today's share price divided by analysts' consensus estimate of the next 12 months of earnings — forward-looking, and generally the more useful of the two for judging whether a stock is priced fairly relative to where its earnings are headed. For Colliers specifically: trailing P/E (44.39x) looks cheap next to its own FY22-25 average (73.00x), but forward P/E (11.65x) is dramatically cheaper still, implying the market expects a sharp near-term earnings recovery as recent acquisitions integrate and margin pressure eases. One more lens worth a glance: the PEG ratio, forward P/E divided by expected annual EPS growth, which sanity-checks whether a "cheap" multiple is actually cheap once growth is accounted for. Using forward P/E (11.65x) against the 3-year consensus EPS growth forecast (12.94%, per stockanalysis.com), Colliers' PEG works out to roughly 0.90 — just under the traditional 1.0 "fair value" line, suggesting the stock isn't just cheaper than its own history, it's also reasonably priced relative to expected growth, assuming that growth actually shows up.
Balance sheet: net debt of $2,020M against FY2025 EBITDA of roughly $657.6M (EBIT + D&A) gives a Net Debt/EBITDA of ≈3.07x — above this framework's <2x bar. On a trailing-twelve-month basis (through Jun 2026), net debt has widened further to $2,977M against TTM EBITDA of $693.5M, or ≈4.29x, reflecting $973M spent on acquisitions (chiefly the Ayesa engineering deal and continued Harrison Street integration) in the past year — leverage that's a direct, deliberate consequence of Colliers' roll-up growth strategy, not a distress signal, but a real and rising number this framework is designed to flag. ROIC of 5.46% doesn't clear stockanalysis.com's own auto-computed 7.38% WACC, let alone this framework's 15% bar. Share count is up 1.41% YoY — mild but real dilution, partly funding the acquisition strategy (2024 alone saw $332M of common stock issuance, likely tied to the Harrison Street deal).

What's Working

Revenue growth is real and still double-digit on a trailing basis, gross margin has actually improved over three years despite the M&A noise, capex is genuinely minimal (1.4% of revenue), the current entry multiple sits well below Colliers' own five-year average, and Jay Hennick's founder ownership plus dual-class voting control give management real, durable skin in the game.

What to Watch

EBIT has gone essentially nowhere for four years while revenue grew ≈8% a year — the acquisition strategy (Harrison Street, Ayesa) is growing the top line but not yet the bottom line, and ROIC sits below even Colliers' own cost of capital. Net debt/EBITDA has widened to roughly 3-4x funding that strategy, well above this framework's leverage bar, and share count is drifting up too. Whether the Engineering and Investment Management bets convert into real operating leverage — or keep diluting margin as they scale — is the central question for Colliers from here.
