DNB is Norway's largest financial group, with market-leading positions in personal banking, corporate lending and asset management, a strong Norwegian-krone deposit base and roughly 11,600 employees. FY2025 total income was NOK 87.9bn (+3.0%) and net income attributable to shareholders NOK 41.9bn, a 15.9% return on equity, with a 38.0% cost/income ratio and 17.9% CET1 ratio. Over FY2021–FY2025 income compounded at ~11.6% a year and net income at ~14.5% — both below this framework's 15% bar, though not by much, and boosted by the rate cycle and a FY2021 base with a 10.7% ROE. Capital return is a defining feature: FY2025 dividends of NOK 18.00 plus buybacks of about NOK 6.79 per share were roughly 86% of earnings, and the share count is down ~6% since FY2021. The Norwegian state owns about 34%. The caveats: profit has now fallen two years running (FY25 net income -4% and 2026 YTD lower again), the Q3 2026 print showed lower lending margins and competition, and at ~11.7x trailing earnings and ~1.7x book value the shares sit above their own FY2021–FY2025 averages (~9.6x and ~1.36x).
Framing note — why this scorecard looks different for a bank: DNB is a bank, so the standard revenue/EBIT/FCF/net-debt framework does not map cleanly onto it. The same substitutions used on the JPMorgan Chase page are applied here, all flagged inline: Total Income stands in for revenue (criterion 1); Net Income substitutes for both EBIT (criterion 2) and Free Cash Flow (criterion 4) — a bank's cash flow statement is dominated by deposit and trading-book swings that do not map to a “free cash flow” concept, so criteria 2 and 4 are necessarily identical here, a genuine limitation of the adaptation and not a workaround; ROE vs. an assumed ~10% cost of equity substitutes for ROIC vs. WACC (criterion 7; DNB reports ROE, not ROTCE; a ~10% cost of equity is used, the low end of the 10–11% range, given the stock's very low beta (~0.4) and AA-/Aa2 credit ratings); CET1 ratio vs. the regulatory requirement substitutes for net debt/EBITDA (criterion 8), scored on the cushion over the requirement; and (dividends + buybacks) ÷ net income substitutes for FCF conversion (criterion 9). Entry multiple (criterion 10) uses P/E as the scored measure with P/B shown alongside, each against its own FY2021–FY2025 history. Criterion 14 uses the efficiency (cost/income) ratio because banks report no gross profit; a falling ratio counts as a margin rise. Everything is reported in NOK and nothing is converted.

What the latest quarter showed (Q3 2026)

Q3 2026 total income was NOK 21.2bn on the S&P Global series (-3.1% YoY) and net income NOK 9.4bn (-8.7% YoY), a third consecutive quarter of lower profit than a year earlier. DNB's own headline figures, as relayed by Finwire/Placera, differ slightly: total income NOK 21.7bn (-4.4% YoY), net profit NOK 9.84bn (EPS NOK 6.52 vs. 6.98), with pre-tax profit of NOK 12.53bn above consensus (NOK 11.65bn) but net profit below consensus (NOK 10.68bn). Net interest income was held back by lower lending margins and competition, while commissions benefited from corporate finance and asset-management activity. A pre-close call flagged a roughly NOK 1bn loss on the Luminor divestment in Q3, which strengthens capital ratios. DNB reiterated its targets of ROE above 14%, cost/income below 40%, 3–4% annual organic loan growth and fee growth above 9%. Goldman Sachs downgraded the stock to Sell in the summer, citing margin compression in Norway (third-party view).

Market position, honestly stated: DNB is Norway's largest bank and financial group, with leading shares in personal and corporate banking and in asset management, which supports criterion #5. The Norwegian market is concentrated — DNB, the SpareBank 1 alliance, Nordea and a handful of others dominate — but a large number of regional savings banks and foreign branches keep it contested, and recent commentary points to competition pressuring lending margins. Concentration is therefore scored 3/5 (Not Met on the Met/Not Met convention, 2 points on the graduated scale). The Norwegian state, as the largest owner, is also a relevant consideration for governance and for how the bank balances commercial and policy aims.

Governance & Capital Notes

The Norwegian state owns 34.4% (Dec 31, 2025) and, together with the DNB Savings Bank Foundation (8.9%) and Folketrygdfondet (6.5%), the top three holders control roughly half the shares. That makes DNB effectively state-anchored: the state's long-term holding supports stability and alignment on capital, but it is not a founder or an executive insider, and its interests can include non-commercial aims. It is scored on the >30% controlling-stake band (5 points) and flagged as such. Management ownership is negligible. DNB's policy is a payout of more than 50% of profit as dividends, with excess capital returned via buybacks; the 2025 total payout was about 86%.

What's Working

Norway's dominant bank, with a 15.9% ROE, 38.0% cost/income and 17.9% CET1 ratio. Profits are more than 1.7x FY2021 levels, the payout is generous (about 86% of earnings via dividends and buybacks) and the share count is down ~6%. Credit quality is strong (negative net impairments in FY24–FY25), the bank is rated AA-/Aa2, and a state-anchored shareholder base supports stability. Capex is light and the Luminor divestment should lift capital further.

What to Watch

Growth has stalled: income growth fell from +19.1% (FY23) to +3.0% (FY25) and net income has declined two years running, with Q3 2026 profit below a year earlier on lower lending margins and competition. The shares have re-rated to ~11.7x earnings versus a ~9.6x average even as profit falls, with consensus at 'Hold'. CET1 fell 1.5pp in FY25 (still above the estimated requirement), cost/income has crept up from its FY23 low, and results depend on Norwegian rates and oil-linked economic strength. State ownership aligns long-term stability but also introduces non-commercial considerations.
