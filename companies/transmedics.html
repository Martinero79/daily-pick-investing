TransMedics Group, Inc. (Andover, Massachusetts; founded 1998 by Dr. Waleed Hassanein, who is still President and CEO) makes the Organ Care System (OCS), a portable device that keeps donor hearts, lungs and livers warm and perfused outside the body, instead of packing them in ice. Since 2021 it has sold more than the device: through its National OCS Program (NOP) it offers transplant centres an outsourced package of organ procurement, OCS perfusion management and transport logistics, flown on its own aircraft (22 owned Embraer Phenom 300s in the US at end-2025, plus nine acquired with PAD Aviation in Germany from July 2026). Revenue rose from $30.3M in FY2021 to $605.5M in FY2025 (+111% a year), and GAAP operating income turned positive in Q4 2023. The model has two revenue lines: product (OCS disposables, $113.4M in Q3 2026) and service (NOP procurement and logistics, $89.6M, growing faster at +60%). That mix shift is why gross margin has slid from about 70% in FY2021-22 to 58.5% in Q3 2026. US liver is the engine: $459.4M, or 76%, of FY2025 revenue. Q3 2026 beat expectations on revenue ($203.0M vs. roughly $177-182M consensus) and the full-year guide was raised, yet the stock sits about 40% below its 52-week high of $156 because growth is guided to slow to 26-28% in FY2026 and 20-25% in 2027 (management indication per a conference-transcript summary), costs are rising faster than revenue, and the balance sheet now carries $351.5M of finance-lease debt alongside $460M of convertible notes.
The pitch, in one paragraph: an “aviation-backed organ transplant monopoly” and a “mid-cap kicker” for a tenbagger from roughly $3.3B. Checked against the data: the $3.3B starting cap is right ($3.24B today). What holds: TransMedics is the only company with an FDA-PMA-approved portable warm perfusion platform covering heart, lung and liver, and the only one that bundles procurement and its own air fleet. What does not: it is not a monopoly (OrganOx, owned by Terumo, has had an FDA-approved normothermic liver device since 2021; Paragonix, owned by Getinge, and XVIVO sell cold preservation; ice-box cold storage remains the default for most transplants), aviation is as much a cost and capital burden as a moat, and a tenbagger would need about $32B of market cap, which the arithmetic in Section 6 shows is a very demanding ask. Not a recommendation.

TransMedics is the leader in portable warm (normothermic) perfusion and is unique in bundling logistics, but it competes for the same transplant decisions as cold-preservation devices and a rival liver perfusion device. Claim 1: “Monopoly” — PARTLY HOLDS in one narrow sense, FAILS as stated. In its own proxy the company describes the OCS as the first and only multi-organ, portable, warm perfusion platform with FDA PMA, and no other listed company combines a perfusion device, procurement and an owned air fleet. That is a differentiated leader, and US liver revenue ($459.4M in FY2025, +48%) shows the leadership converts into sales.
Why “monopoly” does not hold: (1) OrganOx metra, now under Terumo, has been FDA-approved for normothermic liver perfusion since 2021 and has a $1.5B parent behind it; liver is where TransMedics earns three-quarters of its revenue. (2) Cold static storage and devices such as Paragonix's remain the default for most of the ~49,000 US transplants a year; about 5,100 US transplants used the OCS in 2025, roughly one in ten across all organs, so it is a leader in a minority niche, not a market owner. (3) Concentration is also inside the company: liver alone is 76% of revenue, heart 18% and lung 2% (FY2025), and the company lists dependence on a limited number of customers (transplant programs) as a risk (percentages not disclosed in the sources I read). (4) Regulatory and policy exposure is real: a CMS final rule on organ procurement organisations, FDA trial outcomes (ENHANCE, DENOVO), a disclosed material weakness in internal control and securities litigation.
Claim 2: “Aviation as moat” — PARTLY HOLDS. Owning 22 Phenom 300s in the US (and nine more through PAD Aviation in Europe, with 40+ pilots and an EASA operating certificate) makes the NOP hard to copy quickly: a rival would need aircraft, pilots, clinical teams and procurement, not only a device. Management cites pricing improvements and less reliance on charter lift for gross margin gains excluding PAD (61.1% in Q3).
Why aviation is also a drag: capex hit 63% of revenue in FY2023 and 29% in FY2024 for aircraft; a $351.5M finance-lease liability appeared in 2026; interest expense has doubled; gross margin has fallen about 10 points since FY2022 as the logistics-heavy service line grew (service revenue +60% in Q3; cost of service revenue is 68% of service revenue vs 21% for product); and the PAD Aviation consolidation diluted Q3 gross margin by 260bp. The moat is real, but it is bought with capital intensity, debt-like obligations and lower margins, which is the opposite of the asset-light profile that usually supports a tenbagger case.
Claim 3: “mid-cap kicker” — DEPENDS ON DEFINITION. At $3.24B the stock is a small to lower-mid cap depending on the index provider. It is small enough for a large multiple, but that is tested in Section 6, not assumed.

What's Working

Revenue has multiplied twentyfold in four years and Q3 2026 was a record, with organic growth of 35% and a guidance raise. GAAP operating profit has been positive for twelve straight quarters and FY2025 FCF was $133.6M. The product is differentiated: the only FDA-PMA portable warm perfusion platform across heart, lung and liver, bundled with a procurement and aviation network that rivals do not offer. Next-generation OCS trials (ENHANCE Heart Part B, DENOVO Lung) and Europe (Italy tender, PAD Aviation) open new segments, the stock trades at about 4.5x sales vs a 12x five-year average, the CEO-founder owns 4.3%, and cash of $493M covers the $460M converts.

What to Watch

Growth is guided to slow (26-28% in FY2026, about 23-29% implied for Q4, 20-25% indicated for 2027) while operating expenses grew 48% in Q3; 9M GAAP operating income is down 25%. Gross margin has fallen about 10 points in four years and ROIC (~7%) is below the assumed cost of capital. The aviation fleet and a new $351.5M finance lease have lifted debt-like obligations to about 3x EBITDA, with $460M of converts due in 2028 near a ~$94 conversion price. Shares are up 25% since FY2021 and SBC is ~6% of revenue. Liver is 76% of revenue, competition (OrganOx/Terumo, Paragonix/Getinge, XVIVO) is well funded, and a CMS OPO rule, FDA trials, a disclosed material weakness and securities litigation are open risks. Several inputs are third-party or unverified: ROIC and WACC, the conversion price, market-share figures, consensus numbers and the 2027 growth indication.

A six-point checklist for stocks that might multiply tenfold, tested against TransMedics' numbers. Result: 3 pass (starting size, profitability flip, P/S warning not triggered), 1 partial (revenue acceleration), 2 fail (ROIC, dilution). These tests are separate from, and do not change, the 14-criterion scorecard.
1. Small starting cap (under ~$5–10B) — PASS. Market cap is about $3.24B ($93.42 × 34.73M basic shares, Nov 24, 2026 close; about $3.8B on the 40.8M diluted count), matching the pitch's ~$3.3B. For context it was about $4.0B at the end of 2025 ($115.68 × 34.3M) and about $2.1B at the end of 2024; the stock is 40% below its 52-week high of $156.
