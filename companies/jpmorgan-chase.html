JPMorgan Chase is the largest bank in the United States by assets (~$4.4tn) and the most profitable, generating $57.0bn of net income on $185.6bn of net revenue in FY2025 with a 20% return on tangible common equity — a level most global banks would consider excellent. The honest case for including it on a growth/AI-tilted scorecard rests on scale, capital strength and consistent capital return, not growth: revenue compounded at ~11% annually over FY2021–FY2025 (short of this framework's 15% bar) and the net-income proxy used for the EBIT/FCF criteria compounds at just ~4% over the same window — though that figure is distorted by outsized 2021 loan-loss-reserve releases that made FY2021 an unusually easy earnings comparison. CET1 capital sits at 14.5%, comfortably above the ~11.5% regulatory floor, and the bank has bought back stock and raised its dividend every year of the window, cutting share count by roughly 14% since FY2021. The catch: the stock now trades at ~15.5x trailing earnings and ~3.4x tangible book value, both well above its own FY2021–FY2025 range — the "cheap megabank" case is much weaker today than the fundamentals-only case for owning it.
Framing note — why this scorecard looks different for a bank: JPMorgan is the second financial-services company on this site after NN Group (an insurer), and the standard revenue/EBIT/FCF/net-debt framework doesn't map cleanly onto a bank either. Substitutions used throughout, all flagged inline: Total Net Revenue stands in for revenue (criterion 1); Net Income substitutes for both EBIT (criterion 2) and Free Cash Flow (criterion 4) — a bank's cash flow statement is dominated by deposit and trading-book swings that don't map to a "free cash flow" concept, so criteria 2 and 4 are necessarily correlated here, which is a genuine limitation of adapting the framework, not a workaround to inflate the score; ROTCE vs. an assumed ~10–11% cost of equity substitutes for ROIC vs. WACC (criterion 7); CET1 ratio vs. regulatory minimum substitutes for net debt/EBITDA (criterion 8), since banks run leveraged balance sheets by design; and dividends + buybacks ÷ net income substitutes for FCF conversion (criterion 9), since FCF/EBIT would be circular once both use the same net-income proxy. Entry multiple (criterion 10) uses both P/E and P/TBV, the two standard bank valuation lenses.

Market position, honestly stated: JPMorgan is the largest U.S. bank by assets (~$4.4tn) and by market capitalization, and the most profitable — a genuine #1 position that satisfies criterion #5. But U.S. banking is not a monopoly or even a tight duopoly/oligopoly: Bank of America (~$3.3tn assets), Wells Fargo, Citigroup and Goldman Sachs/Morgan Stanley (in investment banking and trading) are all large, well-capitalized competitors, and thousands of regional banks, credit unions and fintechs compete at the margins. That combination — clear #1 share, but a genuinely competitive top tier below it — is why criterion #5 (market share) scores Met while criterion #6 (market concentration) scores Not Met.

What's Working

Undisputed #1 U.S. bank by assets and profitability, with a 20% ROTCE that comfortably clears a reasonable cost-of-equity hurdle in every year of the window. CET1 capital sits well above regulatory minimums even as the bar itself has eased. Capital return is real and accelerating — buybacks tripled from FY23 to FY25 and the dividend has been raised twice in 2025 alone — cutting share count by roughly 14% since FY2021. Efficiency ratio has structurally improved from 59% to 52% over the period, and the physical/capex footprint remains genuinely light for a bank of this size.

What to Watch

Revenue growth (~11%/yr) and the net-income proxy used for EBIT/FCF (~4%/yr, distorted by an easy FY2021 comparison) both miss this framework's 15% growth bar — this is a scale-and-capital-return story, not a growth story. The stock has re-rated well above its own FY2021–FY2025 range on both P/E (~15.5x vs. ~11.1x average) and P/TBV (~3.44x vs. ~2.13x average), so today's entry point is priced for continued excellence, not for a margin of safety. Insider ownership is negligible relative to the 5% bar, typical of a bank this size with no founding family. U.S. banking, while led by JPMorgan, remains a genuinely competitive top tier rather than a tight oligopoly.
