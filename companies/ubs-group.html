UBS is the world’s largest private bank, with USD 7.3tn of invested assets, and since absorbing Credit Suisse in 2023 it is also Switzerland’s only global universal bank. The honest case rests on franchise scale, capital strength and a rising capital return, not growth: total revenue compounded at 9.1% a year over FY2021–FY2025 and net profit at just 1.0% ($7.5B → $7.8B), because integration costs absorbed most of the benefit of the larger business — the cost/income ratio only fell from 72.8% (FY22) to 71.2% (FY25), still well above the ~67% management targets for 2028. Momentum has since improved: 1H26 net profit was $5.8B (vs. $4.1B in 1H25) and return on tangible equity reached 13.9%. The CET1 ratio of 14.4% is comfortable, and the dividend has risen every year of the window (USD 0.50 → 1.10) alongside a $3bn annual buyback. The catch is twofold: Swiss lawmakers are debating much tougher capital rules for foreign subsidiaries (UBS has said it is weighing options including relocating its headquarters), and the stock at ~16x trailing earnings and ~1.6x book trades above its own FY21–FY25 average. Latest reported quarter is 2Q26 (published July 2026); 3Q26 results are due Oct 28, 2026 and are not reflected here.
Framing note — why this scorecard looks different for a bank: the standard revenue/EBIT/FCF/net-debt framework does not map cleanly onto a bank, so the same substitutions are used here as on the JPMorgan Chase page, all flagged inline: Total revenue (net interest + fee + trading and other income) stands in for revenue (criterion 1); Net profit attributable to shareholders substitutes for both EBIT (criterion 2) and Free Cash Flow (criterion 4) — a bank's cash-flow statement is dominated by deposit and trading-book swings, so criteria 2 and 4 are necessarily identical here, a genuine limitation of adapting the framework; return on equity vs. an assumed ~10% cost of equity substitutes for ROIC vs. WACC (criterion 7); CET1 ratio vs. the regulatory requirement substitutes for net debt/EBITDA (criterion 8); (dividends + buybacks) ÷ net profit substitutes for FCF conversion (criterion 9); entry multiple (criterion 10) blends P/E and P/B against the bank's own FY21–FY25 history; and margin stability (criterion 14) uses the cost/income (efficiency) ratio because a bank has no gross-profit line. The criterion-3 “operating margin” is the pre-tax margin (pre-tax profit ÷ total revenue). Banks are valuation-sensitive and capital-regulated, so the bands are applied mechanically and no score has been rounded up. UBS-specific: UBS reports in USD but its primary listing trades in CHF, so financials are shown in USD (nothing converted) and price/valuation in CHF; P/E multiples use S&P’s currency-consistent figures. FY2023 contains a ~$27.4B accounting gain from the Credit Suisse acquisition (negative goodwill), which is excluded from revenue and flagged wherever it distorts a ratio.

Market position, honestly stated: UBS is the largest wealth manager in the world by invested assets ($7.3tn, ~52% of revenue from Global Wealth Management), the #1 bank in Switzerland, and a top-tier investment bank with particular strength in equities and M&A advisory — a genuine #1 for criterion #5. But the markets it competes in are not concentrated: global wealth management has hundreds of competitors from JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley to boutique private banks, and the Swiss home market still has strong cantonal and cooperative banks. That is why #5 scores 5/5 while market concentration (#6) scores only 2/5 and is Not Met. Its G-SIB status cuts both ways: it supports client trust but invites the heavy, still-unsettled Swiss capital regime discussed on page 4.

Reading the valuation: at ~16.2x trailing earnings and ~1.62x book, UBS trades above its own history on both lenses (P/E average 13.6x excluding FY23, P/B average 1.20x), a blended ratio of 1.27, which lands in the 1.15–1.30 band (1/5). Governance & Capital Notes

UBS has no controlling shareholder or founding family; the largest disclosed holders are index/active managers (BlackRock 5.01% as last disclosed in 2023, Norges Bank below 3% as of Feb 2026). Directors and executives hold well under 1%. Employee share plans are significant in absolute terms but the aggregate stake was not available from the sources used and is not credited. Governance risk is regulatory and political rather than ownership-based: the Swiss Federal Council and Parliament are deciding how much extra CET1 UBS must hold against foreign subsidiaries (UBS estimates ~$16B under the 90% version approved by the Council of States, ~$22B under the original proposal), with final terms expected in 2027 and implementation not before 2028.

What's Working

The largest private bank in the world with a clear #1 position, a 14.4% CET1 ratio comfortably above management’s ~14% target, and a rising shareholder return (dividend $0.50 → $1.10, $3bn buyback, share count down ~9%). Cost/income has improved from 72.8% to 71.2% over three years and 1H26 profit was ~40% higher than 1H25 as integration synergies land; management targets a ~67% ratio and ~18% return on CET1 capital by 2028. Capex is low for a group of its size.

What to Watch

Five-year revenue (9.1%/yr) and net-profit (1.0%/yr) growth are both far below this framework’s 15% bar, FY25 ROE (8.9%) was under the cost-of-equity assumption, and the pre-tax margin (17.7%) is below 20%. The stock trades above its own P/E and P/B history, and the biggest swing factor is political: Swiss rules that could require ~$16–22B of extra CET1 — or a relocation/merger in response — are unresolved. Insider ownership is negligible and the home-market regulator is also the main risk to returns.
