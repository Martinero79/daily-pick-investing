Quarterly Development & Accounting/Disclosure Scrutiny
Latest reported period: Q2 2026 (three months to Jun 30, 2026). This page also covers a set of legitimate, well-documented areas where analysts and forensic-accounting courses have historically scrutinized Netflix's financials and disclosures — not an accusation of wrongdoing.
Q2 2026 Headlines

Revenue reached $12.56B, up 13.36% YoY from $11.08B in Q2 2025 — a step down from the mid-teens growth rates of 2024-2025 (Q1 2026 was +16.19%), consistent with the deceleration a much larger revenue base naturally produces. Operating margin was 33.38% for the quarter, and UCAN (US & Canada) remains the largest region at $5.43B, with EMEA close behind at $4.03B. Netflix no longer discloses a quarterly paid-membership figure alongside these results (see callout below).

Gross Margin Trend (FY2021-FY2025)
Gross margin has expanded every year except FY2022, from 41.64% to 48.49% — a +9.12pp improvement versus FY2022 (the window used for criterion #14), driven by slowing content-spend growth relative to revenue and a growing, higher-margin ad-supported tier.
Accounting & disclosure scrutiny, honestly stated: this section exists because it's the kind of thing a forensic-accounting or short-selling-focused CPA course would flag as worth understanding, not because Netflix has been accused of fraud — it hasn't. Four genuinely documented areas: (1) Content amortization — Netflix expenses over 90% of an average content asset's cost within its first four years of availability (a declining-balance rate of roughly 45%/year), an accelerated policy that several analysts have argued is judgmental enough that reported EBIT and margins are sensitive to management's own assumptions about content lifespan; Netflix's own auditor discloses this is a complex, subjective area to test. (2) Discontinued subscriber disclosure — starting with Q1 2025 results, Netflix stopped reporting quarterly paid-membership counts, shifting emphasis to revenue, operating margin, FCF and engagement instead; this was widely covered as a reduction in transparency into what had been a key non-financial KPI, and the stock fell on the announcement. (3) GAAP-vs-FCF gap — this gap has narrowed substantially as Netflix has become durably FCF-positive (FCF now exceeds net income in most recent years, e.g. $9.46B FCF vs $10.98B net income in FY2025), which is a genuine improvement versus the 2018-2021 era when heavy content-spend accounting made the two numbers diverge sharply. (4) Other watch items — password-sharing crackdown revenue recognition and a growing ad-tier are both relatively new revenue streams with less multi-year track record than the core subscription business, and stock-based comp has grown alongside headcount and margin expansion.
Market position, honestly stated: Netflix's #1 position by both revenue and global paid memberships (325M+) is real and durable, and it scores Met on criterion #5. But streaming is a genuinely crowded, multi-front market — Amazon Prime Video, Max (which overtook Disney+ in early 2026), Disney+, Apple TV+ and YouTube (Premium plus ad-supported) are all real, well-funded competitors at meaningful scale. That's why market concentration (#6) scores 4/5 (fragmented) rather than 1-2, and is Not met under this framework's inverted scoring — leadership in a competitive field, not a moat built on scarcity.

Trailing & Forward P/E — FY21-25 & Current
Current trailing P/E of 24.19x sits well below the FY21-25 average of roughly 40.1x — driven almost entirely by the stock's ~43% pullback from its June 2025 all-time high rather than any deterioration in trailing earnings, which kept growing throughout. Forward P/E (22.15x) sits close to trailing P/E, and the PEG ratio (see explainer below) is close to the traditional "fair value" line.
Trailing P/E Forward P/E
Reading the three P/E numbers: current P/E is today's share price divided by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings — a backward-looking snapshot at today's price. Trailing P/E (the FY21-25 series above) is that same backward-looking ratio calculated at each fiscal year-end, showing how the market has re-rated the stock over time relative to what it had already earned. Forward P/E is today's share price divided by analysts' consensus estimate of the next 12 months of earnings — forward-looking, and the more useful of the two for judging whether a stock is priced fairly relative to where its earnings are actually headed. For Netflix specifically: trailing P/E (24.19x) and forward P/E (22.15x) are close together, meaning the market isn't pricing in a dramatic near-term earnings inflection either way — it's a re-rating story, not an earnings-estimate story. One more lens worth a glance: the PEG ratio, forward P/E divided by expected annual EPS growth, which sanity-checks whether a given multiple is cheap once growth is accounted for. Netflix's PEG currently sits around 1.04 — right around the traditional 1.0 "fair value" line, suggesting the discount versus Netflix's own history is real but the stock isn't obviously cheap once analysts' forward earnings growth (consensus EPS growth ≈22% over 3 years) is factored in.

ROIC of 32.43% clears both the assumed 9.0% WACC and this framework's 15% absolute bar by a wide margin.
