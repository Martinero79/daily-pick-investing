HSBC is one of the world's largest banks (total assets ~$3tn+; ~206,000 employees), earning most of its profit in Hong Kong, the UK and its wholesale franchise. FY2025 net operating income (HSBC's “revenue”) was $68.3bn and net income to ordinary shareholders $21.1bn, with a reported RoTE of 13.3% (17.2% excluding notable items) and a CET1 ratio of 14.9%; H1 2026 annualised RoTE stepped up to 18.2%. The honest case for the scorecard is capital strength, returns and capital return, not growth: revenue compounded at ~8% over FY2021–FY2025 and the net-income proxy at ~14%, both under this framework's 15% bar — and both measured from a low-rate, low-earnings FY2021–22 base. The share count fell ~13.5% over the window. The catch is valuation: after the share price roughly tripled from 448.65p (end-2021) to 1,352p, HSBC trades at ~14.6x trailing earnings and ~1.5x book versus FY21–FY25 year-end averages of ~9.1x and ~0.86x. Cost efficiency has also drifted from 48.5% (FY23) to 53.4% (FY25), and CET1 fell to 14.1% after the Hang Seng privatisation.
Framing note — why this scorecard looks different for a bank: HSBC Holdings plc is a bank, so the standard revenue/EBIT/FCF/net-debt framework does not map cleanly. The same substitutions used on the JPMorgan Chase page are applied here, all flagged inline: Total net revenue stands in for revenue (criterion 1); net income attributable to ordinary shareholders substitutes for both EBIT (criterion 2) and free cash flow (criterion 4) — a bank's cash flow statement is dominated by deposit and trading-book swings, so criteria 2 and 4 are necessarily correlated, a genuine limitation of the adaptation and not a workaround; RoTE vs. an assumed ~10–11% cost of equity substitutes for ROIC vs. WACC (criterion 7; 10.5% midpoint used for the points); CET1 ratio vs. the regulatory requirement substitutes for net debt/EBITDA (criterion 8; points banded by the CET1 cushion: 4pp+ = 5, 3–4pp = 4, 2–3pp = 3, 1–2pp = 2, 0–1pp = 1, below requirement = 0); (dividends + buybacks) ÷ net income substitutes for FCF conversion (criterion 9); criterion 10 averages the P/E and P/B ratios versus the bank's own FY21–FY25 year-end history; and criterion 14 uses the efficiency (cost/income) ratio because banks report no gross margin. HSBC reports in US dollars while its London shares trade in pence; nothing is converted on this page except the P/E and P/B history, which uses S&P's own conversion.

RoTE Trend — bank analog to ROIC
Reported return on average tangible equity, full year, vs. an assumed ~10–11% cost of equity. FY21–FY22 are as reported under IFRS 4. FY25 was 13.3% reported (17.2% excluding notable items); H1 2026 annualised 18.2%. FY21 and FY22 sat below the hurdle.
Market position, honestly stated: HSBC is among the very largest banks in Europe by total assets (~$3tn+), the leading international bank in Hong Kong (one of the note-issuing banks) and a top-tier UK retail and commercial bank — enough for a “strong #2-tier” reading (4/5) on market share, so criterion #5 is Met. It is not a clear global #1: US and Chinese banks are larger by assets and market value, and in UK retail it competes with Lloyds, Barclays and NatWest, so market concentration (criterion #6) scores a moderate 3/5 and is Not Met. G-SIB status brings higher capital buffers and tighter regulation, and the Hong Kong/mainland China exposure ties results to geopolitics, property credit and rate paths in two regions.

Governance & Capital Notes

No controlling shareholder or founder: HSBC's largest disclosed holders are BlackRock (~9.1%) and Ping An Asset Management (~8.0%, a strategic holder), plus the depositary for its US ADSs. Directors and executives hold well under 1% in aggregate, so alignment comes from pay and the capital-return policy, not ownership (criterion 12 scores 0/5). Capital policy: CET1 target range 14–14.5%, a 50% dividend payout target for 2026–2028, and buybacks that were paused after the Hang Seng privatisation until CET1 returned toward range (up to $1bn announced with the interims). As a G-SIB, HSBC also carries additional loss-absorbing-capacity and buffer requirements.

What's Working

A large, globally diversified franchise with strong capital (CET1 14.1–14.9%) and a returns step-up (H1 2026 annualised RoTE 18.2%). Capital return is heavy — about 90% of FY25 earnings returned through dividends and $6bn of buybacks — the share count is down ~13.5% since FY21, capex is light (~2% of revenue), and net income compounded ~14% a year from a low FY21 base.

What to Watch

Growth is below the framework's 15% bar (~8% revenue, ~14% net income) and the cost/income ratio has worsened since FY23 (48.5% → 53.4%). The stock has re-rated ~3x since 2021 and sits at ~1.6–1.8x its own P/E and P/B averages, leaving little cushion; the ~9.8x forward P/E depends on a clean earnings step-up. CET1 dropped to 14.1% after the Hang Seng privatisation (further buybacks depend on returning to the 14–14.5% range), results carry Hong Kong/mainland China property and geopolitical exposure, UK wealth restructuring is under way, notable items repeatedly distort reported numbers, and insider ownership is negligible.
