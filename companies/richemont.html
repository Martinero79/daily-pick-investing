Richemont is the Swiss luxury conglomerate built by Johann Rupert's family around Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels — together the group's Jewellery Maisons, which now generate roughly three-quarters of revenue and have been the standout performer across the entire global luxury sector through the 2023-2025 slowdown that hit fashion-led peers like LVMH and Kering much harder. The group's second leg, seven Specialist Watchmakers (Vacheron Constantin, A. Lange & Söhne, IWC, Jaeger-LeCoultre, Piaget, Panerai, Roger Dubuis), has been structurally weaker and more cyclical, with sales declining in FY2026 even as jewelry boomed. A smaller "Other" division (Chloé, Alaïa, Montblanc, dunhill) rounds out the group; Richemont largely exited the Net-a-Porter/YOOX e-commerce business (YNAP) in FY2023, and the associated writedown briefly wrecked that year's reported net income and P/E — a one-off distortion, not an operating problem (see page 4). Financially the picture is one of real but unspectacular growth layered on top of an exceptionally strong balance sheet: revenue and EBIT CAGRs both run well under this framework's 15% bar over FY2022-26, and operating margin has compressed from a 25.5% FY2023 peak to 20.75% in FY2026 on gold-price and FX headwinds hitting jewelry input costs. Against that, Richemont carries a net cash position (€9.1B as of Jun 2026) rather than net debt, ROIC of 18.14% comfortably clears an estimated ~9.25% cost of capital, and the current trailing P/E sits almost exactly in line with the company's own five-year average once the FY2023 distortion is excluded. Q1 FY2027 (quarter ended Jun 30, 2026) showed the jewelry-led momentum accelerating further: group sales rose 20% at constant exchange rates to €6.33B, with Jewellery Maisons up 24% — a seventh consecutive quarter of double-digit growth — and, notably, Watchmakers returning to growth (+8%) after a soft FY2026.
Why operating margin is compressing despite strong sales: gross margin has fallen from 68.74% (FY2023) to 64.39% (FY2026), a real ~4.3-point decline, driven substantially by the surge in gold prices (a direct input cost for the Jewellery Maisons) and adverse currency effects, only partly offset by pricing and mix. Management has flagged this explicitly on recent earnings calls as a genuine cost headwind, not a demand problem — unit demand for Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels has kept accelerating even as gross margin compresses.

Ownership & Voting Structure
Rupert family, via Compagnie Financière Rupert: 10.18% of capital — but 50.60% of voting rights
Free float / institutional (incl. UBS Fund Mgmt Switzerland, 3.02% of votes): ≈89.3% of capital, ≈49.1% of votes
Treasury shares: 0.51% of capital / 0.28% of votes (RSU/PSU hedging)
Capital vs. votes — the two numbers explained: Richemont has two share classes, currently 537,582,089 'A' shares (listed on SIX Swiss Exchange and the JSE) and exactly the same number, 537,582,089, of unlisted 'B' shares (held solely by Compagnie Financière Rupert, the Rupert family's holding vehicle). By the company's Articles, the B-share class as a whole controls a fixed 50% of votes at shareholder meetings, while the A-share class as a whole controls the other 50% — regardless of the fact both classes have equal share counts. Because Compagnie Financière Rupert owns all the B shares plus a small 6.4 million A shares, it ends up controlling the entire 50% B-class vote pool plus a sliver of the A-class pool: 50.60% of total votes, as of 31 Mar 2026. But because B shares represent only 9.1% of total equity capital (A shares carry a CHF1.00 par value vs. B shares' CHF0.10 par, a 10:1 economic ratio), the same stake is only 10.18% of the Company's economic capital. In short: a small, non-controlling economic stake with an absolute, controlling voting majority — a genuinely unusual structure, source: richemont.com capital-structure disclosure (data as of 31 Mar 2026).

What's Working

Richemont's Jewellery Maisons are the standout performer in global luxury right now — seven consecutive quarters of double-digit growth, with Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels gaining share while fashion-led rivals struggled through the 2023-2025 slowdown. The balance sheet is exceptionally strong: a genuine net cash position (€9.1B) rather than net debt, ROIC of 18.14% comfortably clearing estimated cost of capital, FCF conversion above 80%, and capex held consistently under 5% of revenue. The current valuation sits almost exactly at the company's own five-year average P/E once the one-off FY2023 distortion is excluded — not a bargain, but not stretched either. Q1 FY2027 showed the Watchmakers division, the recent laggard, returning to growth (+8%) alongside continued jewelry strength.

What to Watch

Growth on this framework's terms is genuinely modest: revenue, EBIT and FCF CAGRs all sit well under the 15% bar over FY22-26, and operating margin has compressed nearly 5 points from its FY2023 peak on gold-price and currency headwinds that are largely outside management's control. The Specialist Watchmakers and "Other" (fashion) divisions have both shrunk in absolute revenue since FY2024, leaving the group increasingly dependent on jewelry for growth. Share count has crept up modestly (+3.75% over 4 years) rather than declined. Governance is worth understanding clearly: the Rupert family's 50.60% voting control rests on just a 10.18% economic stake via the unlisted B-share class — a legally robust and long-standing structure, but one that means minority A-shareholders have no realistic path to influencing control decisions regardless of how many A shares they hold.
