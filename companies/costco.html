Costco is the standing counterexample to Walmart's thin-merchandise-margin retail model: instead of earning its profit from marked-up goods, Costco prices merchandise near cost specifically to keep its 148.5 million cardholders renewing, and makes the bulk of its real profit from a separate, near-100%-margin membership-fee line. In FY2025, membership fees were just $5.32B — 1.9% of Costco's $275.24B in total revenue — yet funded roughly 51% of the company's $10.38B in operating income. That's the single most important number on this page, and it's why criterion #3 (operating margin, 3.77% vs a 20% bar) looks just as damning as Walmart's 4.18% despite the two companies running genuinely different economic models underneath a similar "thin-margin retailer" headline. Growth is real but unspectacular: revenue grew 8.17% in FY2025 (accelerating from 5.02% in FY2024), and the most recently reported quarter (Q3 FY2026, ended May 10, 2026) came in at $70.53B, up 11.58% year-over-year, with membership fee revenue itself up 10.73% in the same quarter — both comfortably ahead of the FY2025 pace. Where Costco separates itself sharply from Walmart is capital efficiency and the balance sheet: ROIC of 38.44% (current basis) comfortably clears both the assumed 7.5% WACC and this framework's 15% absolute bar, Costco has carried a net cash position in every one of the last five fiscal years, and capex runs just 2.0% of revenue — genuinely asset-light for a company that's opening 14 new warehouses across the US and Canada this fall alone. The stock has never been cheap, though: a trailing P/E of 45.40x sits essentially in line with (marginally above) Costco's own rich FY21-25 average of roughly 44.75x, and the PEG ratio of 3.64 is well above the traditional 1.0 fair-value line — the market has paid a structural premium for Costco's stability and renewal predictability for years, and today's price doesn't offer a discount versus that history. Two criteria round out the honest picture on the weaker side: insider/founder ownership has faded to just 0.17% of shares outstanding (co-founder Jim Sinegal, who retired as CEO in 2012, no longer holds a meaningful personal stake), and the 5-year revenue/EBIT/FCF CAGRs (+8.9%/+11.2%/+9.9%) all miss this framework's 15% growth bars, consistent with a mature, disciplined compounder rather than a high-growth story. Net: 7 of 14 criteria met, Martinero Index 64/100 — a fundamentally different, and by several measures stronger, business than Walmart underneath a superficially similar "thin retail margin" headline, priced accordingly.

Trailing P/E Forward P/E
Reading the three P/E numbers: current P/E is today's share price divided by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings — a backward-looking snapshot at today's price. Trailing P/E (the FY21-25 series above) is that same backward-looking ratio calculated at each fiscal year-end, showing how the market has re-rated the stock over time relative to what it had already earned. Forward P/E is today's share price divided by analysts' consensus estimate of the next 12 months of earnings — forward-looking, and the more useful of the two for judging whether a stock is priced fairly relative to where its earnings are actually headed. For Costco specifically: trailing P/E (45.40x) and forward P/E (41.33x) both sit close to the stock's own FY21-25 average (~44.75x trailing), showing a valuation that has stayed persistently rich rather than re-rating meaningfully in either direction. One more lens worth a glance: the PEG ratio, forward P/E divided by expected annual EPS growth, which sanity-checks whether a given multiple is cheap or expensive once growth is accounted for. Costco's PEG currently runs around 3.64 — well above the traditional 1.0 "fair value" line, and notably higher even than Walmart's own elevated PEG (>3x) — underscoring that at today's price, Costco is valued more like a premium, highly predictable compounder than a name offering a growth-adjusted bargain.
Balance sheet: Costco has carried a net cash position in every one of the last five fiscal years (FY2021: $768M, FY2022: $143M, FY2023: $4.70B, FY2024: $1.20B, FY2025: $5.35B net cash), automatically clearing this framework's <2x Net Debt/EBITDA bar — there is no net debt to measure. ROIC (current/TTM basis) sits at 38.44%, comfortably above both the assumed 7.5% WACC and this framework's 15% absolute bar; the unusually low invested-capital base (Costco's book equity is modest relative to its earnings power, a byproduct of decades of buybacks and a capital-light warehouse model) is the main driver of that high figure, not a data error. Capex ran just $5.50B in FY2025 — 2.0% of revenue, genuinely asset-light despite Costco physically opening 14 new warehouses across the US and Canada this fall alone.

What's Working

A genuinely different economic model from Walmart hiding behind a similarly thin operating margin: membership fees (just 1.9% of revenue) fund roughly 51% of operating income, ROIC of 38.44% comfortably clears both WACC and the 15% bar, the balance sheet has carried net cash for five straight years, capex is a lean 2.0% of revenue despite continued warehouse expansion, gross margin has expanded (not compressed) over the window, and share count has stayed essentially flat. Revenue and membership-fee growth are both accelerating into FY2026 (+11.58% and +10.73% respectively in the latest reported quarter).

What to Watch

None of the three growth CAGRs (revenue +8.9%, EBIT +11.2%, FCF +9.9%) clear this framework's 15% bars — Costco is a mature, disciplined compounder, not a growth story. Insider/founder ownership has faded to a negligible 0.17% as Jim Sinegal's stake wound down over the past decade. And the valuation has simply never been cheap: at 45.40x trailing earnings and a PEG near 3.64x, today's price sits right at — not below — Costco's own rich five-year average, so there's little margin of safety in the entry multiple even though the underlying business quality is high.
