DHL Group (listed parent DHL AG, formerly Deutsche Post AG) describes itself as the world's leading logistics company, operating in over 220 countries and territories with about 584,000 people. It has five divisions: Express (time-definite international parcels and documents, the profit engine at €3.2B of FY2025 EBIT), Global Forwarding, Freight (air, ocean and road forwarding, €0.8B), Supply Chain (contract logistics and warehousing, €1.2B), eCommerce (€0.4B) and Post & Parcel Germany (the domestic mail and parcel business, €1.0B). Unlike DSV, DHL is not asset-light: it owns an air fleet and hubs, runs a large last-mile network with its own drivers and sorters, so capex runs at about 3.6% of revenue rather than 1%. In return it owns the integrated express network that competitors cannot replicate, and that network is where the earnings sit. The last four years were a round trip: COVID pushed EBIT to a record €8.4B in FY2022, normalisation took it down to €5.9B in FY2024, and the “Fit for Growth” cost programme has since pulled it back to €6.1B on lower revenue. Q2 2026 was the strongest quarter in this recovery: revenue €22.4B (+12.8% YoY) and EBIT €1.86B (+30.0%, margin 8.3%), with Express EBIT up 64% on higher shipment weight and tight air-freight capacity. FY2026 EBIT guidance was raised in July to more than €6.5B, free cash flow guidance is about €3.0B, and the buyback programme was lifted to up to €6.5B and extended to the end of 2027. The shares have rallied about 51% in a year, so a company that looked cheap at ~11.8x earnings at end-2024 now trades at ~17.3x, above its own five-year average.
Three distortions to keep in mind: (1) COVID boom — FY2021-22 revenue and EBIT were inflated by extraordinary freight rates, e-commerce volumes and a record Forwarding year (FY2022 Forwarding revenue €30.2B vs €18.6B in FY2025), so every CAGR measured from FY2021 is negative or flat even though FY2025 EBIT is well above the pre-pandemic level. (2) Currency and tariffs — FY2025 revenue (-1.6%) was hit by FX and by lower volumes into the US after the end of the de-minimis exemption; Q2 2026 is compared with a prior-year quarter already depressed by tariffs, and part of its +12.8% is fuel pass-through and a temporary air-capacity squeeze (~€150m EBIT benefit at Express), so it is not a clean run-rate. (3) Reported vs data-vendor figures — S&P/stockanalysis show FY2025 revenue of €83.4B and EBIT of €5.4B because they standardise definitions; this page uses DHL's own reported figures throughout.
Compared with DSV, DFDS and Maersk on this site: DSV scores 48 (7/14), DFDS 47 and Maersk 44; DHL scores 38 (4/14). DHL beats DSV on the things that matter most right now — ROIC above WACC (9.2% vs 7.2% vs 7.9% for DSV), a shrinking share count where DSV's is flat after an equity issue, and a more stable margin. DSV beats DHL on asset-light capital intensity (~1% vs ~3.6% of revenue), FCF conversion (83% vs ~52%), FCF growth and a cheaper entry multiple versus its own history (0.95x vs 1.38x). DHL's score is held back mainly by the flat-to-negative FY2021-25 growth CAGRs, which weigh 32 of the 105 index points.

Strategy 2030 & Latest Quarter (Q2 2026)
The case for the next 18 months is whether Express and Forwarding can hold the margin gains once the tariff-distorted comparisons and the air-capacity squeeze fade. Reported on Aug 5, 2026; Q3 2026 is due Nov 5, 2026.
What the quarter actually showed

Q2 2026 revenue was €22,367m (+12.8% YoY; DHL rounds to +13%) and EBIT €1,858m (+30.0%), with the EBIT margin up 1.1pp to 8.3%. Growth came from higher shipment weight at Express, constrained international air-freight capacity and the pass-through of higher fuel costs, against a prior-year quarter hit by tariffs. Express revenue rose 21.5% and EBIT 64.3% to €1,200m (margin 16.8%), including about €150m of temporary benefit from the air-capacity squeeze. Global Forwarding EBIT grew 21.9% to €240m on revenue +17.9%. Supply Chain revenue grew 12.9% but EBIT fell 12.1% against a prior quarter flattered by one-offs. eCommerce revenue fell 3.7% (the Evri merger removed UK revenue from consolidation) and Post & Parcel Germany EBIT fell 18.7% on higher transport and personnel costs. Free cash flow excluding M&A was €569m (Q2 2025: €329m) despite a working-capital outflow, helped in June by refunds of US IEEPA tariffs that DHL is passing on to customers; H1 FCF was €1.8B vs €1.1B. Net profit after minorities rose 23.9% to €1.01B.

Share Count & Buybacks

Issued share capital is 1,150M shares; after treasury shares about 1.107B are outstanding, down roughly 10% from ~1.24B at end-2021 and down 3.9% in the last year alone (S&P). The buyback programme, launched in 2022, is now sized at up to €6.5B through the end of 2027 (€0.9B bought in 2023, €1.4B in 2025). Dividend per share was €1.80 (FY2021), 1.85 (FY2022-24) and 1.90 (FY2025), a 60.6% payout of FY2025 net profit, slightly above DHL's 40-60% target range.

Founders, insiders & honest caveats: DHL has no founder or controlling family; it was created from the privatisation of the German state postal service, and the CEO (Tobias Meyer, since 2022), CFO (Melanie Kreis) and the board are professional managers. The largest holder, KfW (17.73%), is a state-owned bank holding the legacy federal stake, a strategic anchor but not an insider or founder block, so S&P shows no insider percentage. Directors' dealings in September 2026 were small share-plan purchases by executives. I could not verify the combined management and board holding, so I make no claim about it.
Leverage: total debt including lease liabilities is €28.0B against €4.3B cash (Jun 2026, S&P Global), a net debt of ~€23.7B or 2.8x TTM EBITDA of €8.4B (gross debt/EBITDA 2.57x); interest cover is ~4.9x and the current ratio 1.03. Net debt has risen every year since FY2021 (€13.3B on the same S&P basis) as buybacks and dividends (~€3.5B a year combined) have run slightly ahead of FCF after M&A. DHL's own definition of net debt differs (it treats pension and some lease items differently) and I could not retrieve its latest figure, so the scorecard uses the S&P number consistently with the DSV page.

What's Working

The earnings engine has turned. EBIT recovered from €5.9B (FY2024) to €6.1B (FY2025) on lower revenue, H1 2026 EBIT is up 19%, Q2 2026 delivered a record margin for this recovery (8.3%) and FY2026 guidance was raised to more than €6.5B. Express is the standout, with a 16.8% margin in Q2 and a network competitors cannot replicate, and cost programmes have made earnings less dependent on freight rates. Cash generation is steady (FCF about €3B a year, 52% of EBIT in every year of the window), ROIC clears WACC on both company and S&P definitions, and shareholders are paid through a growing dividend (€1.90) plus a buyback now sized at up to €6.5B through 2027 that has cut the share count about 10% since 2021. Gross margin has been stable and the business is far more diversified than a pure forwarder: contract logistics, German parcels and eCommerce give ballast when freight rates swing.

What to Watch

Growth over the window is nil: revenue CAGR +0.3% and EBIT CAGR -6.5% from the FY2021 base, with FY2025 revenue down 1.6%. Part of the Q2 2026 surge is temporary (a ~€150m air-capacity benefit, fuel pass-through and a weak tariff-hit comparison), Supply Chain and German mail earnings fell in the quarter, and the stock has re-rated from ~11.8x to ~17.3x trailing earnings, above its own average, with the average analyst target below the price. Net debt including leases is ~2.8x EBITDA and has risen every year, H1 capex is up 25%, and conversion of EBIT to FCF is only about 52% because the model is capital- and lease-heavy. Exposure to global trade policy (US tariffs, the end of de-minimis), geopolitics and fuel is large, and Forwarding margins (4.1% in FY2025) show how thin the freight business is. There is no founder or insider block (KfW is a state-owned anchor).
