AB Volvo, founded in 1927 and headquartered in Gothenburg, is the parent of the Volvo Group: heavy-duty and medium-duty trucks (Volvo, Renault Trucks, Mack, plus the Eicher and Dongfeng joint ventures), Volvo Construction Equipment, Volvo Buses, Volvo Penta marine and industrial engines, and Volvo Financial Services, the captive customer-finance arm. It is unrelated to Volvo Cars, which Volvo sold to Ford in 1999 and is now a separate, Geely-controlled listed company. Trucks were ≈68% of FY2025 net sales (SEK 323.5bn) and Volvo CE about 17% (SEK 81.6bn), so this is first a truck stock and only secondarily a construction-equipment competitor to Caterpillar. Services (parts, repair, contracts) are the quality lever: SEK 124.0bn in FY2025, 26% of sales, growing about 13% a year since 2021. The five-year record is a full cycle: net sales rose from SEK 372.2bn (FY2021) to a SEK 552.8bn peak in FY2023, then fell 4.7% and 9.0% as North American freight stayed in recession, EU registrations slipped and the krona strengthened; adjusted operating income went from SEK 41.0bn to SEK 77.6bn and back to SEK 51.2bn, and Industrial-Operations operating cash flow halved to SEK 21.8bn in FY2025 on higher capex (Mexico plant, product launches) and working capital. What has changed in 2026 is the order book: truck net orders were up 33% in Q2 2026 (North America more than doubled) and management raised its European and Chinese market forecasts, while adjusted margin reached 11.7%. On the scorecard this is a mixed picture, with real strengths (net cash in the industrial business, an Industrivärden-anchored share register, 25%-plus ROCE, a flat share count) and real misses (mid-single-digit growth once you stand in the middle of a cycle, cash conversion of about 62% of EBIT, and a trailing P/E of 18.2x against a five-year average near 12.7x).
SEK note — read every krona figure with this in mind: all amounts on this dashboard are in Swedish kronor (SEK) as reported by Volvo Group, which sells mostly outside Sweden (Europe, North America, South America, Asia). Reported SEK sales therefore swing with currency: in FY2025 truck net sales fell 10% in SEK but only 5% adjusted for currency (Volvo's own figures), and currency cost the truck division SEK 3.5bn of operating income. I have not converted to USD or EUR because no verified average rates were pulled; treat cross-company comparisons against dollar reporters, including Caterpillar, with that caveat. Q2 2026 headlines

Net sales were SEK 126.3bn, up +2.8% YoY as reported (SEK 122.9bn a year earlier) and +7% organic, because currency and the divestment of the SDLG construction brand (September 2025) offset the underlying growth. Adjusted operating income rose to SEK 14.8bn (13.5bn) at an 11.7% margin (11.0%), and EPS jumped 40% to SEK 5.10 (3.64), helped by SEK 2.2bn smaller one-off charges than a year ago (excluded from the adjusted figure). Industrial Operations cash flow doubled to SEK 5.8bn (2.9bn). Truck net orders surged 33% to 63,412 vehicles, with North American orders up 122%, orders running well ahead of the 55,687 deliveries (about 1.14x) that should feed second-half deliveries. Volvo CE grew 13% organically at a 14.4% adjusted margin, while Volvo Penta's margin slipped to 16.7% (20.7%).

Gross Margin Trend — Volvo Group (FY2021–FY2025)
Gross margin jumped to 27.0–27.3% in FY2023–24 on pricing and mix, then gave back 2.9pp in FY2025 to 24.4% on lower volume, tariffs and material costs. Versus FY2022's 23.6% (the 3-year window for criterion #14) it is still +0.8pp higher.
Market position, honestly stated: Volvo's own reporting gives share by region rather than a single global number. Through May 2026 its European heavy-duty share was 19.6% for Volvo and 9.3% for Renault Trucks (28.9% combined), North America 8.6% for Volvo and 8.4% for Mack (17.0% combined) and Brazil 23.2%. Trucks delivered from wholly-owned operations were 202,911 in FY2025; adding the non-consolidated Indian and Chinese joint ventures brings it to roughly 407,000 (75,556 Eicher plus 127,632 Dongfeng). An industry share summary for 2025 names Daimler Truck as the leader (over 12% of the global heavy-duty market) and PACCAR, Sinotruk, Traton and Volvo as the other top-five players (32% combined for the five); I could not verify a clean primary-source global ranking, so I treat Volvo as a global top-5 player and, by my own estimate, roughly the #3 Western group, not a clear global top-2 (criterion #5 is therefore Not met, with a score of 2/5). In construction equipment, Volvo CE ranked #7 worldwide in the 2025 Yellow Table with USD 10.5bn of sales (about 4% of a USD 246.6bn market), far behind Caterpillar (15.2%) and Komatsu (11%). Customer concentration is not disclosed as a risk (fleets and dealers); this was not independently verified.
One-offs and events to know: in May 2026 Volvo Group North America settled with the California Air Resources Board over emissions-control documentation on 2010–2016 engines (USD 196.5M of cost, SEK 1.8bn, excluded from adjusted operating income, with a SEK 0.8bn cash outflow in Q2 and the rest over five years, without admission of liability). Volvo CE announced the closure of Rokbak, completed the Swecon acquisition and sold SDLG (September 2025), which is why CE order intake looks halved. A strategic change to the Flexis van business with Renault and CMA-CGM closed in June. At the June 10 Capital Markets Day management set out targets for earnings resilience and growth (doubling Volvo Penta revenue, service growth, autonomous trucks; the group's stated 25% North American truck share ambition comes from its 2025 annual report); I did not verify numeric financial targets from that event beyond the group's standing goal of a reported operating margin above 10% through the cycle.

Trailing & Forward P/E — FY21-25 & Current
Current trailing P/E of 18.23x (SEK 321.20 on TTM EPS of SEK 17.62) is 43% above the FY21-25 year-end average of 12.74x; forward P/E of roughly 13.0x compares with a 12.3x average, only about 6% above. Most of the premium is trailing earnings that are still below the FY2023-24 peak, not a runaway price.
Trailing P/EForward P/E
Reading the three P/E numbers: the trailing P/E is today's price divided by the last twelve months of reported EPS (SEK 17.62); the year-end series is Volvo's own published figure at each December 31 (13.0x, 11.7x, 10.7x, 10.8x, 17.5x). The forward P/E divides the price by analysts' expected next-twelve-month EPS; S&P showed 13.43x at SEK 333.00 on Sep 18 and I scaled that to today's lower price (about 12.95x), so treat it as approximate, and the historical forward figures are also S&P's. The PEG ratio is about 1.3x. For a cyclical, a high trailing multiple on trough-ish earnings is normal (Volvo traded at 17.5x at the end of 2025 when earnings had fallen 32%), so I score on trailing P/E for consistency with the rest of the site but weigh the forward number in my own view.
Balance sheet & approach to leverage: Volvo's own target is that Industrial Operations carries no net financial debt excluding pensions and leases. At year-end 2025 it held a net financial asset position of SEK 63.0bn (2024: SEK 85.9bn; 2021: SEK 66.2bn), roughly 1.0x Industrial Operations EBITDA of SEK 63.0bn, and still SEK 48.4bn net cash after counting pensions and leases; after the SEK 26.4bn April dividend it stood at SEK 34.7bn (SEK 19.9bn including pensions and leases) at June 30, 2026. I use this industrial basis because the Financial Services arm (Volvo Financial Services) borrows to fund customer loans and leases that are matched by receivables; on a consolidated basis, including all of that funding, S&P computes net debt/EBITDA of about 3.8x, and the group's equity ratio is 26.0% (35.4% in Industrial Operations). Credit ratings are A (S&P) and A2 (Moody's), stable, with SEK 71.2bn of unused credit facilities. The tension: the industrial cash pile is large but it is also what funds SEK 26bn-plus yearly dividends, so it can shrink in a weak year, as it just did.

Share Count Trend

Shares outstanding have been flat at 2,033 million since 2021 (444m A + 1,589m B in 2025; 445m + 1,588m in 2021), with no net buyback or dilution. Returns to shareholders come through the dividend instead: SEK 37.6bn was paid in 2025 (SEK 18.50 per share) and SEK 26.4bn in April 2026 (SEK 13.00).

Governance & ownership note: Volvo has two share classes: each A share carries ten times the votes of a B share (A shares are 22% of capital but the bulk of votes), which is how Industrivärden, the Swedish investment holding company, controls about 28% of the votes with under 10% of the capital and is represented on the election committee as a long-term anchor. Geely Holding (the Chinese parent of Volvo Cars) sold all its B shares in April 2024 for about SEK 14bn but kept 88.5 million A shares, leaving it the second-largest voter at about 14.7%. Together the top two hold roughly 43% of the votes. Neither is a founder or a management insider, and narrowly defined insider ownership by the board and management is small (S&P shows about 4.5% insiders, source-definition unclear); I score the criterion on the anchor holders' voting weight and flag it as a judgment. The vote percentages come from secondary sources quoting Volvo's end-2025 shareholder data and Bloomberg coverage of Geely's sale; Volvo's own largest-shareholder table sits behind a cookie wall and was not read directly.

What's Working

A net-cash industrial business (SEK 63.0bn at FY2025) with 25%-plus return on capital employed, an A-rated balance sheet and a group that has stayed above its 10% through-the-cycle margin target on average (11.8% adjusted FY21-25). Services are a growing, steadier profit pool (26% of sales, ~13% annual growth since 2021), the share count has not moved in five years, and Industrivärden's 28% vote anchor gives long-term, stable ownership. The latest quarter is the strongest sign yet of a turn: truck orders +33% (North America +122%), adjusted margin 11.7%, EPS +40%, market forecasts for Europe and China raised, and the CARB and US-tariff overhangs largely quantified and booked.

What to Watch

Growth is low and cyclical (net sales -9.0% in FY2025 after -4.7% in FY2024, +6.5% a year over five years), operating cash flow halved in FY2025 and converts only about 62% of EBIT over the cycle, and the trailing P/E of 18.2x is 43% above its own five-year average. North American retail truck sales are still 18% below last year and the 265,000-unit forecast needs a strong second half; the 2027 emissions rules, tariffs and a stronger krona all remain swing factors. The dividend is variable by design (the extra payment fell from SEK 10.50 to SEK 4.50), capex is stepping up, and Volvo CE, the direct Caterpillar rival, is only a #7 player with less than a third of Caterpillar's sales.

Maarten's note

I put Volvo next to Caterpillar because the two overlap in construction equipment, but the comparison is really trucks versus machines: about 68% of Volvo's sales are trucks, and Volvo CE is only around 17%. That makes Volvo a bet on freight and replacement cycles in Europe and North America, where Caterpillar is a bet on mining, infrastructure and a big services and aftermarket business. As far as I can tell Caterpillar is the higher-margin, higher-returning franchise and the global #1, whereas Volvo CE is a solid but much smaller number seven.

What I like about Volvo is very Nordic: a net-cash industrial core, an Industrivärden-anchored register and a dividend that pays out the surplus in good years, SEK 18.50 in 2025 and SEK 13.00 this year, about a 4% yield today. The flip side is that the extra dividend is variable by design, cash conversion is only around 62% of EBIT and the shares are not cheap against their own history on trailing earnings. I would rather watch whether the order surge shows up in North American deliveries and margins than treat the yield as a floor. Not a recommendation, just how I think about it.
