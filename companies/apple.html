Apple is an extraordinary business by almost any operating measure — a 32% operating margin, a ROIC-WACC spread few companies can match, a genuinely net-cash balance sheet even after hundreds of billions in buybacks, and a share count that keeps shrinking. What this framework catches, though, is that Apple today is a mature, wonderful-quality compounder rather than a growth story: revenue has compounded at just 3.3% a year since FY2021, and free cash flow hasn't grown at all over that span despite record profits, largely because operating cash flow growth has been mostly offset by tax and working-capital effects. None of that makes Apple a bad business — it's a case of this framework's heavy growth weighting doing exactly what it's designed to do.

Trailing P/E Forward P/E
Reading trailing vs. forward P/E: current P/E is today's share price divided by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings. The trailing-P/E series above is that same backward-looking ratio calculated at each year-end, showing how the market has re-rated Apple relative to what it had already earned. Forward P/E divides today's price by analysts' consensus estimate of the next 12 months of earnings — forward-looking, and it captures expected growth (or decline) that trailing P/E can't see. For Apple, forward P/E (≈33.8x) tracks close to trailing (≈35.5x), only a modest discount — consistent with a mature, slower-growing business where the market isn't pricing in much of an earnings inflection either way. That same maturity shows up starkly in the PEG ratio: dividing forward P/E by consensus EPS growth (low-double-digits) puts Apple's PEG north of 3 — well above the traditional 1.0 "fair value" line, meaning Apple's multiple isn't being justified by growth at all, but by the market's confidence in the ecosystem, buybacks, and earnings durability instead.
Balance sheet: Apple held a net cash position of roughly $31B as of Q3 2025 (cash and marketable securities less total debt) — comfortably net cash even after issuing debt specifically to fund part of its buyback program, a deliberate capital-structure choice rather than a sign of financial strain.

What's Working

Apple runs one of the most efficient, capital-disciplined businesses of any large company — a 32% operating margin, a ROIC-WACC spread most companies can't approach, and a genuinely net-cash balance sheet despite hundreds of billions returned to shareholders over the years. Q3 FY2026 was a real acceleration (+16% revenue, +27% net income), the December FY2026 holiday quarter set an all-time record, and Services keeps compounding as a high-margin, recurring layer on top of the hardware base. The premium smartphone tier has become a genuine Apple-Samsung duopoly, with Apple alone capturing roughly half of industry revenue.

What to Watch

The core scorecard miss here is growth: revenue has compounded at just 3.3% a year since FY2021, and free cash flow hasn't grown at all over that span even as profits hit records — a real sign of a maturing, not expanding, business. There's no founder or meaningfully-sized insider stake, the current multiple sits modestly above its own five-year average, and FCF conversion has dipped just under the 80% bar. Tariff policy is also a live, non-trivial swing factor given Apple's reliance on Chinese and broader Asian manufacturing — Q3 FY2026 results already included a real tariff-refund tailwind that may not repeat.
