Toyota is the world's largest automaker by volume, and the site's framework mostly sees the strengths of a very large, conservatively run industrial company: the industrial (non-financial services) businesses hold ¥7.1T of net cash, free cash flow compounded at roughly 24% a year from ¥1.0T (FY2021) to ¥3.0T (FY2026), and the share count is down about 7% over the period. But growth and margins are those of a mature car maker, not a compounder: industrial revenue grew about 12.9% a year and operating income about 11.5% a year over FY2021–FY2026, and the margin peaked at 11.5% in FY2024 before falling to 6.3% in FY2026 as tariffs (press coverage cites about ¥1.4T of impact), a stronger cost base and weak North American profit (a ¥193B segment loss in FY2026) took hold. Management's own FY2027 forecast is for operating income of about ¥3.0T, down roughly 20% again. Net income looks healthier than operations because it includes equity-method income, investment gains and currency effects — the latest trailing P/E (≈7.8x) is flattered by that. Quarterly Trend — Tariffs Hit Operations, Non-Operating Items Lift Net Income
Eight quarters on Toyota's own fiscal calendar (Q1 FY2027 = Apr–Jun 2026, reported Aug 2026). Consolidated figures; quarterly industrial-only data is not tabulated here.
What actually moved the numbers

Consolidated revenue has grown in every one of the last eight quarters against the year-earlier quarter, reaching ¥13.53T in Q1 FY2027, +10.4% YoY, a record. Operating income, however, did not follow: Q1 FY2027 was ¥1.06T, about 8.8% below the ¥1.17T of the year-earlier quarter, and the March 2026 quarter fell to just ¥0.57T (4.5% margin) as tariff costs and a North American operating loss landed. FY2026 as a whole saw operating income fall 21.5% to ¥3.77T on revenue up 5.5%, with press coverage of the results attributing about ¥1.4T to tariffs. Net income tells a different story: Q1 FY2027 net income was ¥1.48T (+75.6% YoY) because of other non-operating income, foreign-exchange and investment items rather than car-making profit — the gap between ¥1.06T operating income and ¥1.96T pre-tax income is the tell. In the same quarter Toyota also spent about ¥3.66T repurchasing its own shares (per its cash-flow statement), cutting the share count roughly 6%.

Reading the three P/E numbers: current P/E is today's price divided by the last 12 months of reported earnings; the trailing series is that same ratio at each fiscal year-end. Forward P/E divides today's price by analysts' estimate of the next 12 months of earnings. For Toyota the two diverge: trailing earnings (TTM EPS ≈¥349) include a large amount of equity-method income, investment gains and foreign-exchange effects, while the company itself guides FY2027 net income to about ¥3.0T (roughly ¥250–260 per share on the post-buyback share count), so the forward multiple of ≈13.46x is the cleaner read of what the market pays for guided earnings. A PEG ratio is not meaningful here: consensus EPS growth is not available from S&P (listed n/a) and Toyota's earnings are guided to decline in FY2027. What's Working

Toyota is the largest automaker by volume and its industrial businesses sit on ¥7.1T of net cash, so a downturn is funded from the balance sheet rather than from the market. Industrial free cash flow has roughly tripled since FY2021 and converted at about 100% of operating income in FY2026. Gross margin has been broadly stable over three years (−0.3pp) even through a tariff shock, the dividend has risen every year (¥44 → ¥95, with ¥100 guided), and the share count has fallen, most recently through a large Apr–Jun 2026 repurchase. Q1 FY2027 revenue grew 10.4% YoY, and Toyota's hybrid-heavy lineup has been selling strongly in the US.

What to Watch

Growth and margins are those of a mature car maker: industrial revenue CAGR of about 12.9% and EBIT CAGR of about 11.5% miss the 15% bars, and the operating margin has fallen from 11.5% to 6.3% in two years, with management guiding operating income down roughly another 20% in FY2027. North America swung to an operating loss in FY2026 on tariffs. The trailing P/E looks cheap partly because net income is boosted by non-operating items, forward P/E is higher, and ROIC (≈8% on my estimate, 4% on S&P's) is thin. The industry is fragmented with overcapacity and an EV/software transition that Toyota is approaching more slowly than some rivals (a risk and a deliberate strategy), insiders hold almost nothing, and the consolidated balance sheet carries about ¥30T of net debt inside the captive finance arm. Yen strength, and further tariff or trade changes, are the key swing factors.
