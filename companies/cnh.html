CNH Industrial is the maker of Case IH and New Holland agricultural equipment (tractors, combines, balers, precision-ag technology) and Case and New Holland Construction equipment (excavators, loaders, backhoes), plus CNH Capital, a captive finance arm that funds dealer inventory and customer purchases. It was spun out of the old CNH Industrial conglomerate when Iveco was demerged effective January 1, 2022, and is now a pure-play ag and construction company, redomiciled with a primary NYSE listing and a secondary listing in Milan. Exor, the Agnelli family holding company, remains the anchor shareholder. The business is highly cyclical: FY2022-23 were record-ish years on post-COVID farm profitability and pricing; since then, low crop prices, high input costs, high interest rates and tariff uncertainty have pushed North American large-tractor and combine demand sharply lower, and the company has deliberately under-produced against retail demand to bleed dealer inventory. Industrial net sales fell from $22.1B (FY2023) to $15.3B (FY2025), and adjusted EBIT of Industrial Activities from $2.73B to $663M — a 76% collapse. Management calls 2026 the industry trough and expects a recovery to start in 2027; Q2 2026 already showed consolidated revenue turning modestly positive (+2%), but margins are still compressing (Industrial adj. EBIT margin 4.0% in Q2 vs 5.6% a year earlier). For a fixed-checklist investor this is a low-scoring name today, and it is worth being honest that most of that low score is cyclical, not structural — the real question is what a mid-cycle margin looks like (management targets substantially higher margins by 2030) and whether CNH can get there against Deere's technology and dealer lead.
Trailing P/E is very high because earnings are near a trough, not because the stock is expensive on mid-cycle earnings: S&P Global shows a trailing P/E of ~52x (GAAP EPS $0.25 after Raven and Monarch impairments) and a forward P/E of ~24x, against a five-year average of ~13x. Both look rich versus history, but both divide today's price by depressed earnings — so the scorecard's valuation criterion fails mechanically. Use it as a cycle marker, not a verdict.
Maarten's note: I'm covering CNH as the competitor to today's Caterpillar pick, not as a candidate in its own right. Next to Caterpillar it looks like the weaker business: Caterpillar is broader (construction, mining, power and energy), has a much bigger services and aftermarket base, and doesn't lean on one crop-price-driven end market. Next to Deere, the ag leader, CNH has the cheaper-looking stock and the more dramatic recovery story, but Deere has the technology, precision-ag and dealer edge that I think justifies its premium. If I wanted ag-cycle exposure I would study Deere first; CNH is the one I would only look at as a deliberate trough-recovery bet, and I would want to see the 2027 recovery show up in orders and margins before believing it. These are my own notes, not a recommendation.

Recent Performance, Guidance & the Ag Downcycle
Sales have stabilized, but margins are still falling. Management frames 2026 as the trough and points to a 2027 industry recovery.
Where we are in the ag cycle

CNH forecasts global industry retail demand for agricultural equipment down roughly another 5% in 2026, to "historic trough levels", after several years of decline, driven by low commodity prices, high input costs, high rates and an uncertain trade environment. Q2 2026 industry data illustrate it: North American tractors over 140 HP were down 17% and under 140 HP down 16% year over year, combines down 7%; EMEA tractors down 11%; South American combines down 29%. Asia Pacific tractors were the bright spot (+15%). Construction is the opposite: industry heavy-equipment volume was up 17% in Q2 and light up 6%. CNH says it has been deliberately producing below retail demand to clear dealer inventory, which depresses factory absorption and margins now but should position it to benefit quickly when retail demand turns. Management expects ag demand to resume growth in 2027 — that is the company's view, not something I can verify.

How to read the multiple: the trailing P/E history (15.3x, 10.6x, 6.9x, 11.4x, 22.5x) averages ~13.3x; the forward P/E history averages ~13.1x. The 1.8x ratio on forward earnings is the fairer test, and it still fails. If FY2027 earnings recover materially, today's forward multiple would compress quickly — that is the whole bull case — but the checklist scores what is on the page, not what could be. The PEG ratio S&P shows (~0.95) leans on an assumed ~22% three-year EPS growth rate off a very low base and is not something I put weight on.

Ownership note: Exor holds a minority economic stake but 45.6% of voting power through CNH's loyalty-voting structure (special voting shares for long-term holders), making it the effective controlling shareholder. That is meaningful alignment for outside holders — and a governance concentration to be aware of. The ~27% economic figure comes from an April 2025 disclosure and may have drifted with buybacks and share-count changes; I did not find a newer number.

What's Working

A top-three global ag-equipment franchise with two strong brands (Case IH, New Holland), a broad dealer network and a captive finance arm that supports sales through the cycle. Industrial leverage is manageable (~1.8x at year-end) despite the earnings trough, capex is moderate (~3.5% of sales), and the share count has fallen about 10% since end-2023 on buybacks. Exor's controlling voting stake gives long-term alignment. Sales have stopped falling (Q2 2026 consolidated +2%, Construction +12%), 2026 guidance was raised in August, and management expects to land at the higher end of its ranges. If the ag cycle turns in 2027 as management expects, earnings leverage from a $15B revenue base at 4% margins is large, and the multiple would compress fast.

What to Watch

Every growth measure is negative over five years and margins are still compressing (Industrial adj. EBIT margin 4.0% in Q2 2026 vs 5.6% a year earlier, Agriculture 5.2% vs 8.1%). Returns on capital are below any reasonable cost of capital, and the stock trades at ~24x forward and ~52x trailing earnings against a ~13x history, so the recovery is already partly priced in. Deere's technology and dealer lead, tariff costs, South American weakness, and the risk that the 2027 recovery slips are the main issues; the company also booked impairments (Raven, Monarch Tractor) in 2025. Industrial leverage rose to ~2.7x on TTM earnings by June 2026, the dividend was cut to $0.10, and the sizable Financial Services book ($20.8B net debt) adds credit-cycle exposure in a farm downturn. Market-share, WACC and Exor-stake figures here are third-party or dated and flagged as estimates.
