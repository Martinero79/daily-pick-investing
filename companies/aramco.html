Saudi Aramco is, by almost any measure, one of the most profitable companies on earth: a 44% operating margin, a 20.8% return on invested capital against an estimated ≈8.3% cost of capital, and a balance sheet so lightly levered it barely registers net debt against roughly $220B of EBITDA. Ownership is concentrated too, just not in the founder-led sense this framework usually flags — the Saudi government and its Public Investment Fund together hold roughly 90% of shares outstanding. What the scorecard doesn't reward is growth: revenue, operating income and free cash flow have all been roughly flat-to-declining since FY2021 as oil prices normalized down from their 2022 peak, and FY2026's sharp profit rebound is tied to a genuine but unusual supply shock — a prolonged Strait of Hormuz disruption linked to the ongoing regional conflict pushed crude prices to roughly $108/barrel in Q2 2026, versus $67/barrel a year earlier. That's a real, disclosed driver of the recent numbers, not a fabricated one, but it's also not a trend to extrapolate forward.

A double-edged geopolitical driver: the Strait of Hormuz disruption tied to the ongoing regional conflict has boosted Aramco's near-term profits by pushing crude prices sharply higher — but the same tail risk cuts both ways. Any de-escalation or resolution could normalize oil prices back down just as quickly, and a wider or more direct disruption to Gulf shipping routes would carry serious operational risk for a company whose own export infrastructure sits in the same region. This is real, disclosed news (Reuters, GuruFocus, OilPrice.com, and others have covered it extensively through July-August 2026), not a hypothetical — but it's a reminder that Aramco's fortunes remain tightly linked to events well outside the company's control.

Balance sheet: Aramco held roughly $97.0B in total debt against $76.9B of cash & investments as of FY2025-end (Dec 31, 2025) — a net debt position of only about $20.1B against roughly $220.0B of EBITDA (net debt/EBITDA ≈0.09x). This is negligible leverage for a company of this scale, and net debt has fluctuated between small net-debt and net-cash positions across the FY21-25 window as the dividend and capex program flexed with oil prices.

Governance note: Aramco isn't founder-led in the traditional sense this framework usually rewards — instead, the Saudi government directly owns roughly 81.5% of shares, with the sovereign Public Investment Fund (PIF) holding a further ≈8%, for combined state ownership near 90%. The remaining ≈10-11% free float has grown gradually through the December 2019 IPO and a further secondary offering in June 2024. This framework's "insider/founder ownership" criterion treats this concentrated, stable ownership base as a Met, though a sovereign majority owner's incentives (dividend policy tied to government budget needs, national strategic priorities) differ meaningfully from a private founder's.

What's Working

Unmatched profitability and balance-sheet strength: a 44% operating margin, a 20.8% ROIC against an estimated 8.3% cost of capital, and net debt/EBITDA of just ≈0.09x. Aramco is the clear #1 global crude producer and one of the largest reserve holders, ownership is stable and heavily aligned behind a single controlling shareholder (the Saudi state), share count has stayed essentially flat, and the current P/E sits modestly below its own FY21-25 average despite recent earnings volatility.

What to Watch

Growth is the real structural weak point — revenue, operating income and free cash flow have all been roughly flat-to-declining since FY2021, and capex intensity (≈11.4% of revenue) is climbing as Aramco invests in gas (Jafurah) and downstream/chemicals. FY2026's strong headline results are meaningfully inflated by a real but unusual supply shock tied to a Strait of Hormuz disruption from the ongoing regional conflict — a geopolitical tail risk that cuts both ways, since any de-escalation could just as quickly normalize oil prices lower. More broadly, Aramco's fortunes remain tightly linked to global oil prices and OPEC+ production policy, and its dividend policy (payout ratio over 90% of FY2025 net income) is itself tied to Saudi government budget needs rather than pure capital-allocation discipline.
