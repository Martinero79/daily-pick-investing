Hermès International is the family-controlled French luxury house behind the Birkin and Kelly handbags, founded as a Paris harness workshop in 1837 and still run on the same principle of artisanal scarcity: rather than chasing volume, Hermès deliberately caps production of its icon bags at roughly 12,000-15,000 units each per year, against demand many multiples higher, creating genuine multi-year waitlists that few other brands can replicate. That scarcity, combined with near-total vertical integration (Hermès owns its own tanneries, secures effectively all of its high-grade calfskin and 100% of its crocodile/alligator skin supply, and manufactures roughly 55% of what it sells in-house across more than 50 French production sites), is the foundation of a 41%+ operating margin — about double LVMH's and the highest of any major listed luxury house. H1 2026 revenue reached €8.16B, up 6.1% at constant currency (accelerating to 6.7% in Q2) even as the broader luxury sector continues to work through a well-documented 2023-2026 demand slowdown; recurring operating income held at €3.35B, a 41.0% margin. The Hermès family — roughly 100 descendants of founder Thierry Hermès across the Dumas, Guerrand and Puech branches — consolidated its control in 2011 via the H51 holding company, and together with Emile Hermès SAS the family now holds roughly 66.7% of share capital and 78.6% of voting rights, the strongest, most durable founder-family control structure of any luxury name on this site. The one place the numbers get more nuanced: Hermès' capex runs meaningfully higher than a typical luxury "brand licensing" peer (historically 3.6%-7.0% of revenue, spiking in FY2024 for new leather-workshop construction) precisely because it owns its manufacturing rather than outsourcing it — a genuine structural trait, not a red flag, but one that also means free cash flow conversion has recently dipped just under this framework's 80% bar. And despite the operating strength, the shares have been hit hard: down roughly 41% from their 52-week high of €2,300 to €1,356.50 amid a sector-wide luxury multiple compression (RBC, HSBC, Deutsche Bank, Citi, Morgan Stanley and JPMorgan have all cut price targets in the past two months) — a real repricing of growth expectations, not a reflection of deteriorating fundamentals.
Not cross-listed on a US exchange: Hermès trades in EUR on Euronext Paris (RMS); US investors can access it only via the unsponsored OTC ADR (HESAY), which carries its own liquidity and spread considerations. All figures on this dashboard are in EUR, Hermès' reporting and primary listing currency.
Why the capex looks "high" for a luxury stock: Hermès spent €708m on capex in FY2025 (4.4% of revenue) and as much as €1,067m in FY2024 (7.0%) building new leather workshops (four planned 2024-2027) to grow production of a deliberately supply-constrained product. A pure fashion-licensing luxury peer would show a lower ratio; Hermès runs higher because it owns the factories, tanneries and workshops that make its products, funding all of it from operating cash flow with zero net debt.

Business Model, Segment Mix & H1 2026 Results
Deliberate scarcity and near-total vertical integration are the two structural pillars behind the margin on page 1 — not a typical "brand licensing" luxury model.
H1 2026 Headlines

Revenue reached €8.16B, up 6.1% at constant exchange rates (1.6% at reported rates, as currency reduced first-half revenue by more than €360m). Growth accelerated through the half: Q1 2026 constant-currency growth was 5.6%, improving to 6.7% in Q2 (4.8% reported, €4.09B). Recurring operating income was €3.35B, a margin of 41.0% (vs 41.4% in H1 2025) — a genuinely minor, 40bps decline despite continued heavy investment in production capacity. Management reiterated confidence in full-year targets, citing broad-based growth across geographies (America, Japan and Europe ex-France all posted gains) even as Perfume & Beauty and parts of the Middle East region remained soft.

Revenue by Division (FY2025 mix, €16.00B total)
Leather Goods & Saddlery — the Birkin/Kelly category — is the core of the business at 44% of revenue and the primary driver of both growth and margin. Hermès is far more concentrated in its highest-margin category than diversified peers like LVMH.
Vertical integration, in plain terms: Hermès owns the Hermès Cuirs Précieux tannery network that secures roughly 90% of its high-grade calfskin needs, and owns tanneries in Australia and Louisiana that supply effectively 100% of its crocodile and alligator skins. Overall, the company manufactures about 55% of what it sells in-house across more than 50 French production sites, versus outsourcing the bulk of production the way most fashion-licensing luxury brands do. This is why Hermès' capex intensity runs higher than a typical luxury peer (see page 4) — it is buying and building factories and workshops, not just marketing budgets and store leases.

Scarcity as strategy: Hermès produces an estimated 12,000-15,000 Birkin bags per year (and a similar order for the Kelly) against demand that industry estimates place at roughly 100x that supply. Rather than expanding output to meet demand, management has held production growth well below revenue growth for the icon leather goods category for over a decade, sustaining multi-year waitlists in most markets. This is the direct source of the pricing power and margin seen on page 1 — and a structural feature this framework treats as a genuine, durable moat rather than a marketing story.

The compression is driven mostly by EPS nearly doubling (€23.30 → €43.00) while the price itself is roughly flat-to-down over the same stretch, not by a collapse in the business.

Share Count Trend

Shares outstanding have been essentially flat for five straight years — EPS-implied count moved from ≈104.94m (FY2021) to ≈105.02m (TTM), a change of roughly +0.08%. There is no meaningful dilution and no active buyback program; Hermès simply doesn't need either lever given its cash generation.

H51 and the family's control structure: roughly 100 descendants of founder Thierry Hermès, spanning the Dumas, Guerrand and Puech branches, consolidated their holdings into H51 SAS in 2011 — explicitly as a defensive measure after LVMH built a secret ~23% stake in Hermès between 2008 and 2010. H51 SAS alone locks roughly 50.2% of capital under a shareholder pact running through 2031; combined with Emile Hermès SAS and other family holdings, the family controls about 66.7% of capital and 78.6% of votes (aided by French double voting rights for long-held registered shares). This is about as durable a founder-family control structure as exists in a public company of this scale, and it is the direct reason Hermès has been able to prioritize the multi-decade Birkin/Kelly scarcity strategy over short-term volume growth that public markets often demand.
Balance sheet: Hermès held €2.31B of total debt against €12.24B of cash & investments at FY2025-end — a net cash position of €9.93B (€94.51 per share), up from €4.92B of net cash just four years earlier. There is no leverage risk of any kind; every euro of the capex program on this page is self-funded.

What's Working

Hermès clears 11 of 14 criteria on this checklist, one of the strongest results on the site. Revenue and operating income have both compounded above 15% a year for four straight years despite already being a mega-cap business, operating margin has never dipped below 40% in five years, and ROIC of roughly 40% dwarfs any reasonable estimate of the company's cost of capital. The balance sheet carries zero net debt (€9.9B of net cash), the share count hasn't moved in five years, and the Hermès family's 66.7%-of-capital, 78.6%-of-votes control via H51 is about as durable a founder-family governance structure as exists anywhere in public markets. Despite trading at an absolute P/E north of 30x, the current multiple sits roughly 39% below the stock's own five-year average — a real, if modest, valuation reprieve after a sharp 2026 de-rating.

What to Watch

The three misses are genuinely connected: FCF CAGR and FCF conversion have both softened because capex (owning tanneries and building new leather workshops) has run at 4-7% of revenue in recent years, well above what a typical outsourced-manufacturing luxury brand would spend — a deliberate structural choice, not a red flag, but one worth tracking if it doesn't moderate. Global luxury also remains a genuinely fragmented, well-competed market (LVMH, Kering, Chanel, Richemont all field strong brands), so the market-concentration criterion fails even though Hermès' own category leadership is not in question. Separately, the shares have been hit meaningfully harder than the fundamentals over the past year — down roughly 41% from a 52-week high of €2,300, with six major sell-side banks cutting price targets since mid-2026 — reflecting a broad luxury-sector multiple reset rather than a company-specific problem, but a real source of near-term volatility for holders.
