DBS is the largest bank in Singapore and Southeast Asia (S$897bn of assets), with leading consumer, wealth, institutional and treasury franchises across Singapore, Hong Kong, Greater China, South Asia and Indonesia. FY2025 total income was S$22.9bn (+2.7%) and reported net profit S$10.9bn, a 16.2% return on equity (17.8% on tangible equity) with a 40.4% cost/income ratio and 17.0% CET1 ratio. Over FY2021–FY2025 total income compounded at ~12.7% and net profit at ~12.6% a year — strong for a bank but below the framework's 15% bar, and heavily helped by the 2022–2023 rate cycle; growth has slowed sharply as margins eased and net profit slipped in FY2025. Management guides 2026 net profit near 2025 levels (third-party call summary) while growing wealth fees, with wealth assets under management of S$680bn and a target above S$1 trillion by 2030. Ordinary dividends have been raised every year and special capital-return dividends added, but the payout (~79% of FY25 profit) is just under the 80% bar. The catch is price: the stock has risen ~2.5x since end-2021 and trades at ~19x trailing earnings and ~3.0x book value, versus ~11.5x and ~1.75x on average over FY2021–FY2025.
Framing note — why this scorecard looks different for a bank: DBS Group is a bank, so the standard revenue/EBIT/FCF/net-debt framework does not map cleanly onto it. The same substitutions used on the JPMorgan Chase page are applied here, all flagged inline: Total Income stands in for revenue (criterion 1); Net Income substitutes for both EBIT (criterion 2) and Free Cash Flow (criterion 4) — a bank's cash flow statement is dominated by deposit and trading-book swings that do not map to a “free cash flow” concept, so criteria 2 and 4 are necessarily identical here, a genuine limitation of the adaptation and not a workaround; ROTE (return on tangible equity) vs. an assumed ~10% cost of equity substitutes for ROIC vs. WACC (criterion 7; DBS reports ROTE, which is used as the ROTCE analog; a ~10% cost of equity is used, the low end of the 10–11% range, as DBS is a very low-beta stock (~0.3) in a AAA-rated sovereign jurisdiction); CET1 ratio vs. the regulatory requirement substitutes for net debt/EBITDA (criterion 8), scored on the cushion over the requirement; and (dividends + buybacks) ÷ net income substitutes for FCF conversion (criterion 9). Entry multiple (criterion 10) uses P/E as the scored measure with P/NAV shown alongside, each against its own FY2021–FY2025 history. Criterion 14 uses the efficiency (cost/income) ratio because banks report no gross profit; a falling ratio counts as a margin rise. Everything is reported in SGD and nothing is converted.

Market position, honestly stated: DBS is the largest bank in Singapore and in Southeast Asia by assets (S$897bn at FY2025), well ahead of OCBC and UOB, which supports criterion #5. Governance & Capital Notes

Temasek, the Singapore government's investment company, is the anchor shareholder at roughly 30% (approximate). DBS has no founder; the stake makes the bank effectively government-linked: it supports stability and a long-term horizon, but means the bank is not a pure private-owner story and policy considerations can matter. It is scored on the 15–30% band (4 points) and flagged as an anchor-shareholder judgment call, not insider ownership. DBS is a D-SIB regulated by MAS. Its policy is a progressive ordinary dividend plus capital-return dividends; the FY25 total of S$3.06 per share includes S$0.60 of capital return.

What's Working

The region's dominant bank: S$897bn of assets, ROTE of 17.8%, a 40.4% cost/income ratio and a 17.0% CET1 ratio (15.0% fully phased-in). Profit is up ~1.6x since FY2021 with ordinary dividends raised every year plus special capital-return dividends; wealth AUM of S$680bn is a growing fee engine. A concentrated home market, government-linked anchor shareholder (Temasek), very low volatility (beta ~0.3) and light capex support the quality case.

What to Watch

Growth is slowing: income +2.7% and profit -3% in FY25, net interest margin down from 2.15% to 2.01%, and 2026 profit guided near 2025 levels. Valuation is the main concern: ~19x earnings and ~3.0x book versus ~11.5x and ~1.75x on average, so expectations are high. The payout (~79%) is just under this framework's bar, ROTE has slipped from 20% to 17.8%, and third-party commentary flags rising bond yields as a risk for Southeast Asian banks. State-linked ownership means policy and geopolitical considerations (US–China tensions, Greater China exposure) matter more than for a purely private bank.
