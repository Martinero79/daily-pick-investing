SpaceX completed the largest IPO in history on June 12, 2026, priced at $135/share and valuing the company around $1.77T on debut, above $2T by the first close. The underlying business is genuinely dominant on the metrics that can be checked: Falcon 9 flew more orbital missions in 2025 than the rest of the world combined, and Starlink is the clear #1 in LEO satellite broadband by a wide margin. But the S-1 also revealed a business under real strain — a consolidated operating loss of $2.589B in 2025 (a -13.86% operating margin), driven almost entirely by a newly-disclosed AI/compute segment that alone lost $6.355B, layered on top of profitable legacy Space and Connectivity (Starlink) segments. Capital intensity is extreme: capex ran somewhere around $20-23B against $18.67B of revenue in 2025 (public sources vary slightly on the exact figure), pushing free cash flow deeply negative even as operating cash flow stayed positive and grew. The one dramatic positive since the IPO is the balance sheet: the $75B raise appears to have flipped SpaceX from roughly $5.5B of net debt pre-IPO to something like $71B of net cash today, based on the company's own Q2 2026 disclosure of ~$100B in combined cash and securities. Elon Musk's ownership and control remain extraordinary — roughly 42% of the economics and about 82% of the vote via a dual-class structure, among the most concentrated founder-control setups this site has seen. None of that changes the core problem for this framework: this site's scorecard is built around 5 years of public trading and reporting history, and SpaceX has had public markets access for less than 3 months. Half the criteria simply can't be tested yet. What can be tested is genuinely mixed — dominant market position and a transformed balance sheet, against a real consolidated operating loss and extreme capex intensity — and the Martinero Index of 59/100 should be read as provisional, not a settled verdict.
Why this dashboard looks different: SpaceX is a normal, tradable public stock (NASDAQ: SPCX) like every other company on this site — it is not lacking legitimacy, just track record. Market Position: Launch, Starlink & the New AI Segment
Market share and concentration are two of the few criteria fully testable without 5 years of history — both are about today's competitive landscape, not a multi-year track record.
Falcon 9 — Launch Dominance

SpaceX flew 165 Falcon 9 orbital missions in 2025 — more than every other launch provider on Earth combined — giving it an estimated ~82% share of global launch cadence/payload mass. Competitors (ULA, Rocket Lab, Blue Origin, Arianespace, and Chinese/Russian state programs) remain far smaller by comparison. Starship, the next-generation super-heavy vehicle, is still in development and does not yet carry commercial payloads — a genuine execution and regulatory-risk item, not yet a revenue contributor.

Starlink — LEO Broadband Leadership

Starlink is the clear #1 in low-earth-orbit satellite broadband, with an estimated 58-62% of global LEO broadband revenue and 10.3M+ subscribers as of the S-1's most recent disclosure (more recent secondary estimates put subscribers closer to 12M), across 9,600+ deployed satellites. Connectivity segment revenue of $11.387B in 2025 grew nearly 50% YoY and generated $4.423B of segment operating income — the most profitable part of the business. Competition is real and growing: Amazon's Kuiper (rebranded "Amazon Leo" in November 2025) is ramping deployment and terminal production, and Eutelsat OneWeb remains a smaller established player. Starlink's first-mover scale and vertically-integrated cost structure are a genuine moat today, but the gap is one to watch, not a permanent given.

Segment Mix, FY2025 Revenue & Operating Result
Connectivity (Starlink) is both the largest and the only clearly profitable core segment; Space (launch/Starship) is roughly break-even to modestly positive; the newly consolidated AI/compute segment is a large loss-maker and is the primary reason the consolidated FY2025 operating margin is negative.

Balance Sheet, Capital Intensity & Ownership
Criterion #10 (entry multiple vs. own history) and #11 (share count trend) are both N/A here — not because the data is unavailable, but because the comparison itself doesn't exist yet 3 months post-IPO. What follows is what actually can be assessed today.
The balance sheet just transformed: as of March 31, 2026 (pre-IPO), SpaceX carried $29.132B of total debt against $15.852B cash + $7.823B marketable securities — a modest net debt position of roughly $5.5B. Its Q2 2026 earnings release (covering the quarter that included the June 12 IPO) reported approximately $100B in combined cash, cash equivalents and marketable securities. Assuming debt is roughly unchanged, that implies a swing to roughly $71B of net cash — comfortably clearing this framework's Net Debt/EBITDA bar (criterion #8, scored Met) against FY2025 consolidated adjusted EBITDA of $6.584B. This is an approximate, derived figure pending a full post-IPO balance sheet disclosure, not a precise company-reported net debt number.
ROIC vs. WACC (#7) — Not met: a precise ROIC figure isn't independently computable from the public disclosure available so far, given how much the invested-capital base just changed with the IPO raise. But the consolidated operating loss (-$2.589B in FY2025) means NOPAT is negative, so ROIC is negative regardless of the exact denominator — it fails both the framework's 15% absolute bar and any reasonable WACC comfortably. This is scored Not Met on that basis rather than left N/A, since the direction of the answer is clear even if the precise magnitude isn't.
Capex intensity (#13) — Not met: FY2025 capex of roughly $20.7-22.8B against $18.674B of revenue is well over 100% of revenue — likely the most capital-intensive name on this entire site, driven by Starlink satellite manufacturing/launch, Starship development, and new AI/compute infrastructure buildout. This is a genuinely capital-hungry business model, not a data artifact.

Public shareholders have essentially no practical ability to influence matters requiring a shareholder vote. Criterion #12 (insider/founder ownership) scores Met on the strength of that alignment — though extreme concentration cuts both ways for minority shareholders.

What's Working

Genuine, checkable market dominance in both launch (Falcon 9, ~82% share) and LEO broadband (Starlink, ~58-62% share); a balance sheet that appears to have swung to roughly $71B of net cash after the IPO; and the most concentrated, aligned founder-control structure of any company on this site (Musk, ~82% of the vote). Revenue has grown every year disclosed, and operating cash flow has been positive and growing throughout.

What to Watch

The consolidated business is currently GAAP loss-making, driven by a large, newly-disclosed AI/compute segment loss layered on top of extremely capital-intensive legacy operations (capex over 100% of revenue). Starship remains under development with real technical and regulatory risk. And structurally: 7 of 14 criteria on this scorecard are N/A simply because SpaceX hasn't existed as a public company long enough to test them — this dashboard should be revisited as more quarters and years of public data accumulate.
