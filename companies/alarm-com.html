Alarm.com Holdings is a cloud software company, not an alarm company in the traditional sense: it builds and licenses the SaaS platform — apps, cloud infrastructure, AI-based video analytics, energy management (EnergyHub), and back-office tools — that independently-owned security and smart-home dealers and commercial integrators use to run their businesses and serve their end customers, across North America and increasingly internationally. Founded in 2000 and headquartered in Tysons, Virginia, it IPO'd on Nasdaq in June 2015. The company doesn't manufacture most of its own hardware at scale, doesn't run monitoring centers, and critically doesn't own the end-subscriber relationship — the dealer does. In return, Alarm.com collects a high-margin, recurring per-account SaaS fee and keeps the business genuinely capital-light: 66% gross margin and capex under 2% of revenue in every one of the last five years. Growth has been steady rather than explosive (revenue CAGR ~7.8% FY2021-25), but profitability has scaled cleanly underneath it — GAAP operating margin nearly doubled from 6.1% (FY2021, distorted by one-off items) / 9.9% (normalized) to 13.6% (FY2025), non-GAAP adjusted EBITDA margin already exceeds 20%, and free cash flow has converted at 100-180% of EBIT in four of the last five years. The balance sheet carries a $500M convertible note raised in 2024 (2.25% due 2029), but most of that was used to fund buybacks and capped-call dilution protection rather than operations, and net leverage remains low at roughly half a turn of EBITDA. Shares outstanding are actually declining (-5.35% YoY) under an active $150M repurchase authorization. FY2026 guidance points to continued moderate, high-teens-adjusted-EBITDA-growth, not a re-acceleration — this reads as a mature, disciplined, high-quality software compounder growing in the high single digits, not a hypergrowth name.
Maarten's note

I built this one right alongside Verisure on purpose, because they sit on opposite ends of the same industry and it's a genuinely useful contrast. Verisure owns the entire stack — hardware, installation, the monitoring center, and the direct subscriber relationship — in exchange for heavier capex, lower gross margins, and concentration risk in specific European geographies. Alarm.com does almost none of that: it licenses a cloud platform to thousands of independent dealers who take on the truck-roll and hardware economics themselves, while Alarm.com collects a recurring SaaS fee at ~66% gross margins and under 2% of revenue in capex. On paper the software layer looks like the better seat — no hardware inventory risk, cheap incremental economics, and exposure spread across a whole dealer network instead of one brand's ability to keep churn low. But it comes with a real trade-off: Alarm.com doesn't own the end customer, so if a large dealer, or ADT/Vivint at scale, ever pushed harder to build or buy an in-house platform, Alarm.com could lose volume it never directly controlled to begin with. Verisure's model is heavier but stickier in a different way — once it installs the hardware and signs the multi-year contract, it owns that relationship completely, no intermediary in between. My honest take: I'd rather sit on the software margins than carry the subscriber-acquisition cost and hardware capex, but dealer concentration and platform-competition risk are the things I'd actually want to track before sizing this anywhere near a name with a harder lock on the end customer.

2024 convertible notes, in plain terms: Alarm.com raised $500M via 2.25% convertible senior notes due 2029, using ~$63.1M for capped-call transactions (capping potential dilution at a $134.28/share strike, a 100% premium to the May 2024 price) and ~$75.0M for an immediate share repurchase, with the remainder earmarked for general corporate purposes including acquisitions, further buybacks, and working capital. Read this as opportunistic balance-sheet management by a cash-generative company, not a distress signal — leverage remains low (~0.5x net debt/EBITDA) either way.

What's Working

Alarm.com's core economics are genuinely SaaS-like and improving every year: 66% gross margin, GAAP EBIT margin expanding from 9.9% (FY2021) to 13.6% (FY2025) even as non-GAAP adjusted EBITDA margin already clears 20%, and free cash flow converting at 100-180% of EBIT in four of the last five years. Capex is structurally tiny (1.6% of revenue) because the company doesn't own hardware manufacturing or the monitoring centers its dealers use. Net debt/EBITDA is low (~0.5x) even after a 2024 convertible-note raise used mostly to fund buybacks, share count is genuinely declining (-5.35% YoY) under an active $150M buyback authorization, and the stock trades at a meaningful discount to its own 5-year average trailing P/E (23.6x vs 41.7x) and forward P/E (18.3x vs 31.7x) — the market has derated this from early-SaaS-era multiples toward something closer to a mature, moderate-growth software business.

What to Watch

Growth is the honest weak point against this site's quality-compounder bar: 5-year revenue CAGR (7.8%) and FCF CAGR (10.5%) both sit well under the 15% threshold, and GAAP EBIT margin (13.6%) is still below the 20% bar even as it's plainly trending the right way. Alarm.com doesn't own the end-customer relationship — it licenses software to independent dealers — so its moat rests on dealer switching costs and network effects rather than a hard contractual lock on subscribers the way a vertically-integrated monitoring company has; a large dealer defecting, or a well-funded in-house platform push from ADT or Vivint, is a real, structural competitive risk rather than a remote one. FY2026 guidance (SaaS/license revenue +9%, adjusted EBITDA +8-9%) points to continued moderate, not explosive, growth.
