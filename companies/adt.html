ADT is the largest name in US home and small-business security, tracing back to a burglar-alarm telegraph business founded in 1874 and carried through decades of corporate ownership (Tyco, then a 2012 Tyco spinoff, then a 2016 Apollo Global Management leveraged buyout that took it private before a 2018 re-IPO on the NYSE). The company's recent history is really a story of portfolio simplification and deleveraging rather than growth: it sold its ADT Commercial fire-and-security division to GTCR for roughly $1.6B in October 2021 (the driver of that year's revenue decline and depressed operating income), took a $450M-then-$150M strategic investment and technology alliance from Google/Nest in 2020 to modernize its hardware roadmap, exited the solar installation business (sold its Sunpro stake to Sunrun in 2023) after that segment's revenue and margins disappointed, and in 2022 sold State Farm a $1.2B, roughly 15%-at-the-time equity stake as part of a home-insurance and smart-home risk-prevention partnership. Apollo, ADT's controlling post-LBO owner, sold down its position gradually over several years and fully exited in a May 2026 secondary offering. What's left is a US incumbent with genuinely dominant share of a mature, well-penetrated market, throwing off strong free cash flow (FCF margin above 25%) that increasingly funds dividends and buybacks (a new $1.5B repurchase authorization was announced alongside FY2025 results) rather than growth capex, against a balance sheet still carrying real leverage from its private-equity past.
Leverage is the legacy, not a current crisis: Net debt/EBITDA of ≈2.82x (TTM) is down meaningfully from the years right after the 2016 Apollo LBO, and interest coverage (≈3.0x) and the company's own capital-allocation choices (raising the dividend, adding a $1.5B buyback authorization in March 2026) suggest management is comfortable operating here rather than racing back toward 2x. Still, it's the single biggest reason this scorecard tops out around 65/100 rather than higher — a genuinely dominant, cash-generative business that has to service a meaningfully levered balance sheet before returning capital freely.
Q2 2026 / raised guidance: Revenue grew 2% YoY to $1.3B, adjusted free cash flow surged 48% YoY to $406M, and ADT returned $684M to shareholders (buybacks + dividends) in H1 2026. Revenue grew 2% YoY to $1.3B with recurring monthly revenue (RMR) of $360M, gross revenue attrition of 13.1%, and revenue payback of 2.3 years — all broadly stable operating metrics. Competitive structure: ADT's own historical SEC disclosures put it at roughly 30% share of the US/Canada residential monitored-security market and about 5x the size of the next-largest residential competitor by customer count (Vivint, SimpliSafe, Comcast/Xfinity, Brinks Home, Telus each serve a fraction of ADT's ≈6.4M customers). That said, the broader market remains genuinely fragmented beneath ADT's leading position — thousands of small regional/independent alarm dealers still operate, and DIY/self-monitored entrants (Ring, SimpliSafe) compete for the lower end of the market in a way that barely existed a decade ago. ADT itself offers a self-monitored tier to compete there rather than cede it.
Capex intensity is genuinely falling: Subscriber-acquisition and hardware installation costs have historically been the industry's biggest drag on free cash flow. ADT's capex fell from $911M (20.8% of revenue) in FY2022 to $572M (11.2% of revenue) in FY2025 — a real structural improvement, not just a cyclical dip, and the main reason FCF has closed most of the gap to EBIT over the same period (see page 4).

Maarten's Note — ADT vs. Verisure

ADT and Verisure are both leaders in monitored-alarm security, but they're different bets on the same business model. ADT is the incumbent in the world's largest, most-penetrated security market (the US), trading as a leveraged, income-oriented public equity: net debt/EBITDA around 2.8x, a dividend yield above 3%, and buybacks steadily shrinking the share count, but top-line growth stuck in the low single digits and gross margin quietly eroding (≈84% in FY2022 to ≈81% in FY2025) as hardware and installation costs bite. Verisure, today's featured pick, is privately held (majority-owned by Hellman & Friedman since 2021, with EQT and GIC among other backers) and pursuing a faster-growing, less-penetrated footprint across Europe and Latin America, where monitored-alarm penetration remains well below the roughly 25-30% seen in the US. The two are effectively running different playbooks on the same underlying moat — recurring monitoring revenue, high switching costs, sticky RMR: ADT is optimizing a mature market for cash return to shareholders, while Verisure is still selling penetration growth in markets that haven't caught up yet. Both face the same long-run question — how much of that moat survives cheaper DIY and self-monitored competition (Ring, SimpliSafe, and their European equivalents) as the category matures.

What's Working

Genuine, durable market dominance (≈30% share, ≈5x the next-largest residential competitor), stable 25-26% operating margins for three straight years, strong and improving free cash flow conversion (98.4% of EBIT in FY2025), a falling capex-intensity trend (20.8% of revenue in FY2022 to 11.2% in FY2025) as subscriber-acquisition economics improve, a share count that's genuinely declining through buybacks, and a valuation that sits at a real discount to its own recent trading history. Strategic capital from State Farm and Google adds commercial alignment beyond a typical shareholder base, and Apollo's full exit in May 2026 closes out nearly a decade of private-equity overhang.

What to Watch

Growth is the clearest weak point on this scorecard — revenue, EBIT (on a clean basis) and FCF are all compounding well below the 15-20%+ bar this site looks for, consistent with a mature, highly-penetrated US market rather than a growth story. Net debt/EBITDA of 2.82x is a direct legacy of the 2016 Apollo LBO and, while declining, still caps how freely capital can be returned. Gross margin has eroded ≈3.2pp over the last three years as hardware/installation costs have risen, and capex intensity, while improving, remains structurally high for this industry. The competitive backdrop is also shifting: DIY and self-monitored entrants (Ring, SimpliSafe) compete for the lower end of the market in a way that barely existed a decade ago, and the Google/Nest alliance and State Farm partnership both carry real commercial dependency risk if either relationship changes.
