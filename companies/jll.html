JLL is a genuine, durable #2 in global commercial real estate services — $26.1B of FY2025 revenue behind only CBRE, a position it has held consistently, not a marginal or contested one. The growth and margin story underneath is a cyclical one: operating margin bottomed at 3.26% in FY2023 during the 2022-23 rate-driven real estate downturn (net income fell to just $225.4M that year) and has since recovered to 4.49% in FY2025, with EBIT nearly doubling off the trough. But four years out from FY2021, EBIT ($1,173M) is barely above where it started ($1,128M), and gross margin has fallen every single year since FY2021 (56.18% → 51.12%) — the exact opposite of what this framework found at Colliers, where gross margin actually improved over the same window. What's genuinely working: the balance sheet is meaningfully cleaner than Colliers', with net debt/EBITDA around 1.3x versus Colliers' 3-4x roll-up leverage; FCF conversion cleared 80% in FY2025; capex is minimal (0.83% of revenue, even lower than Colliers'); and JLL has been quietly shrinking its share count through sustained buybacks (down roughly 11.6% since FY2021). Return on invested capital (10.16%) does clear stockanalysis.com's own auto-computed cost of capital (9.60%), but only by a hair, and falls well short of this framework's 15% bar. There's no founder or controlling shareholder here either — JLL has been a fully institutional, widely-held public company since the 1999 merger that created it, so there's no equivalent of a founder's outsized skin in the game to point to. The stock has recovered strongly off its 2022-23 lows and sits up roughly 9.98% over the past year, with a trailing P/E (16.59x) meaningfully below its own FY22-25 average (23.36x) and a forward P/E (13.16x) cheaper still. Net: 6 of 14 criteria met, Martinero Index 46/100 — a cleaner balance sheet and a more durable market position than Colliers, but a thinner and, unusually among this site's real estate names, structurally eroding margin. This isn't a call to buy or avoid JLL — just what the checklist says today.

Trailing & Forward P/E — FY22-25 & Current
Current trailing P/E of 16.59x sits well below the FY22-25 average of roughly 23.36x — a real discount, though FY2023's 39.86x outlier was inflated by a trough earnings base (net income fell to just $225.4M that year during the CRE downturn) rather than a genuinely rich price at the time. Forward P/E (13.16x) is cheaper still. Unlike Colliers, where historical forward P/E wasn't reliably available, JLL's forward P/E does have multi-year history per stockanalysis.com: 10.55x (FY2022), 18.24x (FY2023), 16.21x (FY2024), 17.18x (FY2025) — a fairly consistent expectation of continued earnings growth through the cycle, not just a today-only re-rating.
Trailing P/E Forward P/E (current)
Reading the three P/E numbers: current P/E is today's share price divided by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings — a backward-looking snapshot at today's price. Trailing P/E (the FY22-25 series above) is that same backward-looking ratio calculated at each fiscal year-end, showing how the market has re-rated the stock over time relative to what it had already earned. Forward P/E is today's share price divided by analysts' consensus estimate of the next 12 months of earnings — forward-looking, and generally the more useful of the two for judging whether a stock is priced fairly relative to where its earnings are headed. For JLL specifically: trailing P/E (16.59x) looks cheap next to its own FY22-25 average (23.36x), and forward P/E (13.16x) is cheaper still, implying the market expects continued earnings growth from here. One more lens worth a glance: the PEG ratio, forward P/E divided by expected annual EPS growth, which sanity-checks whether a "cheap" multiple is actually cheap once growth is accounted for. Using forward P/E (13.16x) against the 3-year consensus EPS growth forecast (19.61%, per stockanalysis.com), JLL's PEG works out to roughly 0.67 — comfortably under the traditional 1.0 "fair value" line, and cheaper on this measure than Colliers' own 0.90, suggesting JLL isn't just discounted versus its own history, it's also genuinely inexpensive relative to expected growth, assuming that growth actually shows up.
Balance sheet: net debt of $2,025M against FY2025 EBITDA of roughly $1,536M (EBIT $1,173M + D&A $363.1M) gives a Net Debt/EBITDA of ≈1.32x — comfortably under this framework's <2x bar, and dramatically cleaner than Colliers' 3-4x roll-up leverage. On a trailing-twelve-month basis (through Jun 2026), net debt has widened slightly to $2,761M against TTM EBITDA of roughly $1,685M (EBIT $1,341M + D&A $344.3M), or ≈1.64x — still comfortably under 2x. ROIC of 10.16% does clear stockanalysis.com's own auto-computed 9.60% WACC, but only by a narrow 0.56pt spread, and falls well short of this framework's 15% absolute bar. Share count has declined from an implied ≈52.06M shares in FY2021 (derived from reported FY2021 EPS and net income) to 46.01M currently, a ≈11.6% reduction — real, sustained buybacks (including $251.1M repurchased in FY2025 alone), not dilution.

Governance note: JLL has had no founder or controlling shareholder since its formation — the company was created by the 1999 merger of Jones Lang Wootton (UK, founded 1783) and LaSalle Partners (US, founded 1968), and has operated as a fully institutional, widely-held public company ever since, with no original founders on the board or in senior management today. What's Working

JLL is a genuine, durable #2 globally by revenue, its balance sheet is meaningfully cleaner than Colliers' (net debt/EBITDA ≈1.3x versus 3-4x), FCF conversion clears 80%, capex is minimal even by services-brokerage standards (0.83% of revenue), the entry multiple sits at a real discount to its own recent history, and the company has been quietly shrinking its share count through sustained buybacks.

What to Watch

Operating margin (4.49%) is the thinnest of the "big five" names looked at so far, and — unlike Colliers — the trend has gone the wrong way: gross margin has fallen every year for four straight years (56.2% → 51.1%), narrowly missing this framework's margin-stability threshold. ROIC barely clears JLL's own cost of capital and falls well short of the 15% absolute bar, and there's no founder or controlling shareholder to point to for alignment — JLL has been a fully institutional, widely-held company since its 1999 formation.
