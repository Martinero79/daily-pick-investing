General Motors is a cash-rich, shrinking-share-count automaker with thin margins. Revenue grew a modest 9.9% a year from FY2021 to FY2025 ($127.0B to $185.0B) but has turned flat, EBIT-adjusted ($14.3B to $12.7B) has gone sideways to slightly down, and gross margin has slid from 13.5% to 10.7% in three years as tariffs, EV losses and mix weighed. What GM does have is cash generation: adjusted automotive free cash flow reached $10.6B in FY2025 ($14.0B in FY2024), and the company has retired roughly 40% of its shares since 2021 — from about 1.47B to about 877M — while still funding roughly $9–10B a year of capex. Basis note: this page uses GM's own EBIT-adjusted and adjusted automotive free cash flow (which exclude captive lender GM Financial) wherever disclosed, and industrial net cash rather than the consolidated ~$104B net debt, most of which funds GM Financial's customer loans and leases. 2026 has started better: Q2 revenue was $48.0B (+1.9% YoY), EBIT-adjusted $3.9B, and guidance was raised twice to $14.0–16.0B. Trailing GAAP P/E looks extreme (≈40x) only because of the $7.2B of Q4 2025 EV charges; on forward earnings the stock trades near 5.8x.

Forward P/E has stayed in a 5–9x range throughout and is ≈5.8x now, about 11% below its own FY21–25 average (≈6.5x). Source: S&P Global via StockAnalysis (year-end values; third-party).
Trailing P/E Forward P/E
Reading trailing vs. forward P/E: trailing P/E divides today's price by the last 12 months of reported GAAP earnings; at GM that denominator now includes the large Q4 2025 EV-realignment charge, so the multiple (≈40x) overstates how expensive the stock is on normal earnings. Forward P/E uses next-12-month consensus (≈5.8x), but it is only as good as the forecasts, and auto earnings are cyclical and exposed to tariffs, EV policy and pricing. PEG is ≈0.35 per S&P data (consensus EPS growth of ~16% a year). The scorecard (criterion 10) is scored on the trailing basis, which gives 0/5 (ratio ≈3.96); on the forward basis the ratio would be ≈0.89 (3/5). Low P/E multiples are normal for automakers and often reflect cyclicality rather than a bargain.
Balance sheet — industrial vs captive: consolidated debt is ≈$129B against ≈$25B of cash (net debt ≈$104B, Q2 2026), but the large majority funds GM Financial, the captive lender, whose receivables and leased vehicles back that debt. On an industrial basis GM carries ≈$16B of automotive debt against roughly $21.7B of automotive cash at year-end 2025 — a net cash position of about $5B (estimate; third-party/aggregated figures). GM Financial's full-year 2025 EBT-adjusted was $2.8B. Industrial net debt/EBITDA is therefore scored as net cash, while the consolidated figure (debt/EBITDA ≈7.7x per S&P) is not comparable to non-financial companies.

What's Working

Cash generation is real: adjusted automotive FCF of $10.6B in FY2025 and $5.0B in Q2 2026 alone, with the industrial balance sheet in net cash. Share count is down about 40% since 2021 through buybacks that shrank the denominator faster than earnings fell, and the dividend has been raised each year since its 2022 reinstatement. 2026 guidance has been raised twice, Q1 and Q2 EBIT-adjusted margins recovered to 9.9% and 8.1%, and GM is the largest US automaker with a strong truck and SUV franchise.

What to Watch

Margins are thin and falling: gross margin has dropped 2.8pp in three years and EBIT-adjusted has gone nowhere since FY2021 despite 46% more revenue. Tariff costs ($2.5–3.5B gross guided for 2026), EV demand and the write-downs already taken, a cyclical downturn in US auto sales (Q3 2026 US sales −5.5%), and share loss to Asian rivals are the main risks. GM Financial adds leverage and credit-cycle exposure, ROIC barely clears an already-low WACC, insiders own almost nothing, and buybacks, while large, do not offset flat earnings power.
