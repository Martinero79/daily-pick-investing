Verisure is Europe and Latin America's largest professionally-monitored home and business security company — alarms, cameras and 24/7 emergency response sold as a recurring monthly subscription. Founded in Sweden in 1988 as Securitas Direct (a division of security giant Securitas AB), spun off in 2006, then acquired and rebranded Verisure by private equity firm Hellman & Friedman in 2011 (H&F became sole majority owner in 2015), the company spent nearly a decade and a half under PE ownership before IPO'ing on Nasdaq Stockholm on October 8, 2025 at €13.25/share, a ≈€13.7bn listing valuation. The underlying business is genuinely strong on the metrics that matter most to this checklist: revenue has grown 9-13% every single year since 2021, adjusted EBIT margin has expanded for three straight years to 25.4% in FY2025 (26.7% by H1 2026), and Verisure holds the #1 position in 13 of its 17 markets, roughly 2.7x the size of its nearest competitor on a weighted basis. What the checklist penalizes is the balance sheet and cash-flow legacy of that PE ownership: net debt/EBITDA was running above 5x before the IPO, capex to acquire and install subscriber alarm equipment consumes 24-26% of revenue every year, and free cash flow only turned reliably positive in the second half of 2025 after years of funding rapid subscriber growth. The stock itself has had a rough first year as a public company — down roughly 39% from its IPO price and sitting at fresh 52-week lows as of this writing, even as the underlying operating numbers have kept improving quarter after quarter. That gap between "the business is executing" and "the stock has been a poor performer since listing" is the single most important thing to understand about Verisure today.
PE-to-public transition, in plain terms: IPO proceeds were used to pay down debt (cutting net leverage from ≈5x to ≈3.0x at listing) and to fund the acquisition of ADT Mexico. Hellman & Friedman retained roughly 46% of shares post-IPO and became free to sell down its remaining stake once its lock-up expired in April 2026 — a real overhang risk worth watching, since a large sponsor with a multi-decade holding period eventually selling down is a standard feature (not a red flag by itself) of post-PE-IPO stocks, but one that can weigh on the share price independent of fundamentals.
Q2 2026 read: revenue €1,024m (+10.3% YoY), adjusted EBIT margin +83bps to 26.3% for the quarter, H1 2026 adjusted EBIT +16.6% to €546m, subscribers near 6.4m (+9.4% YoY), and free cash flow positive for a third consecutive quarter (€56m in Q2 alone, €95m for H1 2026) as capex intensity eased to 23.9% of sales from 25.8% a year earlier. Net debt fell to €4.9bn with LTM leverage down to 2.7x. This is a company executing consistently on its own operating plan — the stock's post-IPO decline reflects de-rating and sentiment (leverage, sponsor-overhang risk, general small/mid-cap Nordic-listing liquidity), not a deteriorating business.

Recent Performance & Market Position
Operating momentum has been consistent through Verisure's first year as a public company, even as the share price has not followed.
Subscriber base & market position

Verisure serves ≈6.4 million subscribers across 17 European and Latin American markets (as of Q2 2026, +9.4% YoY), roughly 90% of revenue from Europe and 10% from Latin America following the 2025 acquisition of ADT Mexico. The company holds the #1 monitored-alarm position in 13 of its 17 geographies and is, on a subscriber-weighted basis, roughly 2.7x the size of its nearest competitor — a genuinely dominant share in a category (professionally-monitored home/business security) that remains structurally underpenetrated in most of continental Europe compared to the US and UK. Average revenue per user (ARPU) was €48.2 in Q2 2026 (+2.4% constant-currency), and EBITDA-per-customer (EPC) reached a record €35.71 (+2.8% YoY), both signs of continued pricing power and successful premium-service upsell (cameras, remote monitoring, senior-care adjacencies) rather than pure subscriber-count growth.

Price marker (€)
Why trailing/forward P/E is used cautiously here: trailing P/E of ≈17.7x reflects thin reported net income after heavy interest expense on Verisure's PE-era debt load (2026 consensus net profit is just €105-112m against €953m of adjusted EBIT) — a leverage effect, not a reflection of the underlying operating business, which is growing adjusted EBIT at double digits. Forward P/E of ≈12.5x (consensus, ~49% forecast EPS growth as interest costs shrink with deleveraging) and a PEG of ≈0.38 both suggest the market is pricing a real near-term profit inflection as leverage keeps falling — but with under a year of public history and only 11 sell-side analysts covering the name, both figures carry wider-than-usual estimate uncertainty. EV/EBITDA (ttm) of ≈9.4x is offered as a supplementary, leverage-neutral cross-check.
CapEx & CapEx Intensity (% of Revenue)
Structurally high and a genuine weak point of this business model: every new or upgraded subscriber requires alarm hardware, installation and often cameras, all capitalized as capex rather than expensed — capex has run 23-26% of revenue in every recent period, easing only modestly as the subscriber base matures.

Share Count Trend

≈1.034bn shares outstanding, essentially fixed since the October 2025 IPO (a mostly-primary offering that raised capital for debt paydown and the ADT Mexico deal, rather than an ongoing dilutive equity program). No buyback program in place given the priority on continued deleveraging. Watch for potential secondary sell-downs from H&F now that its April 2026 lock-up has expired — these would not dilute share count but could pressure the price via supply/overhang.

Ownership note: Hellman & Friedman first acquired control of the business (then Securitas Direct) in 2011, rebranding it Verisure, and became sole majority owner in 2015 — meaning H&F's exposure to this specific business already runs well over a decade, one of the longer PE holding periods among recent European IPOs. Free float at listing was roughly 26% of shares outstanding, on the lower end for a large-cap European IPO, which plausibly contributes to the stock's thin post-IPO liquidity and outsized price swings relative to the pace of change in the underlying business.

What's Working

A genuinely dominant European/LatAm market position (#1 in 13/17 markets, ≈2.7x nearest rival) in a category with real multi-year runway given lower alarm-monitoring penetration outside the US/UK. Revenue has grown 9-13% every year since 2021 with no down year, adjusted EBIT margin has expanded for three straight years to 25.4% (26.7% by H1 2026) with 15 consecutive quarters of double-digit adjusted EBIT growth, leverage has fallen from ≈5x to 2.7x in under two years and keeps improving, free cash flow has turned reliably positive for three straight quarters, and the share count is essentially fixed with no dilution. The stock also now trades roughly 39% below its own IPO price even as every one of these operating metrics has kept improving — a genuine gap between price and fundamentals worth tracking.

What to Watch

Net debt/EBITDA (2.7x) and capex intensity (24-26% of revenue) are the two criteria this checklist was built to catch, and Verisure fails both honestly — a decade-plus of PE ownership and a subscriber-acquisition-heavy business model, not one-off items. FCF conversion (47.7%) and a still-negative ROIC-vs-WACC spread follow from the same two factors. Hellman & Friedman's remaining ≈46% stake is now free of its April 2026 lock-up — a large potential secondary sell-down is a real overhang, separate from operating performance. With under 12 months of public trading history, only 11 sell-side analysts covering the name, and a free float of just ≈26% at listing, this stock's share-price volatility (a 52-week range spanning €8.05-16.61, more than 2x top-to-bottom) is likely to stay higher than its underlying operating consistency would suggest for a while yet.

Maarten's Note

What pulled me into Verisure isn't the chart — the chart is ugly, down almost 40% in a year, and I'd normally scroll past that. It's the gap underneath it. This is a company that has grown revenue 9-13% every single year since 2021 without a single down year, expanded its adjusted EBIT margin for three years straight, and holds the #1 monitored-alarm position in 13 of 17 markets by a wide margin — and the market has spent a year re-rating it downward anyway. That's the kind of setup I find genuinely interesting to dig into: either the market knows something about the leverage or the sponsor overhang that I'm underweighting, or it's mostly a post-IPO de-rating and thin-float liquidity story on top of a business that keeps doing exactly what it said it would do.

The leverage is the real risk, not a footnote. Fourteen years under Hellman & Friedman ownership left this business with a debt load built for a private, patient owner, and net debt/EBITDA above 2x with capex eating a quarter of revenue every year are honest, structural weak points on this checklist — not accounting noise I can explain away. Add in H&F's ~46% stake now free of lock-up, and there's a real, ongoing risk that a large secondary sell-down keeps pressuring the price independent of how the business performs. I'm not pretending that risk away.

But recurring-revenue subscription businesses with genuine pricing power and a decade-plus of category leadership don't come up for public trading at a 39% discount to their own IPO price very often, especially ones where every quarterly print since listing has actually gotten better, not worse. Verisure is a business I want to keep watching closely through a few more quarters of deleveraging before I'd size a real position — the operating trend needs to keep compounding for a while longer before I'd trust that the multiple compression is opportunity rather than a warning I haven't fully priced in yet.
