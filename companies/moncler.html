Moncler is the Milan-based luxury outerwear house built around its namesake down-jacket brand, founded in 1952 and transformed into a genuine luxury powerhouse under chairman and CEO Remo Ruffini, who engineered a management buyout of the struggling company in 2003, relisted it on Borsa Italiana in December 2013, and remains its largest and most influential shareholder to this day. In December 2020 Moncler agreed to acquire a 70% stake in Sportswear Company S.p.A. (parent of Stone Island) from the Rivetti family's Rivetex vehicle, then bought the remaining 30% from Temasek Holdings for €345 million in 2021 to reach full ownership — a deal that valued Stone Island at €1.15 billion (16.6x 2020 EBITDA) and gave Moncler a genuine second growth engine in streetwear-adjacent luxury menswear. The combined group is widely regarded as one of the better-run, more operationally disciplined names in the luxury sector: operating margin has held in a tight 28-30% band every year since 2021, ROIC has stayed comfortably in the high-teens-to-20s throughout, and the balance sheet carries net cash rather than net debt in most recent years. Growth has genuinely decelerated from the 2021-23 pandemic-reopening and Stone Island-integration surge (revenue CAGR FY21-25 works out to +11.2%, EBIT CAGR +12.1% — both real, just short of this framework's 15% bar) to a more mature, single-digit-to-low-double-digit cruise speed, with free cash flow essentially flat over the period (FCF CAGR +0.2%) because FY2021's unusually low capex year set an elevated base. The live 2026 story is a regional split rather than the uniform China-demand-softness narrative affecting some luxury peers: H1 2026 group revenue grew 9% at constant FX, with Asia (including China and South Korea) outperforming while Europe underperformed on weaker tourist flows and softer local demand — the opposite pattern from what several other luxury names have reported this cycle.
Stone Island, in plain terms: Moncler's 2020-21 acquisition of Stone Island (70% from the Rivetti family in Dec 2020, remaining 30% from Temasek for €345m in 2021, full deal valuing the brand at €1.15bn) gave the group a second, faster-growing brand with real scale — Stone Island's combined DTC + wholesale revenue has grown from about €310m (FY2021) to roughly €411m (FY2025), even as its wholesale channel has been deliberately trimmed in favor of directly-operated stores, the same DTC-mix-shift playbook Moncler itself has run for over a decade.
H1 2026 read: Group revenue €1,289.9m, +9% at constant FX (Q1 2026 +12% cFX, decelerating to +5% cFX in Q2 as European tourist flows and local demand softened); EBIT margin around 19% for the half (seasonally the lighter half of the year); management flagged net cash exceeding €1.1bn on its own narrower balance-sheet definition. Asia-Pacific/Japan/Korea revenue was +13% (+12% in Q2 specifically) — a genuine bright spot, not a China-demand-softness story for this particular name.

Recent Performance, Stone Island & 2026 Trading
The 2026 growth story is a regional split, not a uniform slowdown — Asia is a genuine bright spot while Europe has softened on weaker tourism.
Stone Island — acquisition & integration, in plain terms

Moncler announced the acquisition of a 70% stake in Sportswear Company S.p.A. (SPW, Stone Island's parent) from the Rivetti family's Rivetex S.r.l. vehicle in December 2020, funded partly through a capital increase reserved for SPW's shareholders (April 2021, which added roughly 20.9m new Moncler shares). It then bought the remaining 30% from Temasek Holdings for €345 million in 2021, closing at full ownership by March 2021. The combined deal valued Stone Island at €1.15 billion — 16.6x 2020 EBITDA / 13.5x estimated 2021 EBITDA. Stone Island's own DTC+wholesale revenue has grown from roughly €310m (FY2021) to about €411m (FY2025), while its wholesale mix has been deliberately trimmed in favor of directly-operated stores — the same channel-mix playbook Moncler itself has run on its namesake brand for over a decade, and a genuine, ongoing source of incremental growth for the group beyond the core Moncler brand.

Regional split—Asia-Pacific/Japan/Korea +13% cFX; EMEA softer on weaker tourist flows and local demand
Why Europe lagged Asia this cycle: Moncler's Q2 2026 deceleration (to +5% cFX from Q1's +12%) and a mid-2026 share-price drop of over 7% on the print were driven specifically by weaker European tourist flows into Moncler's European stores and softer local European demand — not by the softer Chinese/Asian luxury demand that has weighed on several other luxury names this cycle. Asia-Pacific/Japan/Korea revenue actually grew a healthy +13% cFX in H1 2026 (+12% in Q2). Read this as a genuine, name-specific geographic mix effect rather than Moncler being immune to or unusually exposed to the sector's broader China-demand story.
Analyst target spread: 25 analysts cover the stock with a consensus Buy rating and an average 12-month price target of €61.33 (+33.8% from the current price), though individual targets have moved in both directions through 2026 (Citi raised to €63 in September after earlier cutting to €62 in July; Deutsche Bank cut to €57 from €58 in September while keeping a Hold; Morgan Stanley initiated coverage at Equal Weight, PT €57, in July) — a genuinely split read on how much the European tourism/demand softness matters going forward.

At 18.56x, current pricing sits roughly 32% below the FY21-25 average of 27.15x — a real discount to Moncler's own trading history, though FY2021's 43.86x print (post-reopening euphoria, pre-full-Stone-Island-integration) pulls that average up; excluding FY2021, the FY22-25 average is closer to 22.97x, making today's 18.56x still a genuine, if smaller, discount.
Trailing P/E Forward P/E (current only)
Why the discount is real but not enormous: trailing P/E of 18.56x vs. a 5-yr average of 27.15x implies a 32% discount, but that average is skewed by FY2021's 43.86x post-reopening print; a FY22-25-only average of 22.97x still leaves today's multiple about 19% below history — a genuine, if more modest, re-rating lower alongside the deceleration in growth. Forward P/E of 17.72x is only slightly below the trailing 18.56x, and the PEG ratio (2.80x on the current estimate vintage, using consensus forward growth) sits well above the traditional 1.0x "fair value" line — the market isn't pricing this as cheap relative to its own (now slower) growth rate, even though it looks cheap relative to its own trading history.
Balance sheet: Cash & investments of €1,477m against total debt of €1,128m at FY2025-end (Dec 31, 2025), for net cash of roughly €349m on stockanalysis.com's methodology (which treats IFRS16 lease liabilities as debt). Moncler was in a net cash position at 4 of the last 5 fiscal year-ends (the lone exception being a small €19m net debt position at FY2022-end). Current ratio 2.42x, quick ratio 1.86x (FY2025) — a genuinely conservative balance sheet with no leverage-driven risk to the equity story.

Share Count Trend

Shares outstanding (excl. treasury) at fiscal year-end have stayed essentially flat over FY2021-25: 269.6m (2021, already reflecting the April 2021 Stone Island-related capital increase) to 271.6m (2025), a rise of only about +0.75% — ongoing stock-option exercises have been almost entirely offset rather than causing meaningful dilution, and there is no large-scale buyback program in place.

Ownership note: Remo Ruffini, Moncler's chairman and CEO since he engineered a management buyout of the company in 2003, controls the group through his holding vehicle Double R S.r.l., which owned 18.2% of Moncler (50,089,929 shares) as of the company's own share-information page, last updated Aug 24, 2026. In September 2024, LVMH agreed to buy a minority stake directly in Double R itself (not in Moncler shares) as part of a broader partnership with Ruffini, with Double R's own Moncler stake set to rise toward a ceiling of about 18.5% over roughly 18 months from that deal — the 18.2% current figure sits right at the top of that planned range. This is genuine, direct founder control rather than a passive financial-sponsor stake, with LVMH's involvement adding a strategic-partner dimension rather than diluting Ruffini's influence.

What's Working

Moncler is the clear #1 in luxury outerwear with genuinely exceptional, stable operating economics: EBIT margin has held in a tight 28-30% band every year for five straight years, ROIC has stayed comfortably in the high-teens-to-20s the whole time, and gross margin has actually improved (+1.73pp) rather than eroded. The balance sheet is a clear strength — net cash in 4 of the last 5 fiscal year-ends, with no leverage risk to the equity story. Founder control is real and direct: chairman/CEO Remo Ruffini's Double R vehicle holds 18.2%, a stake LVMH has since bought into as a strategic partner rather than diluted. The stock trades at a genuine, if moderate, discount to its own trading history (18.56x vs a 22.97x FY22-25 average), and the Stone Island acquisition has matured into a real second growth engine rather than a stranded asset. Asia demand, often the pressure point for luxury peers this cycle, has actually been a bright spot for Moncler specifically (+13% cFX in H1 2026).

What to Watch

Growth has genuinely decelerated to the low-double-digits on all three CAGR measures (revenue +11.2%, EBIT +12.1%, FCF essentially flat at +0.2%) — real numbers, just short of this framework's 15% growth bar, and Q2 2026's slowdown to +5% cFX from Q1's +12% shows that deceleration is still playing out. The luxury outerwear category is genuinely fragmented beneath Moncler and Canada Goose, capex intensity has crept up to 7% of revenue as the directly-operated store network keeps expanding, and the forward PEG ratio (2.80x) suggests the market isn't pricing this as cheap relative to its now-slower growth rate even though it looks cheap relative to its own multi-year trading history. European tourist-flow softness is the specific swing factor to watch into H2 2026 — it, not Chinese demand, has been this name's actual pressure point this cycle.
