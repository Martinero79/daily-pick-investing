Rolls-Royce Holdings is a UK-listed maker of power systems built around three businesses: Civil Aerospace (large civil engines such as the Trent family, business-jet engines, and long-term service agreements that bill by flying hour), Defence (combat, transport and naval propulsion, including submarine nuclear work) and Power Systems (mtu engines and generators, increasingly for data-centre backup and prime power), plus a small-modular-reactor (SMR) development programme. After the 2020 pandemic collapse in flying hours, the group has been run since early 2023 under CEO Tufan Erginbilgic through a “transformation programme” of contract renegotiation, cost cutting and aftermarket focus. The numbers are striking: underlying operating profit rose from £414m in FY2021 to £3,462m in FY2025 and £2.5B in H1 2026 alone; the company reinstated a dividend in 2025 and announced a £7-9B buyback for 2026-28, and FY2026 guidance was raised in July to £4.7-4.9B underlying operating profit and £3.8-4.0B free cash flow. The share price has run just as far (about 120p at the end of 2021) but fell about 15% in October 2026; I could not verify a single cause. At roughly 34x trailing statutory earnings, the market is already paying for continued delivery.
Three distortions to keep in mind: (1) Depressed starting base — FY2021 was still a COVID-recovery year (underlying operating profit £414m, free cash flow −£1.5B), so the 4-year CAGRs of +70% (EBIT) are measured from a trough; FCF CAGR is not meaningful from a negative base, so a FY2022-25 proxy is used. (2) Statutory vs underlying — FY2025 statutory net income of £5.8B includes non-cash items such as a £277m UK deferred-tax credit and derivative fair-value gains; statutory EPS and the GAAP P/E history (85x in FY2021, a loss in FY2022) are therefore noisy. (3) Half-yearly reporting — there is no quarterly series; this dashboard uses half-years, and contract catch-ups and long-term service agreement (LTSA) accounting can move profit and cash between periods.

Half-Year Trend & Latest Results (H1 2026)
Rolls-Royce reports half-yearly, so seven half-years (H1 2023 to H1 2026) are shown instead of quarters. Reported July 30, 2026; the next scheduled report is the FY2026 result.
What the half actually showed

H1 2026 underlying revenue was £11,279m (+24.5% YoY) and underlying operating profit £2,534m (+46%), a 22.5% margin versus 19.1%. All three divisions improved: Civil Aerospace 25.3% margin (H1 2025: 24.9%), Defence 21.0% (15.4%) and Power Systems 20.3% (15.3%). Free cash flow was £1,964m (H1 2025: £1,582m) but grew less than profit because LTSA balance growth fell to £86m (from £472m) and net investment and cash tax rose. Net cash was £2,136m. Guidance was raised to £4.7-4.9B underlying operating profit and £3.8-4.0B FCF, which implies a second half of £2.2-2.4B, below the H1 figure of £2.5B (my arithmetic).

Share Count & Buybacks

Roughly flat to slightly down: about 8.3B shares around FY2021 (S&P-implied) versus 8.24B now, with a 0.7% decline in the last year. A £1.0B buyback was completed in 2025 (the first in about ten years) and £2.5B is planned for 2026, so the count should fall faster if the programme continues; employee share issuance partly offsets it.

Founders, insiders & honest caveats: Rolls-Royce has no founder or controlling holder; the register is almost entirely institutional. S&P shows insiders at 0.03%; I could not verify the size of any employee share plan, so none is credited. Named top holders are not asserted here because their percentages were not verified.
Balance sheet: net cash of £2,136m at Jun 30, 2026 (cash £6.5B, gross debt £2.7B, leases £1.7B; includes leases). Liquidity £9.0B. Book equity is small (about £2.9B), so P/B (35x) is not a useful measure. A bond of about £1.1B was repaid from cash in H1 2026 and €1B of new bonds maturing 2031 and 2036 were issued.

What's Working

The transformation shows in every line: underlying operating profit up from £414m to £3.5B in four years with margin up 13.5pp, gross margin up 8pp in three years, FCF conversion of about 95%, and a move from net debt to £2.1B net cash with upgraded credit ratings. All three divisions raised margin in H1 2026, FY2026 guidance was lifted in July, and shareholder returns (dividend plus a £7-9B buyback programme) have restarted. Aftermarket (57% of FY2025 revenue per S&P) provides recurring, flying-hour-linked income, and the engine industry has very high barriers to entry.

What to Watch

The shares are priced for delivery: trailing P/E of 34x is above the company's own (noisy) average, and the stock fell about 15% in October with no verified single cause. Growth is decelerating on a full-year basis (+12.4% in FY2025 vs +15.8%), the FY2026 guidance implies a weaker second half than H1, and LTSA balance growth slowed sharply in H1 2026. Margin of 17.3% is below this site's 20% bar. Results depend on flying hours, supply-chain constraints, contract catch-ups and the Middle East conflict the company flagged. Insiders hold almost nothing, and half-yearly reporting means fewer data points between results. Several inputs are estimates or third-party: WACC, gross margin, capex, ownership and the TTM figures.
