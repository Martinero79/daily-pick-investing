Mercedes-Benz Group AG is the Stuttgart-based maker of Mercedes-Benz, Mercedes-AMG and Mercedes-Maybach cars, premium vans (Sprinter, Vito), and a captive finance arm (Mercedes-Benz Financial Services, with a €128.8B contract portfolio at end-2025). Its history goes back to 1886 and, since the December 2021 spin-off of Daimler Truck, it is a pure-play premium cars and vans company. Between FY2021 and FY2022 it ran a deliberate “fewer cars, higher prices” strategy that lifted Cars adjusted return on sales to 14.6%, but that peak was helped by post-COVID supply shortages and very strong net pricing. Since then the picture has weakened sharply: Group EBIT fell from €20.5B (FY2022) to €5.8B (FY2025) as volumes in China collapsed against local competitors, tariffs and FX added costs, net pricing turned negative, and the company booked restructuring charges for its Next Level Performance program. Q2 2026 stabilised the earnings line (Group EBIT €1.55B, +21.5%; adjusted €2.3B) on cost cuts and a strong finance arm, but Cars adjusted return on sales was only 4.0%, Cars sales fell 7.9% (China −30%), and management lowered its 2026 sales and revenue outlook. The balance sheet is the strongest part of the case: the industrial business holds ~€30B of net cash, the dividend (€3.50) and a €2B buyback are covered by industrial FCF, and the share count is down ~14% since FY2021. At €40.20 the stock trades at ~7.6x trailing earnings, roughly 0.3x sales, close to its 52-week low.
Four distortions to keep in mind: (1) Daimler Truck spin-off — FY2021 is restated for continuing operations; the FY2021 base includes truck-business capex/R&D and a boom-time post-COVID luxury cycle. (2) Captive finance — consolidated debt (€100B+) and consolidated cash flow include the finance arm's lending book; this page uses industrial net liquidity and industrial FCF. (3) Reported vs adjusted EBIT — FY2025 Group EBIT of €5.8B includes ~€2.4B of restructuring/other charges (adjusted €8.2B); the scorecard uses reported Group EBIT and shows adjusted alongside. (4) Peak starting point — FY2021-22 were exceptional years for pricing, so CAGRs measured from FY2021 look worse than a mid-cycle comparison would.

What the quarter actually showed

Q2 2026 revenue was €32.1B (−3.3% YoY) and Group EBIT €1.55B (+21.5%; adjusted €2.3B vs €2.0B), but the improvement came from cost cuts (administrative expenses −14%, R&D −12%), a 70% jump in adjusted EBIT at Mercedes-Benz Financial Services (€492M) and a €131M gain on the planned sale of Athlon, not from the cars themselves. Mercedes-Benz Cars adjusted EBIT fell 26% to €909M (adjusted return on sales 4.0%, vs 5.1%); reported Cars EBIT was only €49M after €704M of non-cash impairments on China equity investments. Cars unit sales fell 7.9% to 417,765 (Europe +4%, U.S. +10%, China −30%; BEV sales +51%). Industrial free cash flow was €1.1B (vs €1.87B), and industrial net liquidity stayed at €30.4B after ~€5B was returned to shareholders in H1. Management cut its 2026 car sales and revenue outlook to slightly below 2025 on China.

Share Count & Buybacks

Shares outstanding fell from ~1,070M (end-2021) to ~922M now (−13.8%) via successive programmes (€4B from 2023, €3B in 2024, a €2B programme from Nov 2025, with up to €1.7B left for 2026, and a further up to €1B announced in September 2026). Policy: industrial free cash flow beyond the ~40% payout ratio funds buybacks. The dividend has been cut for two years (€5.30 → €4.30 → €3.50) as earnings fell.

Founders, insiders & honest caveats: there is no founder or controlling family; the Board of Management (Chairman Ola Källenius) holds a negligible share of the company and I could not verify exact management holdings. The three anchor holders above (BAIC, Li Shufu, Kuwait Investment Authority; ~26% combined) are strategic or sovereign investors rather than insiders, and the BAIC and Geely stakes are politically sensitive (see page 3). Because they are not insiders or founders they receive no credit under criterion 12; holder percentages are from the company's shareholder-structure page and public notifications and may differ slightly by date.
Balance sheet — industrial vs consolidated: the industrial business had net liquidity of €30.4B at Jun 30, 2026 (€32.2B at end-2025, €31.4B at end-2024), so it has more cash and securities than debt; the company targets a strong investment-grade rating (A). Consolidated debt is €102B and net debt ≈ €85B (S&P, Jun 2026), but that is dominated by the funding of the €129B captive-finance portfolio (leases and loans to customers), which is matched by receivables on the other side of the balance sheet. The scorecard's leverage criterion therefore uses industrial net liquidity and is a net-cash case; the consolidated figure (Debt/EBITDA ~8x on S&P) is not meaningful for an automaker with a captive bank.

What's Working

An unusually strong balance sheet for an automaker: ~€30B of industrial net cash, a stated A-rating ambition, and industrial FCF that exceeded Group EBIT in FY2025 (€5.4B vs €5.8B) on better inventory management. The company keeps returning cash — €3.50 dividend, €2B buyback and ~€5B returned in H1 2026 — and has cut shares outstanding ~14% since FY2021. Cost discipline is real: Next Level Performance saved over €3.5B at Cars in 2025, fixed costs are ~25% below 2019, and Q2 2026 admin and R&D spend fell 14% and 12%. The Financial Services arm is recovering (adjusted RoE 15.3% in Q2), Europe and U.S. Cars sales grew in Q2, BEV sales rose 51%, and the 40+ model launch programme (CLA, GLC, GLB, S-Class refresh) has order books filled into H2 2026, per the company. The stock trades at ~7.6x trailing earnings, ~0.3x sales and ~0.4x book.

What to Watch

Earnings are far below the FY2022 peak (Group EBIT −72% from FY2022; Cars adjusted return on sales 4.0% in Q2 2026 against an 8-10% medium-term ambition), and 2026 guidance was cut on China, where Cars sales fell 30% in Q2 and local premium rivals are taking share. Tariffs, FX and negative net pricing remain headwinds (Cars RoS 6.1% excluding tariffs in 2025). ROIC (3.7%) is well below any reasonable cost of capital. The dividend has been cut two years running and industrial FCF is guided slightly below 2025's €5.4B, with ~€1.1B of severance outflows in H1 2026 and further German restructuring (two plants reported under review). The two largest holders (BAIC, Geely's Li Shufu; ~20% combined) create U.S. political risk under the proposed connected-vehicle legislation, and there is no founder or insider anchor. A low P/E on trough earnings is only cheap if earnings recover; the industry also faces structural overcapacity and rising Chinese competition.
