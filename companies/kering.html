Kering is the French luxury conglomerate behind Gucci, Saint Laurent, Bottega Veneta, Balenciaga, Alexander McQueen, Boucheron and a fast-growing eyewear division, controlled since the 1990s by the Pinault family through their holding company Artémis. This dashboard is an honest look at a company in a genuine, severe, multi-year earnings downturn — not a distortion to be explained away. Group revenue peaked at €20.35B in FY2022 and has fallen for three straight years to €14.68B in FY2025, while operating margin has more than halved, from 28.43% (FY2021) to 11.11% (FY2025). Nearly all of this is a Gucci story: the brand alone generated over half of group revenue and the clear majority of group profit as recently as FY2021-22, and its revenue has since fallen from €10.49B (FY2022) to €5.99B (FY2025) amid a genuine brand repositioning under a new creative direction (Demna, formerly of Balenciaga, took over in 2025), persistent softness in Chinese demand, and a broader "affordable luxury fatigue" across the industry that has hit logo-driven, aspirational brands hardest. Notably, Kering's other houses have held up far better: Saint Laurent and Bottega Veneta both grew revenue from FY2021 to FY2025, and Kering Eyewear more than doubled — a real, if partial, offset most headlines miss. The balance sheet took on real leverage during 2023-24, partly funding an aggressive real-estate buying spree (flagship stores on prime retail streets), pushing net debt/EBITDA above 3.5x; two 2025-26 divestitures — selling Kering Beauté to L'Oréal and a stake in prime Paris real estate to Ardian — have since brought leverage back down toward the mid-2x range. A new operational CEO, Luca de Meo (previously CEO of Renault), was installed in 2026 to drive the turnaround, while Fçois-Henri Pinault remains Chairman and the family's Artémis vehicle remains the controlling shareholder at roughly 42.3% of capital and 59.8% of voting rights. H1 2026 showed the first tentative, real signs of stabilization — group recurring operating margin improved 40 basis points year-over-year to 12.8%, and Gucci's own margin rose a full point to 17.0% — but this is still very early, unproven, and priced by the market as a cautious "Hold," not a resolved turnaround.
The whole scorecard, in one sentence: almost every growth, margin, leverage and return metric on this dashboard is failing right now, honestly and by design — this is a real earnings trough, not an accounting artifact, and the criteria that ARE met (concentrated family ownership, a declining share count, and moderate normalized capex) say more about balance-sheet discipline than about the current profit cycle.
Not today's featured pick: this dashboard was built alongside Hermès (today's daily pick) as part of a small luxury-goods competitor batch. Kering is Hermès's opposite case in the same industry — useful for comparison, not a top-conviction position of mine right now.

Gucci, House Mix & the 2026 Turnaround
The group-level collapse is almost entirely a Gucci story. Kering's other houses have held up far better — some have grown since FY2021.
H1 2026: the first real signs of stabilization

Group recurring operating margin was 12.8% in H1 2026, up 40 basis points year-over-year, with management guiding H2 margins above that level as full-year operating expenses are now expected to decline rather than hold flat. Gucci itself posted recurring operating income of €468M on a 17.0% margin, up a full percentage point year-over-year — still far below its historical 30%+ margins, but a genuine, if early, inflection. The plan driving this: a €1B inventory reduction across the fashion & leather-goods houses by year-end 2026, 159 store closures since 2024 (with ~100 more planned in 2026), a creative reset at Gucci under new artistic director Demna (formerly of Balenciaga, appointed 2025), and new operational leadership — CEO Luca de Meo, previously CEO of Renault, was installed in 2026 specifically to execute this turnaround, while François-Henri Pinault remains Chairman and the Pinault family's Artémis holding company remains the controlling shareholder.

Deleveraging via divestiture, not just Gucci's recovery: Kering agreed to sell its Kering Beauté division to L'Oréal (announced Oct 2025, expected to close H1 2026) and, separately, sold a majority stake in several landmark Paris retail properties to Ardian (agreed Jan 2025, ≈€837m). Where the debt came from: capex spiked to ≈19.6% of revenue in FY2024 and ≈13.4% in FY2023 (vs. a normal ≈5-6% for this business) as Kering acquired flagship retail real estate on prime streets in Paris, Milan and elsewhere — a real, deliberate capital-allocation choice, not a recurring cost of doing business. Why P/FCF is misleading here: current P/FCF is just 3.97x vs. a FY21-25 average of ≈20.8x — superficially the deepest "discount" of any luxury name we cover. Don't trust it: TTM free cash flow (€6.9B) is inflated by one-off items — the group's active inventory-drawdown program and asset-sale-related cash movements — not a sudden, durable improvement in recurring cash generation. Forward P/E, which uses analysts' normalized next-12-month earnings estimate, is the more trustworthy read here, and it shows KER trading at a premium to its own history, not a discount. PEG ratio (≈0.63 on the current forward estimate) looks statistically cheap, but is similarly distorted by a low base-year comparison as Kering laps its own FY2025 trough.

What's Working

Kering's non-Gucci houses have held up far better than the headlines suggest — Saint Laurent and Bottega Veneta both grew revenue from FY2021 to FY2025, and Kering Eyewear more than doubled. Gross margin has stayed remarkably stable (74.68% → 72.64%, only −2.04pp) even as operating margin more than halved — this is an SG&A/operating-leverage and restructuring-cost collapse, not lost product pricing power. Real, deliberate deleveraging is underway: the Kering Beauté sale to L'Oréal and the Ardian Paris real-estate stake sale both target leverage directly, and net debt/EBITDA has already improved from 3.84x to 2.57x. Ownership is genuinely concentrated and durable — the Pinault family's Artémis holds ≈42.3% of capital and ≈59.8% of votes, multi-generational skin in the game, not a passive financial stake. New leadership (CEO Luca de Meo, Gucci creative director Demna) is fully in place with an explicit, numbers-backed plan, and H1 2026 showed the first real (if early) improvement: group margin +40bps YoY, Gucci margin +1pp YoY.

What to Watch

This is a real, severe, multi-year profit collapse, not a distortion to explain away: group operating income is down ≈67% and operating margin has more than halved (28.43%→11.11%) since FY2021, driven almost entirely by Gucci, whose revenue has fallen −38.4% over the same span amid a genuine brand repositioning and softer Chinese demand. ROIC has fallen from ≈19% to an estimated 4-5%, below a reasonable cost of capital — a value-destructive spread today. Leverage, even after recent asset sales, remains above this framework's 2x comfort threshold, a legacy of a real-estate buying spree that pushed capex as high as ≈19.6% of revenue in FY2024. Despite falling ≈68% from its 2021 peak, the stock is not obviously cheap against its own trading history once you correct for FCF distortion: forward P/E (27.35x) sits about 21% ABOVE Kering's own FY21-25 average. The turnaround (Demna's Gucci repositioning, CEO Luca de Meo's cost and inventory program) is real but unproven, and analyst consensus remains a cautious "Hold."
