BNP Paribas is France's largest bank and, with Crédit Agricole, one of the two largest in the euro area by assets, with three divisions — Corporate & Institutional Banking, Commercial, Personal Banking & Services, and Investment & Protection Services (insurance, asset management, wealth) — and roughly 180,000 employees. FY2025 net banking income was €51.2bn and net income group share €12.2bn (RoTE 11.6%, CET1 12.6%); in Q2 2026 net income group share reached €4.3bn, helped by an €858m one-off gain, and CET1 rose to 13.0%. The scorecard case rests on capital, efficiency gains and a high payout (FY25 dividend €5.16/share plus a €1.15bn buyback, share count down ~10% since FY2021), not on growth: S&P-standardised revenue compounded at only ~3.6% a year over FY2021–FY2025 and the net-income proxy at ~6.2%. Valuation is more neutral than at HSBC or Santander: the stock is down ~27% from its August high (€113.82) at €83.52, so trailing P/E (~7.2x) sits right at its own FY21–25 average (7.2x), while P/B (~0.68x) is about 19% above its average (0.57x).
Framing note — why this scorecard looks different for a bank: BNP Paribas SA is a bank, so the standard revenue/EBIT/FCF/net-debt framework does not map cleanly. The same substitutions used on the JPMorgan Chase page are applied here, all flagged inline: Total net revenue stands in for revenue (criterion 1); net income attributable to ordinary shareholders substitutes for both EBIT (criterion 2) and free cash flow (criterion 4) — a bank's cash flow statement is dominated by deposit and trading-book swings, so criteria 2 and 4 are necessarily correlated, a genuine limitation of the adaptation and not a workaround; RoTE vs. an assumed ~10–11% cost of equity substitutes for ROIC vs. WACC (criterion 7; 10.5% midpoint used for the points); CET1 ratio vs. the regulatory requirement substitutes for net debt/EBITDA (criterion 8; points banded by the CET1 cushion: 4pp+ = 5, 3–4pp = 4, 2–3pp = 3, 1–2pp = 2, 0–1pp = 1, below requirement = 0); (dividends + buybacks) ÷ net income substitutes for FCF conversion (criterion 9); criterion 10 averages the P/E and P/B ratios versus the bank's own FY21–FY25 year-end history; and criterion 14 uses the efficiency (cost/income) ratio because banks report no gross margin. Annual revenue and net income on this page are S&P-standardised (continuing operations, EUR) so five years sit on one basis; BNP's own reported figures (net banking income €51.2bn, net income group share €12.2bn in FY25) are quoted where they differ.

Market position, honestly stated: BNP Paribas is France's largest bank and, with Crédit Agricole, one of the two largest in the euro area by total assets. It also holds leading positions in Belgium, Luxembourg and Italy (BNL), a top-tier European corporate & institutional banking franchise, a large vehicle-leasing platform (Arval) and an insurance/asset-management arm that grew with AXA IM in 2025 — a “strong #2-tier” reading (4/5) on market share, so criterion #5 is Met. But no single bank dominates: French banking is shared by four or five large groups, Belgium and Italy have strong local rivals, and global wholesale banking is contested by US banks, so concentration is a moderate 3/5 and Not Met. As a G-SIB it carries additional capital buffers.

Reading the valuation: BNP is the one of the three banks whose entry multiple is not stretched against its own history on earnings: the stock has pulled back ~27% from its August high, putting trailing P/E (7.2x) at the FY21–FY25 average and forward P/E at ~6.7x. On book value it is less cheap — P/B of ~0.68x is about 19% above its ~0.57x average, because returns have risen (ROE 7.6% → 9.7%). Averaging the two lenses gives a ratio just above 1.0, so criterion 10 is Not Met, narrowly (2/5). Note that trailing earnings include the Q2 2026 one-off gain, so the “at average” P/E reading is flattered. Own FY21–FY25 year-end averages: P/E 7.2x, P/B 0.57x; current P/E 7.2x (1.00x of average) and P/B 0.68x (1.19x of average). Criterion 10 averages those two ratios (1.09).

Governance & Capital Notes

No controlling shareholder or founder. The largest holders are BlackRock (~7%) and the Belgian federal holding company SFPI (~5.7%); an official investor-relations page shows different shares on a voting-rights basis, so all percentages here are approximate. Employees own roughly 4% through the group savings plan — meaningful, but just short of the ~5% mark for a “notable employee plan”, so criterion 12 scores 1/5. Capital policy: a 60% payout (cash dividends of at least 50%, buybacks up to 10%) for 2025–26 earnings and a minimum of 60% from 2027; a €1.15bn buyback was finalised in Dec 2025, and distribution of surplus CET1 above 13% will be decided annually from 2027.

What's Working

Capital and operating discipline: CET1 13.0% against a 10.52% requirement, cost/income down ~6pp since FY21 to 61.2% (56.7% in Q2 2026, target <56% by 2028), and EPS growing ~9% a year, faster than revenue. Capital return is steady (60% payout, €1.15bn buyback), the share count is down ~10%, and the stock trades near its own earnings average after a ~27% pullback with a ~6% dividend yield.

What to Watch

Growth is the weak point (S&P revenue ~3.6%/yr, net income ~6.2%/yr), well under the 15% bar; Q2 2026 included an €858m one-off gain, so the 13.3% RoTE and +33% profit growth overstate the run-rate. P/B (~0.68x) is ~19% above its own average. The share price moved sharply (−27% from the August high) into Q3 results on Oct 28. French sovereign/political risk, the AXA IM and Arval/Athlon integrations, geopolitical provisions in the cost of risk, and the absence of insider or controlling ownership are further watch-items.
