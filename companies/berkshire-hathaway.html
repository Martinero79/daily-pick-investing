Berkshire Hathaway is not a growth stock and was never going to score well on a checklist built around 15% CAGRs. Revenue compounded at just 7.7%/yr over FY2021-2025 ($276.2B → $371.4B, and essentially flat the last two years), and operating earnings — Berkshire's own preferred non-GAAP measure, which strips out the noisy equity-portfolio marks — grew 12.8%/yr, close to but short of the bar. The honest case for Berkshire was never growth; it's the fortress balance sheet (a $344.1B net-cash pile against total debt of roughly $135B), a genuinely diversified cash-generation engine (insurance float, a top-2 U.S. railroad in BNSF, a large regulated utility in BHE, dozens of wholly-owned industrials), and a 30-year track record of compounding book value at a rate few large companies have matched. 2026 brought the most significant governance change in six decades: Greg Abel became CEO in January 2026, with Warren Buffett (now 95) staying on as Chairman. Abel has kept deploying the record cash pile — buying $23.5B of equities in Q2 2026 alone and resuming buybacks after Berkshire repurchased zero shares in all of 2025. On this framework's own terms, though, several structural features fail cleanly: consolidated operating margin (~12%) sits well under the 20% industrial bar because the mix includes low-margin retail/distribution and manufacturing businesses; the current "operating P/E" of roughly 24-25x is richer than Berkshire's own 5-year average (~22x); and BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy alone spend on the order of $14B/year in capex — a railroad and a national utility fleet are not asset-light businesses, no matter how capital-light the insurance float underneath them is.
Why "operating earnings," not net income: GAAP requires Berkshire to run unrealized gains and losses on its ~$300B+ equity portfolio (Apple, Amex, Coca-Cola, etc.) directly through net income every quarter, even though nothing was sold. That single accounting rule is why GAAP net income swung from $89.8B (2021) to a $22.8B loss (2022) to $96.2B (2023) with almost no change in the underlying businesses. Berkshire itself warns investors not to use these numbers to judge quarterly performance and instead discloses "operating earnings" (net earnings excluding investment/derivative gains-losses and impairments) as the meaningful trend line. This dashboard follows Berkshire's own convention and uses operating earnings as the EBIT-equivalent everywhere on this site's framework calls for one, exactly as the site substituted an insurer's Operating Result for NN Group and flagged Arm's IPO stock-comp charge as a one-off distortion.

What's Working

An unmatched balance sheet ($237.9B net cash, effectively zero net-debt risk), FCF conversion comfortably above the 80% bar, a genuinely durable top-2 position in U.S. rail via BNSF, a multi-year decline in share count, and modest but real structural margin improvement. Warren Buffett's Chairman-level alignment (30.4% voting power, never a seller) is about as strong as insider alignment gets anywhere, even if it no longer sits with the acting CEO.

What to Watch

This is a capital-preservation and steady-compounding story, not a growth story, and the checklist says so plainly: revenue and operating earnings both grew at roughly half the 15% bar or less, operating margin sits well under 20% on a blended basis, and the current operating P/E (~24.5x) is richer than Berkshire's own 5-year average. BNSF and BHE's combined ~$14B/year of capex means the consolidated entity is not asset-light despite the capital-light insurance float underneath it. The biggest open question is the Buffett-to-Abel transition itself: six months in, Abel has started deploying the record cash pile (buybacks resumed, first net equity purchases), but he does not yet carry anything close to Buffett's ownership-driven alignment.
