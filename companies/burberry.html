Burberry is Britain's best-known luxury fashion house, founded in 1856 and famous worldwide for its trench coats, scarves and signature check pattern. This is a genuine "fallen luxury name, is the turnaround real" story, not a smooth compounder. Under a prior CEO/creative-director pairing (Jonathan Akeroyd and Daniel Lee), Burberry attempted an "ultra-luxury" repositioning from 2023 — pushing price points sharply higher and away from its accessible-luxury heritage — that badly misjudged demand: comparable sales fell double digits, the dividend was suspended in July 2024, the company issued profit warnings, and roughly 1,700 roles (about 20% of the workforce) were cut in 2024-25 as part of a cost-savings plan. Joshua Schulman, formerly CEO of Michael Kors and head of Coach, took over in July 2024 and launched the "Burberry Forward" strategy in November 2024: a deliberate retreat from avant-garde ultra-luxury experimentation back toward timeless British heritage, re-centred on outerwear and scarves, with price architecture pulled back closer to the brand's historical positioning. The FY2026 results (year ended Mar 28, 2026) mark a genuine, if still early, inflection: comparable sales grew 2% for the full year, reversing a 12% decline in FY2025, accelerating to +3% in Q3 and +5% in Q4 with Greater China and the Americas each up 10% in the final quarter. Adjusted operating profit jumped to £160m from £26m a year earlier, and Q1 FY2027 (reported Jul 2026) showed comparable sales up a further 5%, with all product divisions returning to growth for the first time in three years. That said, revenue itself is still down year-on-year in reported terms, the operating margin (6.61%) remains a fraction of its pre-crisis level, the dividend has not been reinstated, and leverage (net debt/EBITDA 3.13x) remains above a comfortable level. This reads as real, credible progress on a multi-year repair job — not yet a name that has actually recovered.
The crisis, in plain terms: Burberry's FY2023 "ultra-luxury" repositioning under Akeroyd/Lee pushed prices up and moved away from the brand's approachable-luxury identity just as the broader post-pandemic luxury boom was fading and Chinese demand was softening — a self-inflicted wound layered on top of an industry-wide slowdown. Comparable sales fell as much as 21-22% in some quarters of FY2024-25, operating profit collapsed from £634m (FY2023) to just £26m (FY2025), and the dividend was suspended in July 2024 for the first time in years. Roughly 1,700 jobs (~20% of headcount) were cut as part of a £100m annual cost-savings plan announced in mid-2024.
2026 read: CEO Joshua Schulman called FY2026 "a meaningful inflection point." The "Burberry Forward" strategy — anchoring the brand back in heritage categories (outerwear, scarves), re-aligning price architecture in leather goods, and leaning into viral cultural moments (the brand's cashmere/silk scarves and a high-profile A$AP Rocky collaboration both drove Gen Z attention in 2026) — is showing up in the numbers: full-year comparable sales positive, Q1 FY2027 comparable sales +5% with every division growing. Full-year FY2027 guidance points to further revenue and margin expansion, though management flags "ongoing macroeconomic uncertainty." Activist-adjacent interest is rising too: Frasers Group (Mike Ashley's retail group) disclosed a stake built up to 4.16%, making it Burberry's third-largest shareholder as of July 2026.

The Turnaround: Recent Performance & Guidance
A genuine, broad-based comparable-sales recovery is visible across three consecutive periods; the question is how much of it survives past the easiest year-on-year comparisons.
What actually happened, and what's changed

From late 2022, Burberry pursued an "ultra-luxury" repositioning under then-CEO Jonathan Akeroyd and creative director Daniel Lee — sharply raising price points and leaning into avant-garde design, a strategy widely seen as misjudging both Burberry's brand equity and the timing of a softening global luxury cycle. Comparable sales fell as much as 21-22% in some FY2024-25 quarters, operating profit collapsed from £634m (FY2023) to £26m (FY2025), and the dividend was suspended in July 2024. Joshua Schulman (previously CEO of Michael Kors, and before that head of the Coach brand) took over as CEO in July 2024 and unveiled "Burberry Forward" in November 2024: refocus on heritage categories (outerwear, scarves), pull price architecture back toward historical levels particularly in leather goods, cut costs (≈1,700 roles, ~20% of headcount, and a £100m annualized savings target), and lean into cultural relevance with younger consumers (viral moments around the brand's cashmere/silk scarves and a high-profile A$AP Rocky collaboration in 2026).

Q1 FY2027 detail (reported Jul 17, 2026): Comparable retail sales +5% YoY, with double-digit growth in the Americas and Greater China and — for the first time in three years — all product divisions returning to growth simultaneously. Full-year guidance maintained for further revenue and margin expansion. Management flagged that Iran-related Middle East tensions weighed on European tourist spending in the quarter, a reminder that macro/geopolitical noise, not just execution, still moves the numbers quarter to quarter.
Governance watch: Frasers Group (Mike Ashley's retail conglomerate) disclosed a stake built up to 4.16% in July 2026, becoming Burberry's third-largest shareholder — a stake size that invites speculation about board influence or further building, though Frasers has made no public activist demands to date. Separately, a majority of investors approved a large increase to CEO Schulman's potential pay package at the July 2026 AGM, over the objection of a proxy advisory firm that had recommended a vote against it — a sign the board is willing to pay up to retain the executive credited with the turnaround, but also a governance friction point worth watching.

Valuation, Balance Sheet & Ownership
Trailing P/E is not usable here — FY2025 was a net loss and FY2026 earnings are still a razor-thin £21m, producing a meaningless 170-190x trailing multiple. This section leans on P/FCF as the more representative cross-year valuation read, with forward P/E discussed explicitly.
Price / Free Cash Flow — FY-End vs. Current
Current P/FCF (≈8.7-9.4x) sits meaningfully below Burberry's own FY22-26 average (≈11.6x) — a real discount to its own history on the one multiple that stays usable through both the healthy and the crisis years, since free cash flow stayed positive throughout.

Why P/E isn't usable here: trailing P/E sat in a normal 16-20x range in FY2022-24, then became meaningless once earnings collapsed — FY2025 was a net loss (no P/E computable) and FY2026's tiny £21m profit produces a distorted trailing P/E of roughly 170-190x depending on the exact date. Forward P/E (next-12-month consensus) currently sits around 26-29x, well above Burberry's clean FY2022-24 trailing-P/E average of ≈17.5x — the market is pricing meaningful further earnings recovery, which is a real bet, not a "cheap" signal. The PEG ratio (≈0.48 per stockanalysis.com, based on a 69.8% 3-year consensus EPS growth forecast off a very low base) looks superficially attractive but is distorted the same way — a huge percentage growth rate off a nearly-zero earnings base doesn't mean the stock is fundamentally cheap. P/FCF is used as the primary read instead because free cash flow stayed positive and comparatively stable throughout the crisis; on that basis, Burberry trades at a genuine discount to its own 5-year average.

What's Working

A credible new management team (CEO Joshua Schulman, ex-Coach and Michael Kors) has delivered three straight periods of improving, now positive, comparable sales after the FY2025 collapse, with Q1 FY2027 showing every product division growing simultaneously for the first time in three years. Free cash flow has stayed genuinely resilient throughout the crisis (FCF conversion clears 80% even using the cleaner pre-crisis years), capex has been cut to a genuinely low 2.98% of revenue, the share count is essentially flat, and the current P/FCF multiple sits roughly 19% below Burberry's own 5-year average — a real discount, not an accounting artifact. Frasers Group's rising 4.16% stake adds a wildcard governance angle that could accelerate change if the turnaround stalls.

What to Watch

Every growth and profitability metric on this scorecard is still failing outright: revenue, EBIT and FCF all show negative 4-year CAGRs, the FY2026 operating margin (6.61%) is barely a third of the pre-crisis level, ROIC (3.63%) still sits below even a modest WACC estimate (6.43%), and net debt/EBITDA (3.13x) remains above a comfortable band with the dividend still suspended two years after being cut. Burberry is also a genuinely smaller, weaker-margin player than every major listed luxury peer (LVMH, Kering, Richemont, Hermès), and gross margin still sits 2.63 percentage points below its FY2023 level despite the FY2026 recovery. The comparable-sales recovery, while real, is still being measured against an increasingly easy prior-year base — the next 12-18 months of results, against tougher comparisons, will be the real test of whether Burberry Forward is a genuine structural fix or a cyclical bounce.
