Cerebras priced the largest AI-chip IPO of 2026 on May 13 at $185/share (well above its marketed range), and the stock opened at $350 the next day — an 89% pop — before closing day one at $311.07 (+68.2%), then falling roughly 10% on day two. That volatility has continued: shares now sit around $210, well below the first-day close and roughly 13% above the IPO price, on a market cap near $50B. The underlying business is a genuine, if small, Nvidia challenger: the Wafer-Scale Engine is the largest chip ever built, TSMC's only System-on-Wafer customer, and Cerebras has real, multi-year product revenue predating the IPO — not a pre-revenue story. But that revenue has been extraordinarily concentrated: G42 (UAE) alone was 83-85% of revenue in 2023-2024, and even after diversifying, G42 plus Mohamed bin Zayed University of AI still made up roughly 86% of 2025 revenue between them. The 2026 growth story is now anchored by a different concentration risk — a $20B+, multi-year capacity agreement with OpenAI (750MW through 2028) that OpenAI itself partly financed with a $1B working-capital loan to Cerebras, a vendor-financing arrangement worth watching rather than taking at face value. Profitability is still a genuine problem: the FY2025 GAAP operating loss widened to roughly $145.9M (a headline net profit of $237.8M that year came almost entirely from a one-off $390.7M non-operating gain, not the core business), and the Q2 2026 GAAP net loss of $450.5M was driven substantially by IPO-related stock compensation. Against that, the balance sheet is now very strong (≈$8.6B cash and short-term investments, minimal debt) and the business is capital-light by design: Cerebras is fabless, outsourcing wafer fabrication entirely to TSMC. None of that changes the core issue for this framework: half the checklist requires 5 years of public trading and reporting history that a 4-month-old IPO simply doesn't have. What can be tested today is a mixed picture — real, fast-growing revenue and a strong balance sheet, against deep customer concentration, a widening GAAP operating loss, and a market where Nvidia remains dominant — and the Martinero Index of 54/100 should be read as provisional, exactly like SpaceX's.

Market Position: Wafer-Scale Chips vs. Nvidia
Market share and concentration are two of the few criteria fully testable without 5 years of history — both are about today's competitive landscape, not a multi-year track record.
The Wafer-Scale Engine (WSE)

Cerebras's core product is the Wafer-Scale Engine, the largest chip ever manufactured — an entire silicon wafer built as a single processor, rather than being cut into hundreds of individual chips the way conventional GPUs are. The pitch is that keeping computation and memory on one giant piece of silicon avoids the data-movement bottlenecks that limit GPU clusters, translating into materially faster AI inference in independent benchmarks — the basis of Cerebras's growing inference-cloud business and its 2026 partnership with OpenAI.

A Small Challenger, Not a Share Leader

Nvidia remains dominant in AI training and inference silicon, commonly estimated at somewhere in the 80-90%+ range of merchant AI-accelerator revenue. Cerebras is a genuine, well-funded, technically differentiated challenger — alongside AMD's Instinct GPUs, Google's in-house TPUs, Amazon's Trainium/Inferentia, Microsoft's Maia, and other startups like Groq and SambaNova — but by revenue share of the overall AI compute market, Cerebras remains small: FY2025 revenue of $510M compares to Nvidia's annual data-center revenue in the tens of billions. Criterion #5 (market share, bar: #1 or #2) is honestly scored Not Met on that basis; this is a challenger story, not a leadership one.

Balance Sheet, Capital Intensity, Valuation & Ownership
Criterion #10 (entry multiple vs. own history) and #11 (share count trend) are both N/A here — not because the data is unavailable, but because the comparison itself doesn't exist yet 4 months post-IPO. What follows is what actually can be assessed today.
The balance sheet is genuinely strong: post-IPO, Cerebras carries roughly $8.6B in cash, restricted cash and short-term investments, plus an undrawn $850M revolving credit facility, against minimal disclosed funded debt. That comfortably clears this framework's net-cash bar (criterion #8). The one wrinkle: FY2025 EBITDA is itself negative (the operating loss was roughly -$145.9M, and D&A alone likely doesn't flip that positive), so a clean Net Debt/EBITDA multiple isn't mathematically meaningful. This is scored Met on the strength of the overwhelming net-cash cushion relative to the company's cash burn, not on a precise ratio — the same practical judgment call this site made for SpaceX's ROIC criterion.
ROIC vs. WACC (#7) — Not met: a precise ROIC figure isn't cleanly computable from public disclosure given how much the invested-capital base just changed with the IPO raise. But a negative operating income means NOPAT is negative, so ROIC is negative regardless of the exact denominator — it fails both the framework's 15% absolute bar and a reasonable assumed WACC of roughly 11-12% (typical for a high-beta, recently-IPO'd semiconductor name) by a wide margin.
Capex intensity (#13) — Met: Cerebras is fabless — it designs the Wafer-Scale Engine but outsources fabrication entirely to TSMC, where it is currently TSMC's only System-on-Wafer (SoW) customer. That asset-light model means Cerebras doesn't carry the enormous fixed capex burden of a company that owns its own fabs (contrast Intel or Samsung), which is a genuine structural advantage for capital efficiency even while the company remains unprofitable at the operating line.
Valuation context (not scored): criterion #10 is N/A — there's no 5-year trading history to compare against, and GAAP earnings are still negative in most periods, so a meaningful trailing P/E doesn't exist either. For color only: at a ≈$50B market cap and roughly $41B enterprise value (netting out ≈$8.6B cash against minimal debt), against FY2026 core revenue guidance of $880-890M, Cerebras trades around 46-47x trailing/current-year revenue — a rich multiple typical of a hot, recently-IPO'd AI infrastructure name, not a statement about whether that multiple is justified.

What's Working

Genuine, differentiated technology (the Wafer-Scale Engine) with real, multi-year product revenue predating the IPO — not a pre-revenue story. Revenue nearly doubled again in FY2025 and continued accelerating into 2026 on the strength of the OpenAI deal. The balance sheet is now very strong (≈$8.6B cash, minimal debt), the business model is capital-light (fabless, TSMC-fabricated), and founder alignment is about as strong as it gets anywhere on this site.

What to Watch

Customer concentration remains extreme — roughly 86% of FY2025 revenue from two UAE-linked entities, and the new OpenAI relationship (partly financed by OpenAI itself) introduces a different kind of concentration and vendor-financing risk. The GAAP operating loss widened in FY2025 despite revenue growth, and FY2025's headline net profit was a one-off, not a trend. Nvidia remains dominant in the underlying market. And structurally: 7 of 14 criteria on this scorecard are N/A simply because Cerebras hasn't existed as a public company long enough to test them — this dashboard should be revisited as more quarters and years of public data accumulate.
