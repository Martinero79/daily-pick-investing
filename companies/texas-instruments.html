Texas Instruments is a Dallas-based maker of analog and embedded-processing chips, founded in 1930 and listed on Nasdaq. Analog (power management, signal chain) produces about four-fifths of sales and Embedded Processing (microcontrollers and processors) most of the rest; the products are sold to industrial, automotive, personal-electronics, enterprise and communications customers, mostly on mature process nodes with long product lives. Its distinctive strategy is owning its manufacturing: a multi-year build-out of 300mm (12-inch) wafer fabs in Texas and Utah, supported by up to $1.6B of US CHIPS Act grants plus investment tax credits, was meant to lower unit cost and secure supply for the next decade. The build was paid for in FY2023-25, when capex ran at $4.5-5.1B a year (26-29% of revenue) while the post-pandemic cycle cut revenue 22% from its FY2022 peak: FCF collapsed to $1.3-2.6B, dividends of roughly $5B a year were not covered by free cash flow, and net debt rose by about $11B over four years. The cycle has now turned: Q3 2026 revenue was $6.06B (+27.7% YoY), GAAP EPS $2.62 (+77%), capex fell to $463M and quarterly FCF reached $2.96B, with management guiding 2026 capex to $2-3B (ex-CHIPS) and saying it is on track for more than $8 of FCF per share in 2026. The stock is up roughly 77% over 12 months to $311.49 and trades at about 40x trailing GAAP earnings. The investment question is whether the capacity now in place earns the high incremental margins that the Q3 print suggests, or whether the stock already prices that in.
Three things to keep in mind: (1) The cycle — FY2021-22 revenue and 50%+ margins were a pandemic-era peak; a 5-year CAGR measured from there looks poor partly because of the starting point. (2) Two FCF definitions — TI's own FCF includes CHIPS Act proceeds (about $1.1B TTM); this page's scorecard uses operating cash flow minus capex, without them. (3) Capex is a choice and a commitment — the 2026 range of $2-3B is demand-modulated and does not include CHIPS benefits; a 2027 framework is expected at the February 2027 capital management call (no 2027 figure confirmed). If demand weakens, depreciation on the new fabs continues regardless.

What actually moved the stock

Q3 2026 revenue was $6,055m (+27.7% YoY, +10.8% sequentially), with Analog +31.7% and Embedded Processing +21.0%. Gross margin rose to 64.2% (57.4% a year earlier) and operating profit to about $2.87B (47.5% margin), so profit grew far faster than sales as the new fabs absorbed volume. EPS was $2.62 (+77%; a one-cent tax item was not in guidance). Cash did the rest: operating cash flow was $3.4B, capex only $463M (down 61% YoY), and quarterly FCF $2.96B. Q4 guidance is revenue of $5.80-6.30B and EPS of $2.45-2.79, i.e. roughly flat to up at the midpoint. After the print the stock swung between $277 and $313 and closed Nov 4 at $311.49.

What's Working

The franchise metrics are elite: #1 in analog, a 34.6% GAAP operating margin at the cycle low (41% TTM, 47.5% in Q3 2026) and ROIC of ~30% against ~11% WACC. The capex peak appears to be behind it: quarterly capex fell from $1.3B to $463M while revenue grew 28% YoY, and quarterly FCF reached $2.96B in Q3. Net debt is already down to 0.5x EBITDA, the dividend has risen for 22 years, and management guides to more than $8 of FCF per share in 2026. Gross margin has rebounded from 55.9% to 64.2% in three quarters, and revenue growth has accelerated for three consecutive quarters. Owning its 300mm capacity gives TI supply control in a market where customers value it, and CHIPS Act money partly defrays the cost.

What to Watch

The five-year numbers are weak: revenue, EBIT and FCF are all below FY2021, FCF covered only 18-56% of EBIT in FY2023-25, and ~$5B a year of dividends outran free cash flow, adding about $11B of net debt. At about 40x trailing GAAP earnings (27x forward) the stock trades well above its own average, so the recovery is largely priced in; a demand relapse would hit a heavy fixed-cost base. Depreciation on the new fabs persists whatever the volume, and the 2027 capex plan is not yet disclosed. Analog demand is cyclical (industrial and automotive end markets) and exposed to tariffs, China competition on mature nodes and policy changes to CHIPS incentives. Insider ownership is negligible. The CHIPS-inclusive FCF TI headlines is not the same as the operating-cash-minus-capex figure used here. Several inputs are third-party: market share, ROIC/WACC, forward P/E and year-end prices.
