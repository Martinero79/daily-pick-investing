Intel is the most dramatic risk/reward story in this entire site's coverage right now, and the tension is right there in the numbers: the stock has nearly quadrupled over the past year, while this framework's scorecard is one of the weakest anywhere on the site. That's not a contradiction so much as two different questions being answered separately. The market is pricing in a specific, high-profile turnaround narrative — new CEO Lip-Bu Tan restructured the company and cut 15,000 jobs after replacing Pat Gelsinger in March 2025, the U.S. government converted CHIPS Act support into a 10% equity stake (plus a warrant for another 5% designed to keep Intel's foundry under American ownership), Nvidia bought a $5B stake and struck a co-development deal, and Intel's 18A process node finally reached high-volume manufacturing in January 2026 with yields improving roughly 7% a month. Whether that story pays off is genuinely unresolved. What the actual financials say today: revenue is still nearly 28% below its FY2021 peak, operating margin collapsed from 27.94% to low single digits and has only partially recovered, ROIC sits at 3.42% against a 15.14% cost of capital (meaning Intel is currently destroying, not creating, economic value on this measure), free cash flow swung from a $9.1B positive in FY2021 to deeply negative in FY2022-2025, and funding the recovery has meant diluting existing shareholders by roughly a quarter over five years. Q2 2026 does show real, tangible operating improvement (12.19% margin, the best quarter in years) — but one strong quarter against years of decline is not yet a trend this framework can confidently call.

What makes Intel's ownership genuinely unusual right now is the arrival of two large, non-traditional strategic holders in 2025-26: the U.S. government converted CHIPS Act support into a roughly 10% equity stake, plus a five-year warrant for another 5% (exercisable only if Intel sells majority control of its foundry business — a poison pill designed to keep leading-edge domestic chip manufacturing under American ownership), and Nvidia separately invested $5B for roughly 4-5% of the company as part of a broader x86/AI co-development partnership. Neither of these is a "founder" in the traditional sense this criterion usually rewards, so it's scored as Not Met — but they represent a real, unusual form of strategic alignment worth watching regardless.

What's Working

Q2 2026 shows real, tangible operating improvement (12.19% margin, the best quarter in years, on accelerating revenue growth). The net debt position is shrinking, 18A finally reached high-volume manufacturing with improving yields, and Intel now has two extraordinarily well-resourced strategic partners — the U.S. government and Nvidia — both financially and politically invested in the company's success.

What to Watch

This scorecard is genuinely one of the weakest on the entire site: negative capital returns, heavy and rising dilution, extreme capex intensity, and a valuation multiple that's arithmetically almost meaningless given how thin current FCF still is. The stock's 260% one-year rally is priced substantially on turnaround hope, government/Nvidia backing, and Apple foundry talks — not on demonstrated financial performance. One strong quarter is a genuinely encouraging data point, not yet a trend.
