Spotify is today's "fun" pick — a platform I actually use every day — not a name pre-screened for a strong score, so I want to be upfront about what the checklist actually says. And honestly, it says more than I expected: 11 of 14 criteria met, Martinero Index 86/100, one of the stronger scores on this site. But the two headline growth numbers on page 2 (EBIT CAGR, FCF CAGR) are seriously distorted: Spotify was barely breakeven in FY2021 (0.97% operating margin) and posted real operating losses in FY2022 (-5.62% margin) and FY2023 (-1.50% margin) before swinging to genuine, structural profitability in FY2024-25. Computing a compound annual growth rate from a near-zero or negative starting point produces a huge, almost meaningless percentage — the real story isn't "120% annual EBIT growth," it's a company that crossed from unprofitable to solidly profitable in the last two years. What's genuinely strong, not distorted: revenue growth has been real and consistent (≈15.5% CAGR, never negative), free cash flow conversion is now excellent (130.7% of EBIT in FY2025), the balance sheet is net cash with essentially no debt, capex is close to nothing (0.35% of revenue — about as asset-light as platforms get), and Spotify is the unambiguous #1 in global music streaming by both subscribers and revenue. The valuation looks cheap relative to Spotify's own (very short) history as a profitable company, but that "own history" is really just two data points (FY2024, FY2025), so I'd treat that reading with real caution too. Net: a likeable business with a real, recent profitability inflection — not the "priced-for-perfection growth story" its 28x trailing P/E might suggest at first glance, but not a screaming bargain either given how thin the profitable track record still is.

Read this before trusting the EBIT/FCF growth numbers: a compound annual growth rate is only a meaningful summary of a trend when the underlying series grows roughly smoothly. Spotify's does not. FY2021's €94M operating income was already a razor-thin, near-breakeven result (0.97% margin) — and it got worse from there before it got better: FY2022 was a real operating loss (-€659M, -5.62% margin), FY2023 was a smaller loss (-€199M, -1.50% margin), and only FY2024-25 delivered genuine, structural profitability (8.80% → 12.79% margin). Dividing a healthy endpoint (€2,198M) by a near-zero starting point (€94M) produces a dramatic-looking +119.9% "growth rate" that has nothing to do with steady compounding — it's a base-effect artifact, the same category of distortion this site flags for COVID-depressed base years elsewhere (e.g. Booking Holdings, Airbus). The real signal is the margin trajectory itself, not the CAGR percentage.

Quarterly Development & Market Position
Spotify reports calendar quarters. Latest reported period: Q2 2026 (three months to Jun 30, 2026, reported Aug 2026).
Q2 2026 Headlines

Revenue reached €4.78B, up 14% YoY, in line with guidance. Premium subscribers crossed a milestone, hitting 300 million (+7M in the quarter, ahead of guidance), while total monthly active users reached 777 million. Gross margin hit a record 33.4%, operating income was €655M, and free cash flow was €797M. For Q3 2026, Spotify guided to €5.0B revenue, €670M operating income, 32.9% gross margin, 305M Premium subscribers and 788M MAU — a slightly softer MAU add than some expected, which the company attributed partly to product changes in emerging markets like India and Indonesia aimed at longer-term monetization rather than raw user-count growth. In August 2026, Spotify also upsized its share repurchase program by $1.5B, its clearest signal yet that buybacks (not just stock-based comp dilution) are becoming part of the capital-return story going forward.

Gross Margin Trend (FY2021-FY2025)
Gross margin has improved every year in this window, from 26.80% to 31.98% — a +7.03pp gain versus FY2022 alone (the window used for criterion #14), driven by a shift toward higher-margin marketplace revenue (advertising, podcasts, audiobooks) and better content-licensing terms, not the kind of margin erosion seen at some capex-heavy names elsewhere on this site.
Market position, honestly stated: Spotify is the clear global #1 in music streaming by every widely-cited measure — 300 million paid subscribers, 777 million monthly active users, and roughly 31-32% of global paid music-streaming subscriptions, well ahead of Tencent Music (≈14%, China-concentrated), Apple Music (≈12-13%), YouTube Music (≈12%) and Amazon Music (≈8-9%). That said, this isn't a market with weak competitors: Apple, Amazon, Google/YouTube and Tencent are all trillion-dollar (or near-trillion) companies that can and do bundle music into broader ecosystems (Apple One, Prime, YouTube Premium) as a retention tool rather than a standalone profit center — a structurally different, harder-to-out-compete kind of rival than Spotify itself, which has no other business line to cross-subsidize with. Scored here as "Clear #1" given the subscriber and revenue evidence, with market concentration (criterion #6, separate from market share) scored more cautiously at 3/5 given how many large, well-funded platforms are genuinely competing for the same listeners.

Trailing & Forward P/E — FY21-25 & Current
Trailing P/E is only computable for two of the last five fiscal years (FY2024-25) — FY2021-23 had net losses, so there's no meaningful P/E to plot. The "5-yr average" (≈61.4x) this framework uses is really just an average of those two years, both unusually high because earnings were still small relative to the price. Current trailing P/E (28.01x) sits well below even that limited average — genuine multiple compression as earnings caught up, but treat the "discount" with some caution given how thin the comparable history is.
Trailing P/E (FY24-25, Now) Forward P/E (FY23-25, Now)
Reading the three P/E numbers, and the PEG ratio: current P/E is today's share price divided by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings (28.01x). Trailing P/E (the FY21-25 series above) is that same ratio computed at each fiscal year-end — only meaningful for FY2024-25 here, since Spotify posted net losses in FY2021-23. Forward P/E is today's price divided by analysts' consensus estimate of the next 12 months of earnings (32.44x currently) — useful for judging whether a multiple is fair relative to where earnings are actually headed, though note forward P/E is currently higher than trailing P/E, meaning the market expects near-term earnings growth to moderate somewhat from its recent explosive pace. The PEG ratio (forward P/E ÷ expected annual EPS growth) sits at 1.23 per stockanalysis.com — squarely inside the 1.0-2.0 "reasonably priced growth" range this dashboard checks for, not screaming cheap (like Booking Holdings' sub-1.0 PEG elsewhere on this site) but not expensive either, given how fast earnings have actually been growing off the recent turnaround.
Balance sheet: net cash of €6.52B (current) against essentially minimal total debt (€466M) — Net Debt/EBITDA is negative (net-cash position), comfortably inside this framework's <2x bar under any methodology. FY2022-23 show mathematically noisy Net Debt/EBITDA readings (3.30x and 53.95x per some data providers) purely because EBITDA itself was near zero those years, not because leverage changed — the underlying debt load barely moved (€1.7-1.8B throughout). Free cash flow conversion is now strong and clean: 130.7% of EBIT in FY2025, 165.6% in FY2024. Capex is minimal at €61M (FY2025, just 0.35% of revenue) — a genuinely asset-light platform with almost no fixed-capital requirements, consistent with running data centers and content delivery mostly through third-party cloud infrastructure (notably Google Cloud) rather than owned hardware.

Governance note: economic ownership (≈25% combined for Ek and Lorentzon) understates how much control the founders actually have. Spotify's dual-class structure gives founders "beneficiary certificates" carrying 10 votes each versus 1 vote per ordinary share — pushing Ek's individual voting power to roughly 29-31% and Lorentzon's to roughly 42%, for a combined ≈70-73% of total voting power despite owning only about a quarter of the economic value. That's a meaningfully different governance picture than the ~25% economic figure alone suggests: Ek and Lorentzon retain effective control over major corporate decisions even as their pure economic stake has been diluted by years of stock-based comp and secondary sales (both founders have sold hundreds of millions of dollars of stock over time). Scored on the economic stake for consistency with how this criterion is applied elsewhere on the site, with the voting-control nuance flagged here for honesty.

What's Working

The turnaround from unprofitable to genuinely profitable is real: gross margin has climbed every year (26.80% → 31.98%), operating margin flipped from -5.62% to +12.79% in three years, and free cash flow conversion is now excellent (130.7% of EBIT). The balance sheet is net cash with essentially no meaningful leverage, capex is close to nothing (0.35% of revenue), and Spotify is the unambiguous global #1 in music streaming by subscribers, MAU and revenue. Founders retain real, meaningful control (≈70-73% of votes) despite a diluted economic stake, and the current trailing P/E (28.01x) is well below Spotify's own (short) history as a profitable company.

What to Watch

The headline EBIT and FCF growth rates (+119.9%, +79.6%) are base-effect distortions from a near-zero/negative FY2021-23 starting point — don't read them as smooth compounding. Operating margin (12.79%) is still well short of the 20% bar this framework screens for. Share count has risen roughly 11-12% since FY2021 from stock-based comp, though the new $1.5B buyback upsize (Aug 2026) may start to offset that. And despite being the clear leader, Spotify faces genuinely well-funded competition from Apple, Amazon, Google/YouTube and Tencent, all of which can subsidize music streaming as a loss-leader for a bigger ecosystem in a way Spotify itself cannot.

Maarten's Note

I use Spotify every day — it's the soundtrack to basically everything I do, from commuting to working to falling asleep to a playlist. So when I say this is a "fun" pick, I mean it: I picked this company because I love the product, not because I screened for a strong score first. What actually surprised me running the numbers is that the checklist likes it more than I expected — 86/100, 11 of 14 criteria met. But I don't want to oversell that. The two scariest-looking numbers on this dashboard (+120% EBIT CAGR, +80% FCF CAGR) are basically noise: Spotify was losing money or barely breaking even for most of the last five years, and those huge percentages are just what happens when you divide a real number by a number that used to be near zero. The actual story is simpler and, I think, more honest: Spotify spent years proving the subscription model could work at scale, and only in the last two years has it actually shown it can make real money doing it — healthy margins, strong free cash flow, a clean net-cash balance sheet. That's a good story. It's just a much shorter track record of actual profitability than the headline growth rates make it look like, and I'd rather be straight about that than let a two-year hot streak convince me this is a proven compounder yet. Daniel Ek and Martin Lorentzon still controlling roughly 70% of the vote gives me some comfort that the founders have skin in the game for the long haul, even though their economic ownership has been diluted down to about a quarter of the company. I'm not making a buy call here — I just like using the product, and it turns out the numbers aren't embarrassing either, which isn't always true when I pick a "fun" one.
