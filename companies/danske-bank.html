Danske Bank is Denmark's largest bank and one of the larger lenders in the Nordics, with personal, business and large-corporate franchises in Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland and Northern Ireland plus the Danica life insurer. FY2025 total income was DKK 56.5bn (-0.7% YoY) and net profit DKK 23.0bn, a 13.3% return on equity, with a CET1 ratio of 17.3% and a cost/income ratio of 45.5%. The honest read on growth: total income compounded at only ~4.5% a year over FY2021–FY2025, and the ~16.6% net-income CAGR that clears the growth bar is flattered by a depressed FY2021 base (ROE 7.6%) and a FY2022 net loss from the roughly DKK 15bn Estonia money-laundering provision — it is a recovery and rate-cycle story more than steady compounding. Capital return is the strength: FY2025 distributions of DKK 22.72 per share (including a DKK 5.78 extraordinary component) plus a DKK 4.5bn buyback are about 100% of net profit. The catch is price: the shares have roughly tripled since end-2021 (DKK 113 → DKK 344.50) while earnings roughly doubled, so trailing P/E has re-rated from ~7–8x to ~11.9x and P/B from ~0.6x to ~1.7x — both well above the bank's own five-year range.
Framing note — why this scorecard looks different for a bank: Danske Bank is a bank, so the standard revenue/EBIT/FCF/net-debt framework does not map cleanly onto it. The same substitutions used on the JPMorgan Chase page are applied here, all flagged inline: Total Income stands in for revenue (criterion 1); Net Income substitutes for both EBIT (criterion 2) and Free Cash Flow (criterion 4) — a bank's cash flow statement is dominated by deposit and trading-book swings that do not map to a “free cash flow” concept, so criteria 2 and 4 are necessarily identical here, a genuine limitation of the adaptation and not a workaround; ROE vs. an assumed ~10% cost of equity substitutes for ROIC vs. WACC (criterion 7; Danske reports ROE, not ROTCE; a ~10% cost of equity is used, the low end of the 10–11% range, given the stock's low beta of ~0.6); CET1 ratio vs. the regulatory requirement substitutes for net debt/EBITDA (criterion 8), scored on the cushion over the requirement; and (dividends + buybacks) ÷ net income substitutes for FCF conversion (criterion 9). Entry multiple (criterion 10) uses P/E as the scored measure with P/B shown alongside, each against its own FY2021–FY2025 history. Criterion 14 uses the efficiency (cost/income) ratio because banks report no gross profit; a falling ratio counts as a margin rise. Everything is reported in DKK and nothing is converted.

What the latest quarter showed (Q2 2026)

Q2 2026 total income was DKK 14.87bn (+8.0% YoY on the S&P Global series) and net profit DKK 6.20bn (+13.7% YoY, vs. DKK 5.45bn in Q2 2025), a return on equity of about 14.8% per the results-call summary. Broad-based income growth, fee income and cost discipline drove the beat, and management raised its 2026 net-profit outlook to DKK 23–25bn. Q1 2026 had been DKK 14.0bn of income and DKK 5.69bn of net profit. A third-party summary notes capital requirements are being lowered by roughly 40bp after a supervisory review of resolved legacy cases (to be reflected in Q3 disclosure; not independently verified).

ROE Trend — bank analog to ROIC
Return on average shareholders' equity, full year, vs. an assumed ~10% cost of equity (not drawn). FY21 (7.6%) and FY22 (-3.1%) were below the hurdle; FY23–FY25 sit 2.7–3.4pp above it. Danske does not report ROTCE.
Market position, honestly stated: Danske is the largest bank in Denmark by assets and a top-tier Nordic lender, which supports criterion #5. But Denmark is a concentrated market with credible competitors — Nordea, Nykredit (the dominant mortgage lender), Jyske and Sydbank — and Danske competes in Sweden, Norway and Finland against larger local incumbents. That makes the market a moderately concentrated oligopoly rather than a monopoly or duopoly, so concentration is scored 3/5 (Not Met on the Met/Not Met convention, 2 points on the graduated scale). Danske's recent history also matters: the Estonia money-laundering case led to a US settlement in December 2022, and supervisors had imposed capital add-ons; it is described here as a historical risk event, not a current allegation.

Governance & Capital Notes

No founder or controlling shareholder, but A.P. Møller Holding (the investment vehicle linked to the Møller family and A.P. Møller Foundation) is a long-term anchor holder at roughly 20%. Under the stake bands (15–30% = 4 points) this counts as a large aligned holder; it is not an insider or founder in the strict sense, so this is the most judgment-heavy score on the page — scoring it 0 instead would lower the index by ~4 points. Management ownership is negligible. Dividend policy targets a 60–70% ordinary payout with extraordinary distributions on top when capital allows.

What's Working

A top-tier Nordic franchise with a 13.3% ROE, 45.5% cost/income ratio and 17.3% CET1 ratio, comfortably above a ~10% cost-of-equity hurdle and a ~15% estimated capital requirement. Net profit has been steady at DKK 23–24bn for three years and management has just raised 2026 guidance to DKK 23–25bn. Capital return is generous: ordinary payout 60–70% plus extraordinary distributions and a DKK 4.5bn buyback, roughly 100% of FY25 profit. Capex is negligible and the share count is down ~4% since FY21.

What to Watch

Revenue growth is weak (~4.5% CAGR; FY25 income down 0.7%) and the headline net-income CAGR is flattered by a depressed FY21 base and a FY22 loss. The valuation has re-rated sharply: ~11.9x P/E and ~1.7x book versus ~8.4x and ~0.9x on average, so much of the improvement is in the price (the stock is ~3x its end-2021 level). Net interest income depends on the rate path (analysts cite margin pressure risk), the market is concentrated but contested, and the legacy Estonia case, though settled, shaped capital requirements. The CET1 requirement figure and the 2025 year-end share price used here are estimates.
