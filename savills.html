Savills is a real, long-standing global real estate services firm — 170 years old, genuinely international, with a UK/Asia-Pacific footprint few Western-headquartered peers can match — but this framework's numbers tell a more mixed story than the "Big 5" label usually suggests. Revenue has grown every year except FY2023 (+4.4% CAGR FY21-25), but EBIT and free cash flow have both shrunk over that same span (EBIT: £197.8M → £117.6M, a -12.2% CAGR; FCF: £284.1M → £144.5M, a -15.6% CAGR), driven by a genuinely rough FY2023 in which operating margin bottomed at 2.77% and free cash flow collapsed to essentially zero (£1.4M) amid a global slowdown in property transaction volumes. FY2025 shows real recovery — operating margin back to 4.61%, FCF back to £144.5M, FCF conversion above 100% of EBIT — but neither EBIT nor FCF has yet reclaimed its FY2021 level. What's genuinely working: the balance sheet is conservative by this industry's standards (net debt/EBITDA of roughly 0.49x even before counting the company's own net-cash framing), capex is minimal (under 0.5% of revenue), the entry multiple sits close to Savills' own five-year average rather than at a premium, and share count has actually declined modestly rather than diluting. What's more mixed: ROIC (7.64%) sits almost exactly at this framework's assumed cost of capital rather than clearing it, insider/founder ownership is thin (Savills demutualized via IPO in 1997 and has no controlling family stake), and Savills' claim to "Big 5" status doesn't fully hold up against Colliers, let alone CBRE and JLL, once you compare actual group revenue rather than reputation. The 31 Jul 2026 completion of the Eastdil Secured acquisition (US real estate investment banking, $1.1B enterprise value) is a genuinely significant forward-looking development — H1 2026 underlying profit was already up 47% — but it postdates the FY2021-25 window this scorecard is built on, so its full effect isn't in these numbers yet. Net: 5 of 14 criteria met, Martinero Index 35/100 — a financially conservative, geographically differentiated firm whose recent profit trend has been more volatile than its brand recognition would suggest.

Built this one as part of a batch covering the "Big 5" commercial real estate names alongside Colliers, CBRE, JLL and Cushman & Wakefield — and Savills is the one where the framework pushed back hardest on the label itself. It's genuinely everywhere in UK property conversations (probably the most recognisable name on a London high street of any firm on this list), but by the numbers it's smaller than Colliers, let alone the two giants. What actually impressed me digging in: the balance sheet discipline is real and doesn't get flagged nearly as much as the flashier growth stories on this site, and a management team that keeps capex near-zero while funding an genuinely large acquisition (Eastdil Secured) out of a conservative balance sheet is doing something right operationally, even if EBIT has been going backwards on a 5-year view. I'd want to understand FY2023's near-total FCF wipeout better before forming any view — was that purely cyclical (rates, transaction volumes) or did something structural change — before this checklist result would change my mind either way.

Trailing & Forward P/E — FY22-25 & Current
Current trailing P/E of 19.80x sits close to (a shade below) the FY22-25 average of roughly 22.94x — not a deep discount, but not a premium either. That average is pulled up by FY2023's 33.65x and FY2024's 27.85x, both set against depressed earnings during the FY2023 profit trough, so part of the "average" reflects a smaller earnings base rather than a genuinely rich market multiple in those years. Forward P/E (11.35x, current) is meaningfully below the trailing multiple, implying the market expects a real earnings recovery — plausible given H1 2026's 47% underlying profit growth, though that growth also reflects one-off deal effects (Eastdil Secured) rather than pure organic improvement.
Trailing P/E Forward P/E (current only)
Reading the three P/E numbers: current P/E is today's share price divided by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings — a backward-looking snapshot at today's price. Trailing P/E (the FY22-25 series above) is that same backward-looking ratio calculated at each fiscal year-end, showing how the market has re-rated the stock over time relative to what it had already earned. Forward P/E is today's share price divided by analysts' consensus estimate of the next 12 months of earnings — forward-looking, and generally the more useful of the two for judging whether a stock is priced fairly relative to where its earnings are headed. For Savills specifically: trailing P/E (19.80x) sits just under its own FY22-25 average (22.94x), and forward P/E (11.35x) is dramatically lower still, implying the market expects a real near-term earnings recovery — consistent with H1 2026's 47% underlying profit growth, though some of that growth is deal-driven (Eastdil Secured) rather than purely organic. One more lens worth a glance: the PEG ratio, forward P/E divided by expected annual EPS growth, which sanity-checks whether a "cheap" multiple is actually cheap once growth is accounted for. Savills' current PEG works out to roughly 0.77 per stockanalysis.com — comfortably under the traditional 1.0 "fair value" line, suggesting the stock isn't just cheaper than its own recent history, it's also reasonably priced relative to expected growth, assuming that growth (including the Eastdil integration) actually shows up.
Balance sheet: per stockanalysis.com, net debt/EBITDA was roughly 0.49x at FY2025 year-end (includes IFRS16 lease liabilities, which make up the bulk of reported "debt") and 0.49x on the latest available basis too — comfortably inside this framework's <2x bar either way. Savills' own headline balance-sheet metric, which excludes lease liabilities, was a net cash position of £167.7M at FY2025 year-end; both figures are shown here because they tell a genuinely consistent story (a conservative, low-leverage balance sheet) even though the exact number depends on whether lease liabilities are counted as debt. ROIC of 7.64% (current) / 7.89% (FY2025) sits almost exactly at this framework's assumed 8.0% WACC — a genuine miss under the framework's rules, but a much closer one than most companies that fail this criterion on this site. (Note: a separate stockanalysis.com statistics snapshot showed ROIC of 4.53% using a different calculation methodology; this dashboard uses the ratios-page figures above for internal consistency and flags the discrepancy for transparency.) Share count has declined modestly, from 139M (FY2021, basic) to 136M (FY2025, basic), a -2.2% change — real, if mild, shareholder-friendly behavior rather than dilution.

What's Working

The balance sheet is genuinely conservative — net debt/EBITDA around 0.5x even on the more debt-inclusive measure, and a real net cash position on Savills' own reporting basis. Capex is minimal, FCF conversion in FY2025 was strong, the current entry multiple sits at a modest discount to Savills' own five-year average, and share count has quietly declined rather than diluted. ROIC, while technically missing this framework's assumed cost of capital, is close enough that a small change in assumptions would flip it.

What to Watch

EBIT and free cash flow are both lower today than in FY2021 — not just growing slower, actually smaller — and FY2023 showed how exposed Savills' transaction-heavy segments are to a weak property cycle (FCF fell to near-zero that year). Insider/founder alignment is thin, and the "Big 5" market-share framing doesn't hold up cleanly against Colliers, let alone CBRE and JLL, once actual group revenue is compared. The Eastdil Secured acquisition (completed 31 Jul 2026) is a real, large bet on US capital-markets advisory — H1 2026 results already look much stronger, but that's outside the scored FY21-25 window and integration risk on a deal this size shouldn't be waved away.
