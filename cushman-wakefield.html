Cushman & Wakefield is a genuine top-3 global commercial real estate services firm, and there is a real recovery story underway: revenue grew 8.91% in FY2025 after two down years (-6.06% in FY2023, -0.50% in FY2024) during the 2022-2023 rate-hike downturn that hit transaction-driven CRE services broadly, and TTM revenue (through Jun 2026) is now accelerating to +11.04% YoY. But this framework is built to look past the headline, and what it finds is a company still digging out of a deeper hole than most of its peers. Operating margin fell from 5.77% (FY2021) to just 2.90% (FY2023) as the downturn hit, and even after two years of recovery sits at only 4.46% (FY2025) — still below where it started. EBIT and free cash flow are both lower today than in FY2021 (EBIT CAGR -4.1%, FCF CAGR -12.3% over the period), so the "growth" in the headline hasn't yet translated into growth in profitability. Return on invested capital (7.24%) doesn't clear stockanalysis.com's own auto-computed cost of capital (8.45%), and net debt/EBITDA sits around 4.1x-4.35x depending on the measurement window — a direct legacy of the 2015 Cushman & Wakefield/DTZ merger and the leveraged capital structure the company carried into its August 2018 IPO, and genuinely higher than most other names in this "big five" comparison set. What's genuinely working: capex is minimal (under 0.5% of revenue, classically asset-light), and gross margin has actually stabilized over the past three years rather than continuing to erode. What's not working, beyond the leverage and returns picture: insiders own essentially none of the company (0.82% of shares outstanding, with no founder or controlling stake of any kind), a structural difference from founder-led peers like Colliers. The stock is down roughly 24.8% over the past year to $12.52, which has pulled the trailing P/E (42.14x) to a level that actually looks rich against its own recent history (a FY22-25 average, excluding FY2023's net-loss year, of roughly 26.56x) — though that trailing multiple is itself distorted by a shrinking near-term earnings base (TTM net income of just $69.1M, down 66% YoY) rather than genuine re-rating; forward P/E (8.11x) tells a very different, much cheaper story if the earnings recovery holds. Net: 2 of 14 criteria met, Martinero Index 25/100, one of the lower scores on this site — a real post-downturn recovery that this framework's leverage and capital-efficiency criteria are specifically designed to stress-test, and they do. This isn't a call to buy or avoid Cushman & Wakefield — just what the checklist says today.
Maarten's note

Built this one alongside Colliers, CBRE, JLL and Savills to get a proper side-by-side read on the "big five" CRE services firms, since they all get lumped together casually but clearly aren't the same company wearing different logos. C&W is the one where the checklist earns its keep the most: the revenue recovery after the 2022-2023 downturn is real and I don't want to wave that away, but margin, EBIT and FCF are all still below where they were in FY2021, and the leverage — a direct hangover from the 2015 merger and the debt load it carried into its 2018 IPO — is the highest of any name in this batch so far. Zero meaningful insider ownership is also just worth sitting with: nobody at the top has real skin in the game here the way Jay Hennick does at Colliers. None of that means the stock is a bad trade at today's price — the forward P/E is genuinely cheap if the recovery keeps compounding — but it's a fundamentally different risk profile than "asset-light services company with a temporarily thin margin," and I want to remember that distinction next time I look at this one.

Q2 2026 Headlines

Revenue reached $2.8B, ahead of the $2.65B analyst consensus, with management citing record second-quarter leasing, services and total revenue. Adjusted EPS of $0.35 met consensus. Trailing & Forward P/E — FY22-25 & Current
FY2023 trailing P/E is not shown — C&W posted a net loss that year (-$35.4M), so the ratio is not meaningful and is explicitly omitted rather than forced to a number. Current trailing P/E of 42.14x sits well above the FY22-25 average of roughly 26.56x (computed across the three years with a meaningful P/E) — but that "rich" reading is itself distorted by a shrinking near-term earnings base (TTM net income of $69.1M, down 66% YoY) rather than a genuine price re-rating. Forward P/E (8.11x) tells the opposite story and is only available as a current figure; historical forward-P/E data for prior fiscal years is shown where available for context.
Trailing P/E Forward P/E (current only)
Reading the three P/E numbers: current P/E is today's share price divided by the last 12 months of actual reported earnings — a backward-looking snapshot at today's price. Trailing P/E (the FY22-25 series above) is that same backward-looking ratio calculated at each fiscal year-end. Forward P/E is today's share price divided by analysts' consensus estimate of the next 12 months of earnings — forward-looking, and generally the more useful lens for judging whether a stock is priced fairly relative to where its earnings are headed. For Cushman & Wakefield specifically, the two multiples tell almost opposite stories: trailing P/E (42.14x) looks expensive next to its own FY22-25 average (26.56x, excluding FY2023's not-meaningful net-loss year), but that trailing figure is inflated by a depressed current earnings base rather than a rich share price — TTM net income has fallen 66% YoY even as revenue has grown. Forward P/E (8.11x) is dramatically cheaper, implying the market expects a sharp earnings recovery as the post-downturn rebound broadens and interest costs ease. One more lens worth a glance: the PEG ratio (forward P/E divided by expected annual EPS growth), which sanity-checks whether a "cheap" multiple is actually cheap once growth is accounted for. Stockanalysis.com's own PEG field shows "n/a" for C&W, but computing it directly from forward P/E (8.11x) against the 3-year consensus EPS growth forecast (17.09%, per stockanalysis.com) gives a PEG of roughly 0.47 — well under the traditional 1.0 "fair value" line, suggesting that if the projected earnings recovery actually shows up, today's price would look cheap in hindsight. That's a real "if," not a given, given this company's track record of earnings volatility.
Balance sheet: net debt of $2,312M against an estimated FY2025 EBITDA of roughly $563.6M (EBIT + an estimated D&A of ~$105M, since exact FY2025 D&A wasn't directly available and is proxied from the trailing-twelve-month figure) gives a Net Debt/EBITDA of ≈4.10x — well above this framework's <2x bar and above where most of C&W's "big five" peers run. On a trailing-twelve-month basis (through Jun 2026), net debt was $2,529M against TTM EBITDA of $581.4M, or ≈4.35x. Two additional flags worth surfacing honestly: interest coverage (EBIT/interest expense) sits at just 2.23x per stockanalysis.com, and the Altman Z-Score is 1.88 — below the conventional 3.0 "safe zone" threshold, which the site itself flags as indicating elevated bankruptcy-risk territory (not a prediction of distress, but a genuine signal this leverage load deserves attention). ROIC of 7.24% doesn't clear stockanalysis.com's own auto-computed 8.45% WACC. Share count is roughly flat, up an estimated 2.1% over the FY21-25 window (implied from net income/EPS: ~227.3M shares in FY2021 to ~232.1M in FY2025) and up 0.72% over the most recent year — mild dilution, not a major factor either way.

What's Working

Revenue is genuinely accelerating post-downturn (TTM +11.04% YoY), management just raised FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance rather than merely reaffirming it, capex is minimal (well under 1% of revenue), gross margin has stabilized rather than continuing to erode, and forward P/E (8.11x) looks genuinely cheap if the earnings recovery holds.

What to Watch

EBIT and free cash flow are both still below their FY2021 levels four years later, ROIC sits below Cushman & Wakefield's own cost of capital, and net debt/EBITDA around 4.1x-4.35x is a genuine, structural legacy of the 2015 merger and pre-IPO leverage — not a temporary blip, and the highest leverage level seen so far in this "big five" comparison set. Interest coverage of just 2.23x and an Altman Z-Score of 1.88 (below the conventional 3.0 safe-zone threshold) both deserve attention rather than dismissal. Insider ownership is essentially zero, with nobody at the top holding meaningful personal capital at risk. Whether the current revenue and guidance momentum can outrun the debt load — through further deleveraging, not just growth — is the central question for Cushman & Wakefield from here.
