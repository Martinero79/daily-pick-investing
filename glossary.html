Key terms, plainly explained

Glossary

Every ratio and term that shows up across the 100+ company scorecards on this site, explained without jargon. If a dashboard uses a number you don't recognize, it's probably here.

CAGR Capex Intensity EBIT Entry Multiple EV/EBITDA FCF FCF Conversion Martinero Index Market Concentration mNAV Net Debt/EBITDA PEG Ratio P/E Ratio P/FCF ROIC TTM WACC YoY
CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate)

The smoothed, year-over-year growth rate that would take a starting number to an ending number over a period, as if it grew by the exact same percentage every single year. Used across the scorecards for revenue, EBIT and FCF growth over the FY21-25 window (or the best available window for younger companies). Smooths out single bad or great years but can be distorted by a very low or negative starting base — flagged explicitly on dashboards where that happens (e.g. CoreWeave, Reddit).

Capex Intensity

Capital expenditure (money spent on physical/long-term assets — factories, servers, GPUs, stores) as a percentage of revenue. Lower is generally better for the scorecard, since it means a company can grow without constantly reinvesting most of what it earns. Asset-light software and services businesses often run under 5%; heavy manufacturers, telecoms and AI infrastructure providers can run 15-30%+.

EBIT (Operating Income)

Earnings Before Interest and Taxes — profit from the core business, before financing costs and tax effects are layered on. Used instead of net income across the scorecards because it strips out debt structure and tax-jurisdiction quirks, making profitability more comparable company to company.

Entry Multiple (vs. Own History)

How expensive a stock's current valuation multiple (usually P/E, sometimes EV/EBITDA or P/FCF) is relative to that same company's own 5-year average multiple — not relative to the market or its peers. A ratio at or below 1.00 means the stock is cheaper than its own historical norm; above 1.30 signals a rich premium. This is the criterion that flags names like Shopify and Palantir as "cheap relative to their own history" even at a high absolute P/E.

EV/EBITDA

Enterprise Value (market cap plus net debt) divided by EBITDA (EBIT plus depreciation and amortization). A valuation multiple used in place of P/E for capital-intensive or heavily-levered companies, since it accounts for debt load and ignores non-cash depreciation, which can otherwise distort earnings-based ratios.

FCF (Free Cash Flow)

Cash generated by the business after paying for the capital expenditure needed to run and maintain it. Considered a cleaner measure of real profitability than net income, since it can't be distorted by non-cash accounting items the way GAAP earnings sometimes are.

FCF Conversion (FCF/EBIT)

What percentage of operating profit actually turns into free cash flow. A company converting 80%+ is turning accounting profit into real, usable cash efficiently; a low conversion rate can mean heavy working-capital needs, aggressive capex, or profit that's more "on paper" than in the bank.

Martinero Index

This site's own weighted 0-100 score, built from 14 fixed criteria (growth, profitability, market position, balance-sheet health, valuation, ownership and structural profitability), each graded on a graduated 0-5 scale rather than a strict pass/fail. Weights sum to 105, normalized back to a 100-point scale. Kept deliberately separate from the simpler "X/14 criteria met" tally shown on every dashboard — the index captures degree and nuance, the tally captures a hard yes/no screen. See the About page for the full weighting breakdown.

Market Concentration

How consolidated or fragmented a company's competitive landscape is, scored 1 (near-monopoly, very few real competitors) to 5 (fully open, many players fighting for share). A near-monopoly position generally supports pricing power and durable margins; a fragmented market usually means more competitive pressure on both.

mNAV (Market Cap ÷ Net Asset Value)

Used specifically for companies whose value is dominated by a large holding of an external asset rather than operating earnings — on this site, most notably Strategy Inc. (MSTR), whose value tracks its Bitcoin treasury. mNAV compares market cap to the current market value of the underlying asset held. Above 1.0x means the market is paying a premium over the raw asset value (often for structure, access, or leverage); at or below 1.0x means the stock trades for less than the assets on its own balance sheet are worth.

Net Debt/EBITDA

Total debt minus cash, divided by EBITDA — a standard measure of how leveraged a company is relative to its cash-generating power, and roughly how many years of EBITDA it would take to pay off net debt. Under 2x is generally considered conservative on this site's scorecard; a negative figure means the company holds more cash than debt (net cash).

PEG Ratio (Price/Earnings-to-Growth)

The P/E ratio divided by the expected (or trailing) earnings growth rate, expressed as a plain number rather than a percentage. Built to answer "is this multiple justified by how fast the company is actually growing?" — a high P/E paired with high growth can still be a low, reasonable PEG. Below 1.0 is often read as undervalued (though sometimes a red flag if growth is decelerating), 1.0-2.0 is the range most classic "reasonably priced growth" compounders live in, and 2.0+ signals the price has detached from what the growth rate can realistically justify. A growth-investing tool specifically — not a useful lens for slow, steady blue-chip compounders, which are better judged on plain P/E or dividend yield.

P/E Ratio (Price/Earnings)

Share price divided by earnings per share — the most common valuation shorthand, showing how many years of current profit it would take to "earn back" the price paid, all else equal. Trailing P/E uses the last 12 months of actual earnings; forward P/E uses analysts' next-12-month earnings estimate instead.

P/FCF (Price/Free Cash Flow)

Market cap divided by free cash flow — used in place of P/E when GAAP earnings are distorted by non-cash items (large stock-based compensation, one-off write-downs, accounting quirks) that make the earnings figure a poor reflection of real profitability. Shopify's dashboard is a good example of where this site uses P/FCF instead of P/E.

ROIC vs. WACC (Return on Invested Capital vs. Weighted Average Cost of Capital)

ROIC measures how much profit a company generates per dollar of capital invested in the business; WACC is the estimated cost of that capital (a blend of the return debt and equity holders require). The spread between the two — ROIC minus WACC — shows whether a company is actually creating value with the capital it deploys (positive spread) or destroying it (negative spread, i.e. earning less than the capital itself costs), regardless of how large or impressive ROIC looks in isolation.

TTM (Trailing Twelve Months)

The most recent four reported quarters added together — a rolling annual figure that's more current than the last full fiscal year but smoother than a single quarter. Used on dashboards for companies with limited full-year history (e.g. recent IPOs like CoreWeave) or when the latest quarterly print is more relevant than the last completed fiscal year.

WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital)

The blended rate of return a company needs to generate just to satisfy both its debt holders (interest) and equity holders (required return), weighted by how much of each type of capital the company uses. Functions as the minimum bar a company's returns need to clear to actually be creating value rather than just moving money around. See ROIC vs. WACC above.

YoY (Year-over-Year)

Growth compared to the same period one year earlier (e.g. Q2 2026 vs. Q2 2025) — used for the "latest quarter" growth figure shown alongside each company's longer-run CAGR, giving a more current read on momentum than the multi-year trend alone.
