Key terms, plainly explained

Glossary

Every ratio and term that shows up across the 200+ company scorecards on this site, explained without jargon. If a dashboard uses a number you don't recognize, it's probably here.

ADR (American Depositary Receipt)

A US-listed share that represents shares of a foreign company (for example ASML, TSMC or Novo Nordisk on US exchanges). It trades in dollars, so its price moves with both the company and the exchange rate. The dashboards state which listing and currency each price is quoted in.

ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue)

The yearly value of a software company's subscription contracts at a point in time. A growing ARR signals future revenue, but it is a company-defined metric and not audited revenue, so definitions differ between companies.

Backlog / RPO

Orders already signed but not yet delivered or recognised as revenue (RPO means remaining performance obligations). A rising backlog suggests demand ahead, but it can include contracts that are cancelled or delayed.

Base Effect

The idea that a small company needs far less in absolute terms to grow ten times than a large one. A $2B company turning into $20B is plausible; a $2T company turning into $20T is not. Used in the tenbagger checks.

Basis Points (bp)

One hundredth of a percentage point. 100 basis points equal 1 percentage point, so a margin falling from 24.0% to 23.0% fell by 100bp. Used to describe small changes in margins, yields and interest rates.

Buyback (Share Repurchase)

A company using its cash to buy its own shares, which reduces the share count and raises each remaining share's slice of profits. It only creates value if shares are bought at a sensible price and the buyback is not simply offsetting new shares issued to staff.

CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate)

The smoothed, year-over-year growth rate that would take a starting number to an ending number over a period, as if it grew by the exact same percentage every single year. Used across the scorecards for revenue, EBIT and FCF growth over the FY21-25 window (or the best available window for younger companies). Smooths out single bad or great years but can be distorted by a very low or negative starting base — flagged explicitly on dashboards where that happens (e.g. CoreWeave, Reddit).

Capex Intensity

Capital expenditure (money spent on physical/long-term assets — factories, servers, GPUs, stores) as a percentage of revenue. Lower is generally better for the scorecard, since it means a company can grow without constantly reinvesting most of what it earns. Asset-light software and services businesses often run under 5%; heavy manufacturers, telecoms and AI infrastructure providers can run 15-30%+.

CET1 (Common Equity Tier 1)

A bank's core capital as a percentage of its risk-weighted assets, the main measure of how big a cushion it has against losses. Higher means safer. Used for banks in place of leverage ratios such as net debt/EBITDA.

Cost-Income Ratio

A bank's operating costs divided by its income. Lower is better: 40% means it spends 40 cents to earn each dollar. Used for banks in place of operating margin.

DCA (Dollar-Cost Averaging)

Investing a fixed amount at regular intervals (say, monthly) rather than putting a lump sum in all at once, regardless of where the price sits that day. Removes the need to time an entry — you naturally buy more shares when the price is low and fewer when it's high, averaging out your cost basis over the cycle. The trade-off is simplicity and reduced timing risk in exchange for potentially lower returns than a well-timed lump sum in a rising market — but for non-professional investors who can't reliably call market tops and bottoms, it's the more disciplined default.

Dilution (and Stock-Based Compensation)

New shares being issued, which shrinks each existing shareholder's ownership. It happens when a company raises money by selling shares or pays staff in stock (stock-based compensation). The Share Count Trend criterion checks whether the share count is stable or falling.

Dividend Yield and Payout Ratio

Dividend yield is the yearly dividend divided by the share price. Payout ratio is the share of profit paid out as dividends. A very high yield can signal that the market doubts the dividend will last.

Drawdown

The fall from a share's previous peak to its low, for example -50%. Growth stocks routinely suffer drawdowns of 40-60% even when the business keeps growing, which is why returns on paper do not show what holding them feels like.

EBIT (Operating Income)

Earnings Before Interest and Taxes — profit from the core business, before financing costs and tax effects are layered on. Used instead of net income across the scorecards because it strips out debt structure and tax-jurisdiction quirks, making profitability more comparable company to company.

Entry Multiple (vs. Own History)

How expensive a stock's current valuation multiple (usually P/E, sometimes EV/EBITDA or P/FCF) is relative to that same company's own 5-year average multiple — not relative to the market or its peers. A ratio at or below 1.00 means the stock is cheaper than its own historical norm; above 1.30 signals a rich premium. This is the criterion that flags names like Shopify and Palantir as "cheap relative to their own history" even at a high absolute P/E.

EPS (Earnings per Share)

Net profit divided by the number of shares. It is the 'E' in the P/E ratio. GAAP EPS follows accounting rules; adjusted (non-GAAP) EPS excludes items the company chooses to leave out.

EV/EBITDA

Enterprise Value (market cap plus net debt) divided by EBITDA (EBIT plus depreciation and amortization). A valuation multiple used in place of P/E for capital-intensive or heavily-levered companies, since it accounts for debt load and ignores non-cash depreciation, which can otherwise distort earnings-based ratios.

FCF Conversion (FCF/EBIT)

What percentage of operating profit actually turns into free cash flow. A company converting 80%+ is turning accounting profit into real, usable cash efficiently; a low conversion rate can mean heavy working-capital needs, aggressive capex, or profit that's more "on paper" than in the bank.

FCF (Free Cash Flow)

Cash generated by the business after paying for the capital expenditure needed to run and maintain it. Considered a cleaner measure of real profitability than net income, since it can't be distorted by non-cash accounting items the way GAAP earnings sometimes are.

Forward P/E

The share price divided by analysts' expected earnings for the next 12 months, rather than the past 12. It is lower than the trailing P/E when profits are expected to grow, but it depends on forecasts that can be wrong. Marked as an estimate on the dashboards.

GAAP vs. Non-GAAP

GAAP figures follow standard US accounting rules. Non-GAAP (or 'adjusted') figures leave out items management considers unusual, such as stock-based compensation or restructuring costs. The dashboards show GAAP as the base and label adjusted numbers as such.

Gross Margin

Revenue minus the direct cost of making or delivering the product, as a percentage of revenue. A high and stable gross margin suggests pricing power. It is the basis for the Margin Stability criterion where a company reports it.

Growth Trend (Annual Momentum)

The 'Growth Trend' badge on the scoreboard compares the latest fiscal year's revenue growth with the prior year's: accelerating, steady or decelerating (steady means within about 2 percentage points). It is a signal, not one of the 14 scored criteria.

Guidance

The company's own forecast for upcoming revenue or profit. Raising guidance is usually a positive sign, but shares can still fall if the raise is smaller than investors hoped.

Insider / Founder Ownership

The share of a company owned by its founders, executives and board. High founder ownership can align management with shareholders, but it can also mean one family or person holds control. A scorecard criterion.

Margin Stability (Criterion 14)

The change in a company's gross margin over three fiscal years, in percentage points. A margin that holds or rises scores best; one that falls by more than 2.5 points scores zero. Where a company does not report a gross margin, the EBIT margin is used as a proxy, and the scorecard row says so.

Market Cap (Market Capitalisation)

The share price multiplied by the number of shares outstanding, which is the market's price for the whole company. It is the measure of size used in 'small cap', 'mid cap' and 'large cap'. Marked as an estimate where the share count is not current.

Market Concentration

How consolidated or fragmented a company's competitive landscape is, scored 1 (near-monopoly, very few real competitors) to 5 (fully open, many players fighting for share). A near-monopoly position generally supports pricing power and durable margins; a fragmented market usually means more competitive pressure on both.

Martinero Index

This site's own weighted 0-100 score, built from 14 fixed criteria (growth, profitability, market position, balance-sheet health, valuation, ownership and structural profitability), each graded on a graduated 0-5 scale rather than a strict pass/fail. Weights sum to 105, normalized back to a 100-point scale. Kept deliberately separate from the simpler "X/14 criteria met" tally shown on every dashboard — the index captures degree and nuance, the tally captures a hard yes/no screen. See the About page for the full weighting breakdown.

'Met' and the X / 14 Score

The number of the 14 scorecard criteria a company clears at the pass level, for example 9 / 14. It is a simple count, while the Martinero Index weights each criterion by importance and gives partial credit for near misses.

mNAV (Market Cap ÷ Net Asset Value)

Used specifically for companies whose value is dominated by a large holding of an external asset rather than operating earnings — on this site, most notably Strategy Inc. (MSTR), whose value tracks its Bitcoin treasury. mNAV compares market cap to the current market value of the underlying asset held. Above 1.0x means the market is paying a premium over the raw asset value (often for structure, access, or leverage); at or below 1.0x means the stock trades for less than the assets on its own balance sheet are worth.

N/A (Not Applicable)

A criterion marked N/A cannot be scored fairly, for example FCF growth for a company with negative cash flow at the start of the period, or any 5-year measure for a recent IPO. N/A criteria are removed from the Martinero Index calculation, so the index of a company with several N/As is not directly comparable with fully scored ones.

Net Cash

Cash and similar assets minus all debt. A company with net cash could pay off every debt immediately. The opposite is net debt, measured against EBITDA.

Net Debt/EBITDA

Total debt minus cash, divided by EBITDA — a standard measure of how leveraged a company is relative to its cash-generating power, and roughly how many years of EBITDA it would take to pay off net debt. Under 2x is generally considered conservative on this site's scorecard; a negative figure means the company holds more cash than debt (net cash).

Net Interest Margin (NIM)

For a bank, the gap between the interest it earns on loans and pays on deposits, relative to its assets. Used for banks together with return on tangible equity and the cost-income ratio.

Operating Margin

EBIT divided by revenue: how many cents of operating profit the company keeps from each dollar of sales. A scorecard criterion and the clearest single measure of how profitable the core business is.

Organic Growth / Like-for-Like / Constant Currency

Growth excluding the effect of acquisitions, disposals and exchange-rate moves. Companies report it to show underlying demand. Reported growth can differ a lot, which is why dashboards label which one they use.

P/B and P/TBV (Price to Book / Tangible Book)

The share price relative to the company's book value (assets minus liabilities) per share. Tangible book excludes goodwill and intangibles. Used for banks, where book value is a more useful anchor than earnings.

PEG Ratio (Price/Earnings-to-Growth)

The P/E ratio divided by the expected (or trailing) earnings growth rate, expressed as a plain number rather than a percentage. Built to answer "is this multiple justified by how fast the company is actually growing?" — a high P/E paired with high growth can still be a low, reasonable PEG. Below 1.0 is often read as undervalued (though sometimes a red flag if growth is decelerating), 1.0-2.0 is the range most classic "reasonably priced growth" compounders live in, and 2.0+ signals the price has detached from what the growth rate can realistically justify. A growth-investing tool specifically — not a useful lens for slow, steady blue-chip compounders, which are better judged on plain P/E or dividend yield.

P/E Ratio (Price/Earnings)

Share price divided by earnings per share — the most common valuation shorthand, showing how many years of current profit it would take to "earn back" the price paid, all else equal. Trailing P/E uses the last 12 months of actual earnings; forward P/E uses analysts' next-12-month earnings estimate instead.

P/FCF (Price/Free Cash Flow)

Market cap divided by free cash flow — used in place of P/E when GAAP earnings are distorted by non-cash items (large stock-based compensation, one-off write-downs, accounting quirks) that make the earnings figure a poor reflection of real profitability. Shopify's dashboard is a good example of where this site uses P/FCF instead of P/E.

P/S Ratio (Price/Sales)

Market cap divided by revenue. Useful for companies with no profits yet, but it ignores margins: a P/S of 50x means a lot of future growth is already in the price, which is one of the warning signs in the tenbagger checklist.

ROIC vs. WACC (Return on Invested Capital vs. Weighted Average Cost of Capital)

ROIC measures how much profit a company generates per dollar of capital invested in the business; WACC is the estimated cost of that capital (a blend of the return debt and equity holders require). The spread between the two — ROIC minus WACC — shows whether a company is actually creating value with the capital it deploys (positive spread) or destroying it (negative spread, i.e. earning less than the capital itself costs), regardless of how large or impressive ROIC looks in isolation.

ROTE (Return on Tangible Equity)

A bank's yearly profit divided by its tangible equity: how well it earns on shareholders' money. Used for banks in place of ROIC. A level of 15% or more is generally considered strong.

Share Count Trend

Whether the number of shares outstanding is falling (buybacks), flat or rising (dilution) over the period. A scorecard criterion: stable or falling counts mean your slice of the company is not shrinking.

Tenbagger

A stock whose price rises tenfold. The site's checks look at a small starting market cap, accelerating growth, a move from losses to profit, strong returns on capital, and warning signs such as a very high P/S or heavy dilution. It describes past winners, not a prediction.

TTM (Trailing Twelve Months)

The most recent four reported quarters added together — a rolling annual figure that's more current than the last full fiscal year but smoother than a single quarter. Used on dashboards for companies with limited full-year history (e.g. recent IPOs like CoreWeave) or when the latest quarterly print is more relevant than the last completed fiscal year.

WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital)

The blended rate of return a company needs to generate just to satisfy both its debt holders (interest) and equity holders (required return), weighted by how much of each type of capital the company uses. Functions as the minimum bar a company's returns need to clear to actually be creating value rather than just moving money around. See ROIC vs. WACC above.

YoY (Year-over-Year)

Growth compared to the same period one year earlier (e.g. Q2 2026 vs. Q2 2025) — used for the "latest quarter" growth figure shown alongside each company's longer-run CAGR, giving a more current read on momentum than the multi-year trend alone.
