Regional P/E Gaps: Cheap, or Cheap for a Reason?

The US looks expensive, Europe cheap and China a contrarian bargain. The headline multiples agree, but an index is a bundle of sectors, payout habits and legal systems, and most of the gap sits in what the bundle holds. Comparing the three blocks, and nine scored companies from them, shows what a low multiple does and does not tell you.

What the comparison says

The US premium is mostly a mix effect. Technology, communication and consumer discretionary make up about 57% of the S&P 500, while Europe's largest groups are financials and industrials and technology is only about 9%. High-margin, capital-light businesses get high multiples, while banks, industrials and carmakers with gross margins near 16% do not. A 2022 study put sector weights at about 70% of the US-Europe forward P/E gap and a 2026 commentary at about half. AXA IM adds that even after adjusting for mix, Europe trades at about a 25% discount, against about 10% before Covid, so some of the gap is real and has widened.

Quality sets the multiple, not geography. Hermès, a French company, trades at about the same multiple as Visa, and SAP is priced close to Microsoft. Volkswagen sits at about 6.5x because its margins and earnings are falling, which is a warning, not a bargain. One number for "Europe" or "China" hides a very wide range.

Payout habits differ, and matter less than they seem. US firms returned about $1.02 trillion in buybacks against $665 billion in dividends over the year to September 2025, while STOXX 600 dividends are projected near €454 billion for 2026. Buybacks lift earnings per share, but they are concentrated (four companies made over 22% of Q3 2025 repurchases) and they destroy value if shares are bought above what the business is worth. The common claims that dividends are tax-penalised in Europe or prove that management cannot reinvest are weak: US dividends are taxed too, and Europe's best companies, ASML and SAP among them, reinvest heavily and also pay.

China: the alignment gap. The standard Western assumption is that management works for minority shareholders. In China the state's priorities can come first, and the 2021 crackdown on platform companies showed that margins and business models can be rewritten by regulation, which breaks a normal cash-flow forecast. Many US-listed Chinese names, Alibaba among them, are also held through offshore VIE structures, so an investor owns a Cayman holding company with contractual rights to the Chinese business, not the business directly. Investors price that uncertainty with a lower multiple, and it is a rational discount.

But the "no payouts" story does not hold. A common claim is that Chinese tech reinvests for the state instead of returning cash. The site's own dashboards show the opposite for the two biggest names: Tencent has cut its share count through buybacks in four of the last five fiscal years (about HK$112 billion repurchased in 2024, with a new 10% mandate approved in May 2026), and Alibaba has cut its share count by more than 13% since FY2022. The discount is real, but it is about the risk to those earnings, not about management hoarding them.

The buyer base is also moving. US institutions did pull back, and the risk is live: in June 2026 the Pentagon added Alibaba, Baidu and BYD to its list of Chinese military companies. The listing carries no automatic trading ban, but lawyers describe it as a possible precursor to US investment restrictions. The older delisting threat is dormant: US auditors have had inspection access to China-based audit firms since 2022, though the test is repeated each year. At the same time, mainland investors bought about HK$1.19 trillion of Hong Kong shares through Stock Connect in the 12 months to March 2026, a record, and Bank of America's fund survey shows global active funds moving from underweight to neutral on China in June after four years. So the pool of buyers has not simply shrunk; it is changing hands, and the discount has been narrowing from its lows.

The index hides a wide range. PDD and Tencent pair low multiples with strong scorecards, while Alibaba, at about 15x forward, meets only 4 of 14 criteria. Property stress, weak domestic demand and deflation also weigh on the market. A governance discount makes a safety margin a requirement for Chinese stocks, and it does not make every one of them cheap or every one of them a trap.

A low index multiple is a question, not an answer. The gap is part sector mix, part lower growth and returns, part payout habits and part policy risk. Buying a cheap region for its multiple alone means buying all of those at once, while a cheap company with strong margins and returns is a different thing.

What could change the picture

Earnings growth: if US technology earnings slow, the premium compresses, and if European earnings recover, the discount narrows. Concentration: three stocks are about 55% of the S&P 500's technology sector, so the US multiple leans on very few companies. Policy: Chinese stimulus, tighter regulation or a move from the Pentagon list to US investment limits would move that block overnight. Rates and currencies change what each block is worth to a euro-based investor.

One caution for investors

P/E ratios from different providers differ in earnings basis, timing (trailing or forward) and index definition, and I found no same-day, same-method figure for all three blocks. Return-on-equity comparisons across the regions were not available from sources I could verify, so none are shown. A regional index fund holds every one of these effects together, including the Volkswagens and the Alibabas.
