🏦 Banks: Compounder, Cyclical or Income Stock?

We just added 15 banks to the site. They are easy to like (big dividends, cheap-looking multiples) and easy to misunderstand. Here is what you can realistically expect from owning one, and when it makes sense. 👇

🎯 The short answer

📈 Compounder at 10-15%?
Rarely

Banks grow with the economy and with lending. Revenue and profit growth on our dashboards sits in the single digits to low double digits.

🔄 Cyclical?
Yes

Interest rates, loan losses and the credit cycle swing profits, and the market swings the multiple even more.

💰 Income + value?
Yes

A realistic long-run return is roughly 7-10% a year, with payouts supplying half or more of it.

🧮 Where a bank's return comes from

Return ≈ payout yield + growth ± change in valuationDividends and buybacks, plus how fast book value grows, plus whatever the market does to the multiple.

1. Payout yield (the steady part) 💵. Banks must hold capital, but the rest of the profit goes to shareholders. Several on our site return 80% to over 100% of profit through dividends and buybacks (ING about 107%, DNB about 86%, Nordea about 84%, DBS about 79%). On a P/E of 10 to 15, that is a total yield of roughly 5-7% a year.

2. Growth (the slow part) 🌱. What a bank keeps grows its book. If it earns 14% on tangible equity and pays out 80%, the book grows roughly 3% a year, plus whatever lending growth and inflation add. Think 2-4%, not 15%.

3. The valuation (the wild card) 🎲. If you buy at 1.0x tangible book and the bank earns 12%, you earn about 12% before any change in valuation. Pay 3x and the same earnings give you about 4%. The multiple at purchase matters more for banks than for most sectors.

Add 1 and 2 with an unchanged multiple and you get about 7-10% a year, which matches the 5-10% intuition, with payouts making up half or more.

🔄 The three cycles that move a bank

CycleWhen it helps 🟢When it hurts 🔴
💹 Interest ratesRising or high rates widen the gap between what loans earn and deposits cost (net interest income).Falling rates squeeze that gap, and customers move deposits to higher-paying products.
💳 CreditBoom times: few defaults, banks even release reserves (that flattered 2021 earnings).Recessions: defaults rise and banks set aside money for losses, so profit can collapse fast.
⚖️ Rules and trustStrong capital and a clean record: the bank can pay out more.New capital rules, fines or a run on confidence. Credit Suisse and the 2018 Wells Fargo asset cap show it.

That is far above 5-10%. Why? 2021 was a low starting point (rates near zero, banks priced at a discount to book) and rates then rose, so profits recovered and the market paid higher multiples. ICBC is the clearest case: its earnings per share barely moved (RMB 0.95 to 1.00), but its share price re-rated from about 2.7x earnings to about 6.4x. Danske Bank shows the same: revenue growth of only about 4.5% a year, but a share price up about 25% a year. That is a change in valuation, not growth. Do not extrapolate it, because it happens once. ⚠️

Bank of America (+2.6%) and Nordea (+7.2%) are a reminder that the same sector gave very different results depending on starting price and business mix.

✅ When a bank is worth owning

CheckGood sign 🟢Warning 🔴
💎 Price vs. bookP/TBV near or below what the return on equity justifies (roughly 1x for a 10% ROTCE, 1.5x for 15%).Paying 3x book for a bank earning 12%.
🛡️ CapitalCET1 comfortably above the requirement (the dashboards show this buffer).Thin buffer, so dividends come first to be cut.
⚙️ EfficiencyCost-to-income falling or low (DBS, DNB and Santander run at roughly 40% or lower).Cost-to-income above 65%, or rising.
🔁 PayoutHigh, steady dividends plus buybacks that shrink the share count.Dividends that depend on one-off gains or asset sales.
🧱 Business mixStable deposits and fees (wealth management, retail).Earnings that rely on trading, or on a single rate or country.

When to be careful: after a strong run at a high multiple (our dashboards flag several banks above their own five-year P/E and P/B history, including JPMorgan, Goldman and Danske), when rates are about to fall, or when the bank is state-controlled and profits serve policy goals before shareholders (the Chinese banks).

Do banks beat 3% over five years? More likely than not, but not for sure. The gap exists because owners are paid for taking risk, and it narrows if you buy after a big run. 🎲 For retirees: bank dividends can be a useful slice of income, but they are cut in the same crises when share prices fall. Many keep a cash buffer for a few years of spending and spread dividend stocks across sectors, not only banks.

🧠 So: investable or not? Investable, but as an income and value holding, not a compounder. A bank can sit well in a long-term portfolio for its payouts, as long as you buy at a sensible price relative to its returns, accept a cycle that can cut profit sharply in a recession, and do not expect 15% a year. If your goal is compounding, banks are a smaller part of the answer. 🎯

🔎 How our scorecard treats banks

Banks do not report gross margin, EBIT or free cash flow in the usual way, so each bank dashboard swaps in bank metrics: net revenue, net income, return on tangible equity vs. cost of equity, CET1 capital vs. the requirement, payout ratio, and cost-to-income. Because the bar is 15% growth, banks score lower than software or luxury, which is the point: the checklist says banks are not growth compounders. Their scores mostly tell you about quality and price, not growth. Dashboards: DBS · Danske · DNB · JPMorgan · ING · Nordea · HSBC · ICBC · Citigroup and more via the archive.
