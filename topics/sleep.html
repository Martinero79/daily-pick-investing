Sleep: Who Actually Makes Money From Your Night?

Everyone wants to sleep better, and a lot of companies sell to that wish. But a bad night is a very different product depending on how it is sold: a medical device prescribed for life, a gummy at the pharmacy, a smart bed in a showroom, or a tracker on your wrist. Scoring five listed names on the same 14-point checklist shows which layer pays.

The stack: four ways to sell a good night

Medical devices. Obstructive sleep apnea, where breathing repeatedly stops during sleep, is common: a 2019 study in The Lancet Respiratory Medicine (led by ResMed) estimated about 936 million adults worldwide have it, and that most cases are undiagnosed. ResMed and Philips make the CPAP machines and masks that treat it. Patients need replacement masks every few months, which makes the revenue recurring.

Supplements. Melatonin and sleep gummies are cheap to make and easy to sell, but the products are easy to copy, so the money goes to the brand owner with shelf space, not to the sleep claim.

Smart beds. A bed that adjusts and tracks your sleep is a large, infrequent purchase tied to housing and consumer confidence.

Wearables. Sleep scores and breathing alerts on a watch are a feature that supports the device, not a business of their own.

What the scores say

The prescribed device wins. ResMed turns a medical need into repeat revenue: sleep and breathing health was about 88% of its $5.65B FY2026 sales, with a 33.4% operating margin, net cash and $1.65B of free cash flow. Growth is steady, not explosive (about 10% a year), and the stock is not cheap, but the scorecard is the cleanest in the group.

The big debate is GLP-1 drugs. Lilly's Zepbound won US approval for moderate-to-severe sleep apnea in obese adults in December 2024. If weight-loss drugs shrink the patient pool, device demand could fall in the long run. So far ResMed says GLP-1 users are entering CPAP therapy at least as often as before, but the multi-year effect is not yet visible in the results. It is a live risk, not a settled one.

Philips shows what a recall costs. Its Respironics sleep and breathing devices were recalled in 2021, and a 2024 US consent decree bars new US sales of sleep and respiratory devices until quality systems are certified. With Philips largely absent from new US sales, ResMed faces less direct competition there. Underneath, Philips has repaired much of the rest: gross margin rose from 40.4% to 45.2%, but sales are flat and returns sit below the cost of capital. Scoring only 4 of 14 criteria, it is a turnaround, not a compounder.

Supplements are a small slice, and now a divested one. Church & Dwight's sleep and melatonin gummies sat inside Vitafusion, which it sold on 31 December 2025. The company is a solid consumer-staples business (50/100), but sleep was never a meaningful part of it.

Smart beds show the risk of a big-ticket product. Sleep Number's sales fell from $2.19B in FY2021 to $1.41B in FY2025, with negative equity and going-concern doubt, and the company said common shares are expected to receive no recovery. Owning the sleep brand did not protect a fixed-cost store network from a weak housing and consumer cycle.

Selling sleep pays when the buyer has to keep buying: a prescribed device with replacement masks beats a one-off bed or a gummy that anyone can copy. The market for a good night is large, but the profits sit with whoever is hardest to replace.

What could change the picture

GLP-1 drugs could shrink or reshape the sleep-apnea patient pool. Philips' return to the US market, if its consent decree is lifted, would bring back a large competitor to ResMed. Home-sleep testing and wearables may find more undiagnosed patients, which would help device makers. Housing and the consumer cycle drive bed and mattress sales. And valuation matters: a great business can still be a poor entry price.

One caution for investors

"Sleep" is a theme, not an industry. A basket built on it would mix a high-margin medical device maker with a bankrupt bed retailer and a tiny gummy business, and the theme gives no reason to expect them to move together. The checklist looks at each company's own numbers, and on those numbers only one name here clears the bar.
