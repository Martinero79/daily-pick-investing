💱 Currencies: The Hidden Second Return in Every Foreign Stock

Buy a US stock with euros or Danish kroner and you earn two returns: what the share does in dollars, and what the dollar does against your money. Sometimes the second one gives you a free lift. Sometimes it eats your gain. 👇

🧮 The one formula

(1 + share return) × (1 + currency move) − 1 = your returnBoth numbers measured in the same direction: from your home currency's point of view.

Say a US stock rises 10% in dollars. If the dollar is worth 10% less in euros when you sell, you did not make 10%. You made 1.10 × 0.90 − 1 = −1%. The company did nothing wrong. The exchange rate took the gain.

🇩🇰 Euro and Danish krone: nearly the same thing

Denmark pegs the krone to the euro, so it trades in a narrow band around 7.46 DKK per euro (the central bank defends it within ±2.25%). For a Danish investor, the dollar moves almost exactly as it does for a euro investor. Everything below applies to both. A Swedish, Norwegian or Swiss investor has a floating currency, so their own krona or franc adds a third moving part.

2025 is the lesson. The S&P 500 rose about 16% in dollars, but with the dollar down about 12% against the euro, a euro investor kept only about +3%. A US investor and a European investor owned the same shares and had a very different year. ⚖️

Over a short time the currency can dominate. Over decades it tends to average out, but "decades" is a long wait, and you may need the money before it does. ⏳

📦 Where the currency shows up in company stories

🏷️ Listing currency

The currency the share trades in. This is the one you convert from and the one your return depends on.

🌍 Revenue currency

Where the company earns. A Swedish firm selling mostly in dollars and euros can be a "dollar stock" listed in kronor.

🏭 Cost currency

Where it pays wages and suppliers. Dollar sales and krona costs help the margin when the dollar rises, and hurt when it falls.

🔧 Example: a global exporter listed in a small currency

Atlas Copco makes compressors and vacuum pumps and sells nearly everything outside Sweden, yet lists in Swedish kronor. In 2025 a strong krona cut the translated value of its foreign sales: revenue fell 4.8% in kronor, mostly from currency. A strong home currency hurts exporters' reported numbers, and a weak one flatters them. For the shareholder it adds a second layer: the share price moves in SEK, and your own conversion into euros or DKK comes on top.

🛡️ Can you remove the currency effect?

Hedging locks the exchange rate with a forward contract, usually inside a currency-hedged fund. It removes the swing in both directions, but it has a price: roughly the gap between the two currencies' short-term interest rates. When US rates are above euro rates, as they have been, a euro investor who hedges pays that gap every year. Many long-term investors skip hedging and accept the volatility. 🤝

Diversify instead. Owning several currencies spreads the risk, and a company that earns across the world is partly its own hedge.

🧠 Remember three things. (1) A foreign stock is a share plus a currency bet. (2) The swing can be bigger than the company's own year, as 2025 showed. (3) The stock's listing currency and the currencies it earns in are not the same thing. Check both before you judge a return. ✅
