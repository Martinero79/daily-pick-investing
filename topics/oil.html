🛢️ Oil Majors: Cash Machines at the Top of the Cycle?

Shell and TotalEnergies just posted their biggest quarterly profits since 2022, several majors hit record share prices this month, and Brent sits near $114. It is tempting to see cheap earnings and fat payouts. Here is what you can realistically expect from owning an oil major, and why the cheapest-looking moment can be the riskiest. 👇

🎯 The short answer

📈 Compounder?
No

Growth is flat to modest. Capex has risen at the big US majors while free cash flow has fallen from the 2022 peak.

🔄 Cyclical?
Very

Profit follows the oil price and refining margins, which follow wars, supply and demand.

💰 Income holding?
Yes, but

Payouts are generous, but they are paid from today's price. Ask what they look like if oil falls.

🧮 Where an oil major's return comes from

Return ≈ payout yield + small growth ± the oil cycleDividends and buybacks, plus slow growth, plus or minus what the oil price and the market multiple do.

1. Payouts (the steady part) 💵. Majors return most of their free cash flow. The share count tells the story: since 2021 Shell has retired about 27% of its shares, Equinor about 26%, BP about 24% and TotalEnergies about 15%. Exxon has a 45-year record of dividend increases and buys back roughly $20B a year.

2. Growth (the weak part) 🌱. Oil is a depleting asset: to stand still, a major has to keep spending. Revenue and earnings on our dashboards are flat to down versus the 2022 peak.

3. The cycle (the wild card) 🎢. Q3 profits were lifted by Brent near $114 and record refining margins, in a period shaped by the Iran conflict. Shell's Q3 profit was reported at about $10.8B and it raised its buyback to $5B, but those are prices the market does not promise to keep.

⚠️ Why a low P/E can mislead at the peak

For most companies, a low P/E can signal a bargain. For oil it often signals the opposite: earnings are at their highest, so the multiple looks lowest right before they fall. Our dashboards compare each stock's P/E with its own five-year average, and the average is distorted by the huge 2022 profits. Even so, several majors trade above that average: ConocoPhillips at about 19.6x versus a 12.2x average, and Chevron at about 1.4 times its own average. The lesson is to look at earnings over a full cycle, not at the latest quarter.

✅ How to look at an oil major

CheckGood sign 🟢Warning 🔴
🛢️ Where is the price?Oil is average or below, and sentiment is poorOil at a spike, and everyone is bullish
🏦 Balance sheetNet debt under about 1x EBITDA, as at most majors nowHigh debt that needs high prices to service
💵 PayoutDividend covered even if oil falls backPayout set at peak earnings
🏗️ CapexSpending stays disciplinedCapex rising while free cash flow falls (as at Exxon and Chevron)
🔁 BuybacksShare count shrinks steadilyBuybacks paused when prices drop (BP did this in February)
🏛️ OwnersClear capital disciplineState owners who may put policy before shareholders (Equinor, Eni)
🧠 So: what to expect? An oil major is a cash-flow and income holding tied to the oil price, not a compounder. Over a full cycle, payouts do most of the work and the share price follows the commodity. The best time to own one is usually when oil is out of favour and the P/E looks high, not when it is at a record and the P/E looks low. Today looks closer to the second case. 🎯
