Real Multi-Baggers: 14 Stocks That Actually Returned 10x or More

I was handed a slick-looking list of "25 historical multi-baggers" with return multiples and tidy narrative explanations for each. It had the shape of real research but I couldn't verify where the numbers came from, a few looked inflated, and the categorization used a valuation framework that doesn't match how this site actually scores companies. So I threw out the framework, kept only the idea, and independently re-verified every number against real split-adjusted price history before publishing anything.

How to read this: every multiple below is current price ÷ split-adjusted historical starting price, sourced from stockanalysis.com (S&P Global Market Intelligence). Companies with a long split history (five, six, nine corporate actions over 30-40 years) carry more room for rounding error than a company with one or two clean splits — I've marked confidence accordingly rather than presenting every number as equally precise. Two names from the original list didn't survive fact-checking at all: ServiceNow (only ~7.6x since its 2012 IPO — doesn't clear the bar) and a first-pass Netflix estimate that undercounted by roughly 10x because it missed Netflix's November 2025 stock split. Both mistakes are exactly why every number here got re-checked before publishing rather than taken on faith.

AMZN		 IPO May 1997, split-adj. $0.075 $258.45 ~3,450x HIGH Priced as an unprofitable bookseller burning cash through the dot-com bust; AWS (launched 2006) was essentially given away free by the market for years before it became the real profit engine.

The actual pattern, once the noise is stripped out

Every genuine multi-bagger here shares one thing: the market was pricing the business as something smaller or more fragile than it turned out to be — a cyclical hardware maker (NVIDIA), a near-bankrupt PC company (Apple), an unprofitable bookseller (Amazon), a bankruptcy-priced chipmaker (AMD). None of them got cheap on a PEG ratio and stayed cheap because the market was lazy — they got cheap because the market was, for a while, factually wrong about what the business actually was. That's a much higher bar than "this looks undervalued," and it's also why most stocks that look cheap on paper never turn into this.

Two corrections worth sitting with: ServiceNow, despite being a real and respected company, doesn't clear a 10x bar since its 2012 IPO at today's price — a useful reminder that not every well-regarded SaaS name is secretly a hidden multi-bagger. And my own first-pass Netflix number was off by roughly 10x because I missed a recent stock split — the kind of silent, single-digit error that's easy to make and easy to publish by accident, which is exactly why every figure above got a second, independent check against real price history before going live.

What PEG Actually Looked Like at the Launchpad

This ties directly back into this week's newsletter topic. If PEG were a reliable multi-bagger detector, every name above should have shown a clean, reasonable PEG right before it took off. It didn't. The real pattern splits into two camps, and neither one is "PEG in the sweet spot":

Company PEG at the launchpad What that actually looked like
Amazon, Tesla, Salesforce, AMD, Netflix Undefined / N/A No PEG to calculate — these were losing money or barely profitable at the moment their real run began. A PEG screen wouldn't have flagged them as cheap or expensive; it would have skipped them entirely, since there was no "E" to divide by.
NVIDIA, Broadcom (Avago), Apple (post-'97) Looked "too cheap" / priced for decline Earnings existed but the market treated them as a shrinking, cyclical, or dying business — a low multiple here wasn't a bargain signal, it was the market betting against the company's future entirely.
Alphabet, Mastercard, Visa, Meta Looked "too expensive" / doubted Real, growing earnings, but the market openly doubted the growth would hold — a mocked IPO structure, antitrust overhang, a credit-crisis backdrop, a botched debut. The PEG at the time reflected genuine skepticism, not oversight.
ASML, Costco Slow-burn, unremarkable Nothing about the entry multiple stood out either way for years — these compounded quietly for a long time before the market re-rated them, which a PEG screen at any single point in time would have simply missed.

The honest conclusion: PEG is a decent tool for gut-checking whether today's price is reasonable relative to today's growth. It is not, and was never meant to be, a multi-bagger detector. Every stock on this page either had no PEG to look at, or had a PEG that told a plausible, defensible story about why the market was right to be cautious — until it wasn't.

A personal note

I own NVIDIA, and it's been a good position — but it was never a tenbagger for me. I came to it late, well after the compounding that actually built the multiple above had already happened. The only genuine multi-bagger in my own portfolio is ASML, and even that wasn't the result of some clever multi-bagger-hunting process. I just held it, for ordinary reasons, and at some point the market decided EUV lithography was the most important choke point in the entire chip industry and re-rated the stock accordingly. I didn't predict that moment. I was just still there when it happened.

That's really the honest takeaway from this whole exercise. Picking a genuine multi-bagger can do wonders for a portfolio — one real one can carry everything else you own. But there's no clean formula for finding one in advance, and this page proves it: the entry PEGs above are all over the map, undefined, too high, too low, or completely unremarkable. What they share instead is more uncomfortable to admit — a bit more risk taken than usual, some genuine vision about where an industry was headed, and, more than either of those, luck in being early and, just as importantly, still holding when the re-rating actually came.
