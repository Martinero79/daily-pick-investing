Cars: A Crowded Industry With Thin Margins

Everyone needs a car, and the industry sells more of them than ever. Yet the companies that make them mostly earn very little on each one. Scoring seventeen listed carmakers on the same 14-point checklist shows how crowded the field is, how thin the gross margin is, and why one name, Ferrari, looks different from all the rest.

Not a cozy oligopoly

The car industry is often pictured as a handful of big players. It is closer to a global battlefield. There are at least ten large legacy groups, each with several brands: Toyota, Volkswagen Group, Stellantis, Hyundai-Kia, General Motors, Ford, Honda, the Nissan-Renault alliance, BMW and Mercedes-Benz. China has added dozens more, led by BYD and Geely and now exporting hard. Tech companies such as Xiaomi have entered too. One market-cap ranking lists 61 listed automakers, and that excludes the unlisted ones.

When many companies can build millions of capable cars, supply tends to outrun demand. China is the clearest case. Chinese trade reporting (Caixin, 36Kr) puts the industry-wide profit margin at a record low of 4.1% in 2025, with production capacity of about 47.5 million vehicles against roughly 35 million sold. A factory that sits idle still costs money, so makers cut prices to keep it running. On the dashboards, Geely's H1 2026 growth came mostly from exports (+158%) while its home market contracts, and BYD's revenue fell 7% in the same period as rivals cut prices.

The missing gross margin

Gross margin is what is left of each euro of sales after the direct cost of making the product. It has to pay for research and development, marketing, warranty, and finally profit. Most carmakers keep only about 14 to 20 cents of each euro at this first step. GM keeps about 11 cents, Ford and Stellantis about 5 to 6. Rivian is barely above zero. Software and payments platforms typically keep far more. There is very little room for error in a business where each model costs billions to develop.

Among the sixteen carmakers other than Ferrari, the middle gross margin is about 16%. The dashboards tell the same story in criterion 14, which checks whether gross margin held up over three years. Mercedes-Benz, Porsche AG, Stellantis, Ford, Honda, Volkswagen, GM, BMW and Tesla all lost margin, and most lost it fast: Porsche AG and Stellantis dropped well over ten percentage points. Only Geely, Ferrari and Xiaomi (from a low base) improved clearly.

What the scores say

Ferrari stands alone. It makes about 13,600 cars a year on purpose, works through an order book that stretches into 2027, and earns a 51.7% gross margin and a 29.5% EBIT margin. That is not a mass-market model, it is a scarcity model closer to luxury goods, which is why it sits near Hermès on the site. It is also the pick of the day.

The scale giants are cheap on paper and thin in practice. Toyota is the best-run of the volume makers, with net cash in its industrial business, but margins are slim and falling under tariffs. Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz, BMW and Porsche AG have large net-cash industrial arms, yet their margins have fallen sharply from the 2022 peak and several have cut guidance. Low valuation multiples often reflect earnings at or near a trough, not a bargain.

Growth is not profit. Xiaomi, Geely and BYD grow far faster than the legacy groups, but their operating margins sit between roughly 3% and 7%. Rivian shows the extreme: a record 19,248 deliveries in Q3 2026, yet an operating margin near −67% and a share count up about 61% since its IPO.

Strong demand does not guarantee strong returns. A stock is worth the cash it will eventually earn for its owners, and in an industry with extreme competition, thin gross margins and heavy fixed costs, most of the value created goes to customers, not shareholders. The checklist rewards the opposite: pricing power, high returns on capital and stable margins. Only one name here shows all three.

What could change the picture

Consolidation and alliances could cut costs, though so far they look defensive, aimed at sharing development bills rather than building pricing power. Tariffs and trade rules reshape who can sell where. Software and autonomy might create margins that cars never had, but they need heavy spending first. The cycle: today's earnings at several makers are close to a trough, so a recovery can lift profits quickly, and the checklist will show it when it does.

One caution for investors

A basket of carmakers holds all of these at once, including the loss-makers and the turnarounds. Whether such a basket works depends on whether the industry as a whole earns more than its cost of capital. On this evidence, most of it does not, and the exceptions are a handful of brands with real scarcity or pricing power.
